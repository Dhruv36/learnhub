Quality Gates That Work Building & Testing

What you'll master: the property that decides whether a gate improves anything — a gate is only a control if people cannot route around it, and every gate people can route around makes things worse than having no gate at all, because it adds cost while providing false assurance; the false-positive budget, where a check that fires wrongly more than about 1% of the time is retried rather than read, measured at a security scanner whose 847 findings produced 3 fixes; Goodhart's law applied to CI, with the coverage gate met by testing getters and the 15% of commits carrying [skip ci]; how to convert blocking gates into preventive, invisible controls — the paved road, the formatter that rewrites instead of complaining, the template that cannot express the wrong thing; the ratchet pattern that makes a legacy codebase improve monotonically without a big-bang cleanup (a 2,400-violation backlog fixed in nine months with zero dedicated effort); how to introduce a gate without a revolt — observe, then warn, then block, with the numbers published at each stage; and the override that every gate needs, because a gate with no break-glass path is a gate that gets disabled permanently during the first incident. Prerequisites: Test Strategy and Flaky Tests — a gate built on an untrustworthy signal is worse than no gate.

Part 1 — What a Gate Is For

A quality gate is an automated decision that stops a change from progressing. That sentence contains the two questions that most gates fail: what decision, and stop it from progressing to where.

QuestionBad answerGood answer
What does it protect?"Quality""A change that would let an unauthenticated request read another tenant's data"
What is the remedy on failure?"Someone looks at it""The author fixes the specific line the message names, in under five minutes"
What is its false-positive rate?UnknownMeasured, and under 1%
Who owns it?Nobody / "security"A named team who fix it when it misfires
How is it bypassed in an emergency?It cannot be — or: anyone can, silentlyAn audited break-glass path with a follow-up obligation
THE RULE THAT SUBSUMES MOST OF THIS LESSON

  A gate that people can route around is WORSE THAN NO GATE, because:
    - it costs the same in time and runner minutes
    - it produces false assurance ("we have a security gate")
    - it trains everyone that the correct response to red is a bypass
    - it makes the NEXT gate you introduce less credible

  ROUTING AROUND TAKES FIVE FORMS, ALL MEASURABLE:
    1. [skip ci] in the commit message ........ 15% of commits
    2. an admin merge ("just this once") ...... 41 in one quarter
    3. an inline suppression comment .......... 1,208 in the codebase
    4. re-running until it passes ............. 78 retries/week
    5. deleting or weakening the check ........ 6 threshold reductions,
                                                each "temporary"
🔑 Measure the bypasses before you argue about the gate. Every one of the five is a countable event in your Git host or your codebase. A gate with 1,208 inline suppressions is not a control; it is a tax with a pressure-release valve, and the number ends the debate about whether it is working far faster than any opinion.

Part 2 — The False-Positive Budget

Every check has a false-positive rate — findings that are not real problems in this context. Human attention to a check collapses at a threshold, and it collapses much sooner than people expect.

MEASURED: FOUR BLOCKING CHECKS, ONE QUARTER

  check                    fires   true positives   FP rate   what people did
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  unit tests               1,204        1,196         0.7%    read and fixed
  type check                 388          388         0.0%    read and fixed
  dependency scanner         847            3        99.6%    suppressed
  "complexity" linter        502           41        91.8%    suppressed

  BEHAVIOURAL CLIFF, from the same data:
    FP rate under 1%    -> the failure is read; median time to fix 6 min
    FP rate 1-10%       -> read, but with suspicion; retries begin
    FP rate over ~20%   -> not read at all. The response is a suppression
                           comment or a retry, applied WITHOUT reading.

  the dependency scanner produced 847 findings and 3 fixes, and taught
  1,208 suppressions — which now also hide the findings that are real.
SymptomDiagnosisAction
Suppression comments growing faster than the codebaseFP rate is above the attention thresholdMove the check out of the blocking path until it is tuned
The same finding suppressed in dozens of placesThe rule is wrong for this codebaseDisable that rule globally rather than 40 times locally
People re-run the check rather than reading itIt is perceived as noiseMeasure its true-positive rate before defending it
A check that has never failedEither it works perfectly, or it is not runningBreak something deliberately and confirm it catches it
A check nobody can explainIt was inherited; its rationale is lostDelete it, or find its owner and its purpose
⚠️ A noisy security scanner is a security problem. It is tempting to argue that a high false-positive rate is acceptable for security because the cost of a miss is high. The measured consequence is the opposite: 99.6% noise produced a habit of suppressing without reading, so the three real findings were suppressed alongside the 844 others. Tuning the scanner — scoping it to reachable code paths, filtering by exploitability, and failing only on new findings — is the security work, not a compromise of it.

Part 3 — Goodhart's Law in CI

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Every gate turns a measure into a target by construction, so the question for each one is how it will be satisfied when someone is in a hurry.

GateIntended behaviourBehaviour you actually get
Line coverage ≥ 80%Tests for meaningful logicTests for getters and constants; assertion-free "smoke" tests. Measured: 84% coverage, 47% mutation score
Zero linter warningsClean code1,208 inline suppressions; the rule is disabled repository-wide within a year
No CVEs above severity XFewer vulnerable dependenciesVersion pinning to an older release with no advisory yet; blanket ignore files with no expiry
Every PR approved by two reviewersCareful reviewReciprocal rubber-stamping; measured median review time 47 seconds for a 400-line diff
Max PR size 200 linesReviewable changesOne change split into five PRs that must merge together, reviewed less carefully than one would have been
Deployment freeze in DecemberStabilityA January release containing three months of changes — the batch-size problem from lesson 1
THE DIAGNOSTIC QUESTION FOR ANY PROPOSED GATE

  "If I were behind schedule and this gate blocked me,
   what is the cheapest thing I could do to make it pass?"

  If the cheapest thing is FIX THE PROBLEM -> it is a good gate.
  If the cheapest thing is anything else   -> that is what will happen,
                                              at scale, within months.

  examples run through the question:
    type check fails       -> cheapest fix: correct the type.        GOOD
    tests fail             -> cheapest: fix the code (or delete the
                              test — so watch deleted-test counts)   MOSTLY
    coverage below 80%     -> cheapest: test a getter.               BAD
    no HIGH CVEs           -> cheapest: add to the ignore file.      BAD
    two approvals          -> cheapest: ask a friend.                BAD
    secret scanner fires   -> cheapest: remove the secret.           GOOD

Part 4 — Preventive and Invisible Beats Blocking and Visible

The strongest control is one that makes the wrong thing impossible or automatic to fix, rather than one that detects the wrong thing later and complains.

Instead of a gate that…Build a control that…Effect
Fails on formattingFormats the code (pre-commit hook + a CI job that pushes the fix)The class of failure ceases to exist
Fails when a secret is committedBlocks the push at the client, and scans server-side as a backstopThe secret never reaches history, where removal is expensive
Fails when infrastructure is misconfiguredProvides a module where the safe configuration is the defaultNobody has to know the rule
Fails when a dependency is unapprovedPoints the package manager at a curated registryThe unapproved thing cannot be installed
Fails when a PR lacks a ticket referencePopulates it from the branch name in a templateCompliance without friction
Warns that an image runs as rootShips base images that already have a non-root userThe default is correct; the gate becomes a backstop
THE MEASURED DIFFERENCE — SAME GOAL, TWO MECHANISMS

  GOAL: no container images running as root in production

  MECHANISM A — a blocking CI check
    3 months: 214 failures, 189 fixed by adding USER, 25 suppressed with
    "# nosec: base image constraint" (none of which was true),
    average 22 minutes lost per failure, and a steady undercurrent of
    "why is the pipeline blocking me on this again"
    -> compliance 88%, and 3 engineers now dislike the security team

  MECHANISM B — a base image with a non-root user, plus the same check
    kept as a non-blocking backstop
    3 months: 2 failures (both genuinely novel), compliance 100%,
    zero suppressions, and the gate now MEANS something when it fires

       that catches something real, twice a quarter"
💡 Every blocking gate is a bet that the wrong thing is easier to detect than to prevent. Sometimes that is true — you cannot prevent a logic bug, so you test for it. But a surprising number of gates exist because prevention was never attempted: the formatter, the safe default, the curated registry and the template each delete an entire class of failure rather than policing it, and each one converts a recurring argument into a one-time engineering task.

Part 5 — The Ratchet: Improving Without a Big-Bang Cleanup

The universal objection to any new gate on an existing codebase is correct: "we have 2,400 violations and we cannot stop to fix them." The ratchet resolves it — the gate applies to change, not to the whole codebase, so quality improves monotonically at zero dedicated cost.

THREE RATCHET SHAPES

  1. DIFF-SCOPED — only the lines this PR touched are checked
       $ ruff check --diff origin/main...HEAD
       $ diff-cover coverage.xml --compare-branch=origin/main --fail-under=80
     new code meets the standard; old code is untouched until edited.

  2. BASELINE / COUNTER — the total may never increase
       # .quality-baseline.json  (committed, updated only downward)
       { "eslint_errors": 2412, "mypy_errors": 863, "todo_fixmes": 411 }
       CI fails if any count EXCEEDS the baseline; a job auto-lowers the
       baseline whenever a run comes in under it.
       -> the number can only fall. There is no way to add debt.

  3. TIME-BOXED EXEMPTION LIST — every suppression has an expiry
       # .eslintignore-until.yml
       - path: legacy/billing/**
         rule: no-any
         until: 2026-12-01
         owner: "@billing-team"
         reason: "typed as part of the billing rewrite"
       expired entries FAIL the build, so nothing is exempt forever.

MEASURED, 9 MONTHS, DIFF-SCOPED LINT + BASELINE COUNTER

  month:        0     1     2     3     5     7     9
  eslint errors 2412  2380  2201  1943  1204   612     0
  dedicated cleanup effort: ZERO. Every reduction happened because
  somebody edited a file for a normal reason and fixed that file.
Ratchet propertyWhy it matters
New code is held to the standard immediatelyThe problem stops growing on day one, which is most of the value
No cleanup project needs fundingThe objection that blocks every quality initiative disappears
The number can only fallProgress is monotonic and visible without anyone tracking it
Exemptions expire"Temporary" becomes a dated commitment with an owner
Failure messages are localThe author sees violations in code they are already reading

Part 6 — Rolling a Gate Out, and the Override It Needs

6.1 Observe → warn → block

  STAGE 1 — OBSERVE (2 weeks, nobody is blocked, nobody is told off)
    run the check, publish nothing but numbers:
      how often does it fire? on whose changes? true-positive rate?
      what would the last 200 PRs have looked like?
    DECISION POINT: if the FP rate is above 1%, tune it or abandon it.
    Half of proposed gates die here, correctly, and cheaply.

  STAGE 2 — WARN (2-4 weeks, annotations only)
    the check posts a PR comment or annotation, does not block.
    measure: what fraction of authors fix it voluntarily?
      >70% voluntary fix rate -> the check is understood and reasonable
      <30% -> the message is unclear, or the fix is too expensive.
              Fix THAT before blocking anyone.

  STAGE 3 — BLOCK (with the ratchet, and only for new code)
    announce the date, publish the numbers from stages 1 and 2,
    ship the exemption mechanism at the same time.

  MEASURED, ONE ROLLOUT (a type-checking gate on a 400k-line codebase)
    stage 1: fired on 61% of PRs — too noisy. Scoped to changed files.
    stage 1b: fired on 12% of PRs, FP rate 0.4%.  Proceed.
    stage 2: 78% of authors fixed it voluntarily; the other 22% asked
             good questions, which improved the error messages.
    stage 3: blocked, diff-scoped. 4 complaints in the first month,
             all about the same confusing message, which was then fixed.
    zero suppressions added in the first quarter.

6.2 Break glass

  EVERY GATE NEEDS AN OVERRIDE, because the alternative is worse:

  ❌ NO OVERRIDE
     3am incident, the fix is one line, the dependency scanner is down
     (or firing on an unrelated finding). The engineer cannot deploy.
     Someone with admin rights disables the gate FOR EVERYONE.
     Nobody re-enables it. Measured: 3 of 5 disabled gates in one org
     were disabled during an incident and never restored.

  ✅ AUDITED BREAK GLASS
     - an explicit, named mechanism: a label, a workflow_dispatch input,
       or an approval from a specific group
     - it is LOUD: posts to the team channel, names the person and the
       reason, and opens a ticket automatically
     - it is BOUNDED: valid for this one merge, not a mode you leave on
     - it is REVIEWED: a weekly report of every use
     measured usage after introducing it: 11 times in a quarter,
     of which 9 were legitimate incidents and 2 revealed a gate that
     was misconfigured — which is exactly the feedback you want.

  THE PROPERTY THAT MAKES IT SAFE: using it is EASY and VISIBLE.
  If it is hard, people disable the gate instead. If it is invisible,
  it becomes the normal path.
🔑 Design the override at the same time as the gate, not after the first incident. A gate without a break-glass path does not stay strict under pressure; it gets switched off entirely by someone who is not thinking about your control while production is down. An easy, loud, bounded override is what keeps the gate in place for the other 99% of changes — and its usage log is one of the better sources of information about which gates are wrong.

❌ An 80% coverage gate, a dependency scanner with a 99.6% false-positive rate, a two-approval rule, a "no linter warnings" policy on a 2,400-violation codebase, no break-glass path, and no measurement of any of it. Coverage reads 84% while mutation testing shows 47% of injected bugs survive, because the cheapest way to satisfy the gate was testing getters. The scanner produced 847 findings and three fixes, and taught the codebase 1,208 suppression comments that now hide the real findings too. Two approvals became reciprocal rubber-stamping with a median review time of 47 seconds on a 400-line diff. The lint policy was impossible to satisfy, so the rule was disabled repository-wide within a year. Fifteen per cent of commits carry [skip ci], there were 41 admin merges last quarter, and when the scanner misfired during a 3am incident someone with admin rights disabled it for everyone — where it remains, four months later, because nobody remembered to turn it back on.

✅ Gates chosen by asking what the cheapest way to pass them is, kept under a 1% false-positive budget, made preventive where prevention is possible, applied as a ratchet to new code, rolled out observe-warn-block, and equipped with a loud, bounded override. For every proposed gate, ask what someone behind schedule would do to make it pass — if the answer is anything other than "fix the problem", that is what will happen at scale. Measure the false-positive rate before defending a check; above roughly 1% it is retried rather than read, and above 20% it is suppressed without being read at all, which makes a noisy security scanner a security problem rather than a security control. Replace gates with prevention wherever the wrong thing can be made impossible: a formatter that rewrites, a base image that is already non-root (214 failures and 25 false suppressions became 2 genuine findings), a curated registry, a template with the safe default. Apply the ratchet so the standard binds new code only — a 2,400-violation backlog reached zero in nine months with no dedicated effort, because the number could only fall. Roll out in three stages with the numbers published at each, and let half your proposed gates die cheaply at stage 1. Then ship the break-glass path on day one: easy, loud, bounded and reviewed weekly — 11 uses in a quarter, nine legitimate and two that exposed a misconfigured gate.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is a quality gate, and what makes one good?

A quality gate is an automated decision that stops a change from progressing — a failing test suite, a type check, a secret scanner, a security policy.

A good one has five properties:

  • A specific thing it protects, expressible as a sentence about a real failure — not "quality".
  • A cheap, obvious remedy: the message names the line, and the fix takes minutes.
  • A low false-positive rate — under about 1% for anything blocking, because above that people retry rather than read.
  • An owner who fixes it when it misfires.
  • An audited override, because a gate with no break-glass path gets disabled entirely during the first incident.

The single best test of a proposed gate is the question: "if I were behind schedule and this blocked me, what is the cheapest thing I could do to make it pass?" If the answer is anything other than "fix the problem" — add a suppression, test a getter, ask a colleague for a rubber stamp — then that is what will happen at scale within months.

Beginner: Why can a quality gate make things worse than having no gate at all?

Because a gate that people can route around costs everything a real gate costs and delivers none of the protection, while adding two new harms.

False assurance: the organisation believes a class of problem is being prevented, so it stops thinking about it. A dependency scanner with a 99.6% false-positive rate produced three fixes and 1,208 suppression comments — and those suppressions now hide the genuine findings too, so the codebase is less safe than if the scanner had never been installed.

Cultural damage: every bypass teaches the team that red means "find the workaround" rather than "something is wrong". That habit generalises to gates that are meaningful, and it makes the next gate you introduce less credible.

The bypasses are all countable, which is how you settle the argument: [skip ci] in commit messages, admin merges, inline suppression comments, retries, and quietly weakened thresholds. If those numbers are large, the gate is not working, regardless of anyone's opinion about its importance.

Mid: How do you introduce a new blocking check to a team without a revolt?

Three stages, with the numbers published at each — and a genuine willingness to abandon the check at stage 1.

StageDurationWhat you learn
Observe — run it, block nobody, tell nobody off~2 weeksHow often it fires, on whose changes, and its true-positive rate. If the FP rate is above 1%, tune it or abandon it. Half of proposed gates die here, correctly and cheaply
Warn — annotate the PR, still non-blocking2–4 weeksThe voluntary fix rate. Above 70% means the check is understood and the fix is reasonable; below 30% means the message is unclear or the fix is too expensive — fix that before blocking anyone
Block — announced date, diff-scoped ratchet, exemption mechanism shipped with it—Whether you were right

Three details that decide the outcome. Scope it to changed code, so nobody is asked to fix a decade of history to merge a two-line change. Ship the exemption and break-glass mechanism at the same time, with owners and expiry dates, because a gate with no valve gets disabled under pressure. And publish the numbers — "this fired on 12% of PRs with a 0.4% false-positive rate, and 78% of authors fixed it voluntarily during the warning phase" is an argument; "we need better quality" is a preference.

One more thing I would insist on: the gate needs an owning team who will fix it when it misfires. A check that fires wrongly and has no owner is guaranteed to be routed around, because the workaround is the only available action.

Mid: Your codebase has 2,400 lint violations. Leadership wants a zero-warnings policy. What do you propose?

I would agree with the goal and reject the implementation, then propose the ratchet — which reaches zero without a cleanup project.

Why a blanket zero-warnings gate fails here: nobody can merge anything until 2,400 violations are fixed, so the rule is either preceded by a months-long cleanup that competes with product work, or it is switched off within a year. Both outcomes are common; the second is more common.

The ratchet, in three parts:

  • Diff-scoped enforcement: only lines this PR touched are checked (ruff check --diff origin/main...HEAD). New code meets the standard from day one, so the problem stops growing immediately — which is most of the value.
  • A baseline counter committed to the repository: the total may never increase, and a job lowers it automatically whenever a run comes in under it. There is now no way to add debt, and progress is monotonic and visible.
  • Time-boxed exemptions with an owner, a reason and an expiry date that fails the build when it passes — so "temporary" is a dated commitment.

The measured outcome on one codebase: 2,412 → 0 in nine months with zero dedicated cleanup effort, because every reduction happened when somebody edited a file for a normal reason. That is also the honest pitch to leadership — it is slower to reach zero than a cleanup sprint, and it costs nothing, never competes with product work, and cannot regress.

Senior: A security team wants to block all builds with any HIGH or CRITICAL CVE. How do you respond?

I would support the intent and negotiate the mechanism, because as stated it will reduce security rather than improve it.

What the gate does as specified: most HIGH CVEs in a typical dependency tree are not reachable from your code — a vulnerability in an unused code path of a transitive dependency. The measured pattern is a scanner firing 847 times and producing three real fixes. At that noise level the cheapest ways to pass are: add it to the ignore file, or pin to an older version that has no advisory yet — which is strictly worse security. Meanwhile the blocking rule cannot be satisfied at all when a CVE has no fix available, so builds stop for reasons nobody can act on.

What I would propose instead:

ChangeEffect
Block on new findings introduced by this change, not on the existing inventoryThe problem stops growing; nobody is blocked by history
Filter by reachability/exploitability, not severity aloneCuts the noise by an order of magnitude and makes each finding actionable
Require a fix path: block only when a fixed version existsRemoves the unsatisfiable case, which is what breaks trust fastest
Time-boxed exemptions with an owner and expiryNothing is ignored permanently, and the list is auditable
An SLA on the inventory, tracked separately from the gate"Fix HIGHs within 30 days" is a commitment; a build gate is a blunt instrument for it
Automated dependency updates so most CVEs are fixed before anyone sees themThe prevention that removes most of the gate's work — see dependency management

The framing that usually lands with a security team: 1,208 suppression comments are a worse security outcome than a slightly less strict gate, because they hide the real findings and they are permanent. Tuning the scanner is the security work.

Senior: How would you audit an organisation's existing gates and decide which to keep?

Inventory them, measure four numbers each, and then act — most of the value is in deletion.

MeasureWhere it comes fromWhat it tells you
Fire rateCI run history per checkHow often it participates at all
True-positive rateSample 50 failures and classify themWhether it deserves to block
Bypass rate[skip ci], admin merges, suppression comments, retries, threshold reductionsWhether it is a control or a tax
Time costDuration × frequency, plus median time-to-fixWhat it is charging the organisation

Then the decision, per gate:

  • High TP, low bypass → keep, and protect it from noise creep.
  • High TP, high bypass → the remedy is too expensive or unclear. Fix the message, the scope or the fix path; keep it blocking.
  • Low TP, low bypass → it rarely fires; make it non-blocking and cheap, or delete it.
  • Low TP, high bypass → delete it today. It is producing false assurance and training bypass behaviour. This is the most common category, and deleting a few of these buys credibility for everything else.
  • Never fires → prove it works by breaking something deliberately; if it does not catch that, it has been decorative for however long it has existed.

The organisational move that matters most: give every surviving gate a named owning team and a published false-positive budget, and review the bypass numbers quarterly. Gates decay — codebases change, rules drift, noise accumulates — and an unowned gate becomes a routed-around gate on a predictable schedule. I would also convert two or three of the survivors into preventive controls during the audit, because each one deletes a category of failure instead of policing it, and that is where the durable wins are.

  # ci/ratchet.sh
  #!/usr/bin/env bash
  set -euo pipefail
  BASE="$(jq -r ".$1" .quality-baseline.json)"
  CURRENT="$2"
  if [ "$CURRENT" -gt "$BASE" ]; then
      echo "::error::$1 rose from $BASE to $CURRENT — this change adds debt"
      exit 1
  fi

      jq ".$1 = $CURRENT" .quality-baseline.json > tmp && mv tmp .quality-baseline.json
      # committed back by a bot on main only, never on a PR branch
  fi

6 (hard): Design and implement break glass: an override that is easy, loud, bounded and audited — then use its logs to find a broken gate.
Solution 
THE MECHANISM — A LABEL, NOT AN ADMIN RIGHT

  # .github/workflows/break-glass.yml
  on:
    pull_request:
      types: [labeled]

  jobs:
    break-glass:
      if: github.event.label.name == 'break-glass'
      runs-on: ubuntu-latest
      steps:
        - name: Require a reason
          run: |
            set -euo pipefail
            BODY=$(gh pr view "$PR" --json body --jq .body)
            echo "$BODY" | grep -q '^BREAK-GLASS-REASON:' || {
              gh pr edit "$PR" --remove-label break-glass
              gh pr comment "$PR" --body \
                "Break-glass needs a line starting 'BREAK-GLASS-REASON:' in the description."
              exit 1; }

        - name: Announce loudly
          run: |
            gh pr comment "$PR" --body \
              "🚨 **Break glass used** by @${ACTOR}. Checks bypassed: ${CHECKS}.
               Reason: ${REASON}. A follow-up issue has been opened."
            ./ci/notify-slack.sh '#eng-alerts' \
              "Break glass on PR #${PR} by ${ACTOR}: ${REASON}"

        - name: Open the follow-up obligation
          run: |
            gh issue create --title "Break-glass follow-up: PR #${PR}" \
              --body "Bypassed: ${CHECKS}. Reason: ${REASON}.
                      Close this when the bypassed check passes on main." \
              --label break-glass --assignee "${ACTOR}"

        - name: Satisfy the required check (this PR only)
          run: gh api "repos/${REPO}/statuses/${SHA}" -f state=success \
                 -f context="ci/required" \
                 -f description="bypassed via break-glass by ${ACTOR}"

  PROPERTIES, EACH DELIBERATE
    EASY     one label. No admin rights, no ticket, no waiting for a person.
    LOUD     a PR comment, a Slack message, and an auto-assigned issue.
    BOUNDED  applies to this SHA only. It is not a mode; the next commit
             is gated again. The label is auto-removed on the next push.
    AUDITED  every use is an issue with a labelled query, reviewed weekly.

⚠️ Never make break glass require a human approver. The scenario it exists for is 3am during an incident, when the approver is asleep and the alternative is an admin disabling the gate globally. Make it self-service and noisy: you will get an accurate log of every use, and the social cost of the announcement is a far better deterrent than an approval queue that simply gets bypassed a different way.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • A gate people can route around is worse than no gate — same cost, false assurance, and it teaches everyone that red means "find the workaround".
  • The five bypasses are all countable: [skip ci] commits, admin merges, inline suppressions, retries, and quietly lowered thresholds. Measure them before arguing about a gate.
  • The diagnostic question for any proposed gate: "if I were behind schedule, what is the cheapest thing I could do to make this pass?" If the answer is not "fix the problem", that is what will happen at scale.
  • False positives have a behavioural cliff. Under ~1% the failure is read; above ~20% it is suppressed without being read. A scanner firing 847 times produced 3 fixes and 1,208 suppressions.
  • A noisy security scanner is a security problem, not a strict one — the real findings get suppressed alongside the noise. Tuning it is the security work.
  • Separate the gate from the inventory: block on new, reachable findings that have a fix available; track the existing inventory as a ticket queue with an SLA. Retuning took blocking findings 847 → 7 and real fixes 3 → 23.
  • Goodhart's law is guaranteed: coverage gates are met by testing getters (84% coverage, 47% mutation score), approval counts by rubber-stamping (47-second median review of a 400-line diff), CVE gates by pinning to older unlisted versions.
  • Preventive and invisible beats blocking and visible. A non-root base image turned 214 failures and 25 false suppressions into 2 genuine findings and 100% compliance.
  • Prevention candidates worth converting first: a formatter that rewrites, client-side secret blocking, a curated package registry, safe-by-default modules, and templates that cannot express the wrong thing.
  • The ratchet answers the "we have 2,400 violations" objection: enforce on changed lines only, keep a baseline counter that can only fall, and expire every exemption.
  • Measured: 2,412 → 0 ESLint errors in nine months with zero dedicated cleanup effort, because every reduction happened when someone edited a file for a normal reason.
  • Never let a human raise the baseline; the moment there is an exception it becomes a threshold, and thresholds get lowered "temporarily" three times in two years.
  • Roll out observe → warn → block, with a decision point at each stage and the numbers published. Whole-repo type checking fired on 61% of PRs; scoping it to changed files brought that to 12% with a 0.0% false-positive rate.
  • Expect half your proposed gates to die at the observe stage — that is the cheapest possible outcome and it builds credibility rather than spending it.
  • The warn stage's number to watch is the voluntary fix rate: above 70% means the check is understood; below 30% means the message or the remedy needs work before anyone is blocked.
  • Every gate needs break glass, designed on day one. Without it, someone disables the gate for everyone during an incident — 3 of 5 disabled gates in one organisation were never restored.
  • Break glass must be easy, loud, bounded to one commit, and audited; never gated behind a human approver, because the scenario is 3am and the alternative is a global disable.
  • Break-glass logs are the most honest inventory of broken gates you will ever have — 8 of 11 uses in one quarter named the same misconfigured check.
  • Give every surviving gate a named owner and a published false-positive budget; an unowned gate becomes a routed-around gate on a predictable schedule.
  • A check that has never failed is not proof of health — break something deliberately and confirm it is caught, or it has been decorative for however long it has existed.
