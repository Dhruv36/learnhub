DORA Metrics, Honestly Operating & Measuring

What you'll master: what the four keys actually measure and — more usefully — what they are proxies for, so they become a diagnostic rather than a scoreboard; the definitional traps that make cross-team comparison meaningless, where changing only the definition of "lead time" moved a team's number from 4 hours to 9 days without anything about the team changing; how each metric is gamed and the paired metric that catches it, because deploy frequency rose 340% in one quarter through a bot that deployed unchanged artifacts nightly; what DORA does not measure at all — value, product quality, developer experience, security posture — and why "elite performer" benchmarking is the least useful thing you can do with the data; how to run an improvement cycle where the four keys identify the constraint, you fix it, and the constraint moves — measured across three quarters as it travelled from CI duration to review latency to database migrations; and how to instrument all of it from data you already have, including the sampling problems that make early dashboards lie. Prerequisites: the whole track — this lesson connects batch size, pipeline duration, rollback and incident response.

Part 1 — The Four Keys, and What They Are Proxies For

MetricDefinitionProxy forPaired with
Deployment frequencyHow often you successfully release to productionBatch size, and confidence in the release processChange failure rate
Lead time for changesCommit → running in productionThe length and friction of the delivery pathChange failure rate
Change failure rate% of deployments causing degraded service requiring remediationQuality of the change processDeployment frequency
Failed deployment recovery timeHow long to restore service after a failed deploymentBlast-radius control and rollback maturityChange failure rate
WHY THEY ARE PAIRED — THE ONLY REASON THE SET WORKS

  THROUGHPUT            STABILITY
  deploy frequency      change failure rate
  lead time             recovery time

  each pair pulls against the other, so gaming one shows up in the other:
    - deploy 10x more often by shipping garbage -> failure rate rises
    - cut failure rate to zero by never deploying -> frequency collapses
    - cut lead time by skipping tests            -> failure rate rises
    - cut recovery time by never declaring
      incidents                                  -> shows up in your SLOs

  REPORTING ANY ONE OF THE FOUR ALONE IS THE MOST COMMON MISUSE, and it
  reliably produces the behaviour the pairing exists to prevent.
🔑 The four keys measure the delivery system, not the people using it. They are diagnostics for the path from commit to production: where it is slow, where it breaks, how quickly it recovers. The moment they appear on an individual's performance review they stop measuring the system, because the cheapest way to move them is to change how work is recorded rather than how it flows.

Part 2 — Definitions Decide the Numbers

THE SAME TEAM, THE SAME QUARTER, FOUR DEFINITIONS OF LEAD TIME

  A) first commit on the branch -> production          9 days
  B) pull request opened        -> production          4 days
  C) pull request approved      -> production          6 hours
  D) merge to main              -> production          4 hours

  All four are "lead time for changes". Nothing about the team differs.
  D is the most flattering and the least useful: it measures only the
  pipeline, and hides the review queue that is the actual constraint.

  THE HONEST CHOICE: A or B — because they include the waiting, and the
  waiting is usually where the time goes. Then publish which one you use,
  and never change it silently.
QuestionTrapHonest choice
What counts as a deployment?Counting every environment, or every canary stage, inflates frequency 5×A production release that reaches users; count a canary ramp once
When does lead time start?Starting at merge hides review and queue timeFirst commit, or PR opened — the developer-perceived clock
What is a change failure?"Only if we declared an incident" undercounts; "any alert" overcountsAny deployment requiring remediation: rollback, hotfix, flag off, forward fix within N hours
When does recovery end?"When the fix deployed" vs "when users were fine"Service restored for users — not the deploy timestamp
Which deployments count?Excluding config and flag changes flatters everythingAnything that changes production behaviour
Per what unit?Per organisation hides everythingPer service or per team, then trend
THE DEFINITIONS DOCUMENT — TWO PARAGRAPHS THAT PREVENT A YEAR OF ARGUMENT

  deployment:      a change to production behaviour that reaches users.
                   A canary ramp is ONE deployment, counted when it
                   reaches 100%. Flag flips that release a feature COUNT.
                   Infrastructure-only changes count.
  lead time:       from the first commit on the branch to the moment that
                   commit is serving production traffic. p50 and p85.
  change failure:  a deployment followed within 24h by a rollback,
                   hotfix, flag disable, or an incident of any severity
                   attributed to it.
  recovery time:   from the start of user-visible impact to service
                   restored — NOT to the fix being merged.
  unit:            per service. Team and org numbers are the medians of
                   their services, never a single pooled number.

Part 3 — How Each Metric Is Gamed

MetricGamingWhat catches it
Deployment frequencyA bot deploying unchanged artifacts nightly; counting every environment; splitting one release into fiveDeployments containing zero commits; lead time unchanged; failure rate flat while frequency triples

THE MEASURED GAMING, AND HOW IT WAS FOUND

  Q3 report: "deployment frequency up 340%!"

$ # deployments containing zero commits since the previous one
$ ./ci/deploy-log.sh --env production --last 90d \
  | awk 'NR>1 { if ($3 == prev) empty++; prev=$3 } END
         { printf "empty deploys: %d of %d (%.0f%%)\n", empty, NR-1, 100*empty/(NR-1) }'
  empty deploys: 412 of 601 (69%)

  a nightly job redeployed the current digest to "keep the number up".
  real deployment frequency was unchanged.

  THE CROSS-CHECKS THAT MADE IT OBVIOUS
    lead time p50 ......... unchanged (4h -> 4h)
    commits per deploy .... 3.1 -> 0.9  (the tell)
    failure rate .......... unchanged
  -> frequency rose without any other number moving. That combination
     is almost always a definitional artifact rather than an improvement.
⚠️ If one of the four moves a lot and none of the others move at all, suspect the measurement. Genuine improvements propagate: faster pipelines shorten lead time and usually raise frequency; better rollback shortens recovery and often lowers failure rate, because people stop batching. An isolated 340% is a story about instrumentation.

THE BENCHMARK TRAP

  "elite performers deploy multiple times per day" is a description of
  a population, not a target for your team.

  a payments processor with a regulated release process and a 40-year-old
  mainframe integration is not going to deploy on demand, and chasing the
  benchmark produces theatre: nightly empty deploys, a "lead time" clock
  that starts at merge, and incidents that are quietly not declared.

  USE THE NUMBERS AS A TREND AGAINST YOURSELF:
    are we faster than last quarter?
    did the thing we fixed actually move the number we predicted?
    which of the four is our constraint RIGHT NOW?
  those questions are answerable and actionable. "Are we elite?" is not.

Part 5 — Using Them: Find the Constraint, Fix It, Watch It Move

THE CYCLE

  1. MEASURE the four keys per service, honestly, for a month.
  2. IDENTIFY THE CONSTRAINT — the one number whose improvement would
     most change how the team works.
  3. DECOMPOSE it. "Lead time is 9 days" is not actionable; the
     breakdown always is.
  4. FIX the biggest component.
  5. RE-MEASURE, and expect the constraint to MOVE somewhere else.

MEASURED: THREE QUARTERS OF THE CONSTRAINT MOVING

  Q1 — lead time p50 = 9 days. DECOMPOSED:
       first commit -> PR opened ......... 3.1 days   (branch age)
       PR opened -> approved ............. 4.2 days   ← the constraint
       approved -> merged ................ 0.4 days
       merged -> production .............. 1.3 days
       ACTION: review SLA, smaller PRs, reviewer rotation
       RESULT: lead time 9d -> 4.1d

  Q2 — lead time p50 = 4.1 days. DECOMPOSED:
       first commit -> PR opened ......... 2.6 days
       PR opened -> approved ............. 0.6 days   (fixed)
       approved -> merged ................ 0.3 days
       merged -> production .............. 0.6 days
       ACTION: branch age is now the constraint -> feature flags,
               trunk-based development (<lesson 1>)
       RESULT: lead time 4.1d -> 1.2d, deploy frequency 2/wk -> 9/wk

  Q3 — lead time p50 = 1.2 days, but CHANGE FAILURE RATE rose 11% -> 19%.
       DECOMPOSED the failures:
       schema-related ..................... 7 of 11   ← the new constraint
       config ............................. 2
       code ............................... 2
       ACTION: expand/contract discipline, migration rehearsal
               (<database migrations>)
       RESULT: failure rate 19% -> 6%, recovery time 41min -> 9min

  THE POINT: each fix revealed the next constraint. A team that had only
  tracked "deployment frequency" would have seen a flat line in Q1 and
  concluded that nothing was working.
If the constraint is…Decompose intoUsual fixes
Lead timeBranch age, review wait, merge wait, pipeline, deploy waitSmaller PRs, review SLA, pipeline speed, auto-deploy on merge
Deployment frequencyBatch size, release ceremony, approvals, deploy windowsTrunk-based development, flags, remove freezes
Change failure rateCause categories: schema, config, code, dependency, infraWhichever category dominates — usually migrations or config
Recovery timeDetect, decide, act, verifyAlerting, automated rollback, rollback drills

Part 6 — Instrumenting It From Data You Already Have

DEPLOYMENT FREQUENCY AND LEAD TIME — FROM DEPLOYMENT EVENTS

  # emit one event per production deployment (you may already have this
  # from the promotion record in <environments and promotion>)
  {
    "service": "shop-api",
    "environment": "production",
    "digest": "sha256:9e1c4a…",
    "commits": ["4f2a91c…", "a913bb0…"],       // since the previous deploy
    "deployed_at": "2026-08-21T10:14:07Z",
    "run_id": "9481123"
  }

  lead time per commit = deployed_at − commit.authored_at
  deployment frequency = count of events per service per week
  and the tell for empty deploys: commits == []

CHANGE FAILURE RATE AND RECOVERY — FROM INCIDENTS, LINKED TO DEPLOYS

  {
    "incident_id": "INC-4412",
    "service": "shop-api",
    "started_at": "2026-08-21T10:22:00Z",      // user impact BEGAN
    "detected_at": "2026-08-21T10:23:40Z",
    "restored_at": "2026-08-21T10:31:10Z",     // users fine again
    "caused_by_deploy": "sha256:9e1c4a…",      // the link that matters
    "remediation": "rollback"
  }

  change failure rate = deploys linked to an incident ÷ total deploys
  recovery time        = restored_at − started_at

  THE LINK IS THE HARD PART. Make it cheap:
   - the incident template has a "caused by deployment" field, prefilled
     with the last three production deploys for that service
   - any rollback automatically opens an incident record
   - a hotfix branch name convention links back to the deploy

SAMPLING AND DATA-QUALITY PROBLEMS THAT MAKE EARLY DASHBOARDS LIE

  1. MISSING DEPLOYMENTS — manual deploys that emit no event.
     symptom: frequency looks low, lead time looks enormous.
     check: compare deployment events with image digests actually
            running (<supply chain>). Any digest without an event is a
            deployment you are not counting.

  2. UNLINKED INCIDENTS — failure rate looks brilliant.
     check: incidents whose start time falls within 24h of a deploy but
            which are unlinked. Review those weekly.

  3. LEAD TIME SKEWED BY OUTLIERS — one 90-day branch destroys the mean.
     use p50 AND p85, never the mean.

  4. LOW-VOLUME SERVICES — 3 deploys a quarter makes failure rate
     0% or 33% with nothing in between.
     aggregate to a longer window, and say so.

  5. BACKFILLED DATA — a dashboard that starts "green" because the
     first month has no incident records yet.
     wait a full cycle before drawing conclusions.
💡 Instrument deployments first and incidents second, but do not report anything until both exist. A dashboard showing frequency and lead time without failure rate and recovery is exactly the unpaired reporting that produces gaming — and it is the natural order to build in, so it takes deliberate restraint to hold the report back for a few weeks.

❌ A quarterly slide showing deployment frequency up 340% against an "elite performer" benchmark, lead time measured from merge, change failure rate derived from declared incidents only, recovery time measured to the fix deploy, and the whole thing broken down per engineer. Sixty-nine per cent of those deployments contain no commits, because a nightly job redeploys the current digest to keep the number rising. Lead time reads four hours because the clock starts after review, hiding the 4.2 days a pull request waits for a reviewer — which is the actual constraint. Change failure rate looks excellent because a rollback is recorded as "a planned revert" and nobody declares an incident for something fixed within the hour. Recovery time ends when the hotfix deploys rather than when users stop seeing errors. And because the numbers are per engineer, the rational response is to record work differently rather than to change how it flows — so the delivery system improves not at all while every number on the slide goes up.

✅ Publish the definitions, report the four keys as pairs, use them to find the constraint, and check the data before believing it. Write down what counts as a deployment, when the lead time clock starts, what makes a change a failure, and when recovery ends — then never change a definition silently, because changing only the definition moved one team from 4 hours to 9 days. Report throughput and stability together so gaming one shows up in the other, and treat any metric that moves alone as an instrumentation story until proven otherwise. Decompose the constraint rather than staring at the headline: 9 days of lead time was 3.1 days of branch age, 4.2 of review wait, 0.4 of merge and 1.3 of deploy — and fixing review took it to 4.1 days, after which branch age became the constraint, and then schema changes did. Pair the four with what they cannot see: SLOs for reliability, time-to-patch for security, cost per change, and a developer survey for the experience the numbers hide. Then verify the data continuously — deployment events against digests actually running, incidents unlinked but suspiciously timed — and report per service, as a trend against yourselves rather than against a benchmark population.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What are the four DORA metrics, and why are they used together?
MetricMeasuresProxy for
Deployment frequencyHow often you release to productionBatch size and confidence
Lead time for changesCommit → running in productionFriction in the delivery path
Change failure rateShare of deploys needing remediationQuality of the change process
Recovery timeHow long until service is restoredBlast-radius control and rollback maturity

They are used together because the first two measure throughput and the last two measure stability, and each pair constrains the other. Deploy ten times more often by shipping carelessly and the failure rate rises; drive the failure rate to zero by never deploying and frequency collapses.

That is also why reporting one alone is the most common misuse: it removes the constraint that keeps the set honest. In one measured case, deployment frequency rose 340% in a quarter because a nightly job redeployed the current artifact — and the giveaway was that no other metric moved at all.

Beginner: Where does "lead time for changes" start?

That is exactly the right question, because the answer changes the number by more than most improvements do. The same team, same quarter:

Clock starts atLead time p50
First commit on the branch9 days
Pull request opened4 days
Pull request approved6 hours
Merge to main4 hours

Prefer first commit or PR opened. They include the waiting — branch age and review latency — and in most organisations the waiting is where the time actually goes. Starting at merge measures only your pipeline, which is usually the part you have already optimised.

The practical rule: pick the definition that includes the friction you want to fix, publish it, and never change it silently. A metric whose definition drifts is worse than no metric, because the trend line is fiction.

Mid: A team's deployment frequency triples but nothing else changes. What do you check?

I would treat that pattern as an instrumentation story until proven otherwise, because genuine improvements propagate: a faster pipeline shortens lead time and usually raises frequency; better rollback lowers recovery time and often lowers failure rate because people stop batching.

The specific checks:

  • Deployments containing zero commits — the classic tell. In the measured case, 412 of 601 production "deployments" contained no new commits, because a nightly job redeployed the current digest.
  • Commits per deployment — it fell from 3.1 to 0.9, which is arithmetic, not improvement.
  • What counts as a deployment — did someone start counting staging, or each canary stage?
  • Lead time — if frequency tripled because batches got smaller, lead time should have fallen too. It did not move.
  • Failure rate — a real tripling of frequency usually perturbs it in one direction or the other, at least briefly.

And then the harder conversation: why was someone optimising the number? Usually because a single metric was being reported to leadership without its pair, which is a reporting design problem rather than a team integrity problem.

Mid: What do the four keys fail to tell you?

Quite a lot — they measure the delivery system, not the software, the users, or the people.

Blind spotConsequenceComplement
ValueYou can ship the wrong thing very fastProduct outcomes; experiment results
Reliability as experiencedA slow, degraded, always-up service scores perfectlySLOs and error budgets
Developer experienceGood numbers are achievable with a miserable processSurveys; onboarding time; wait time
SecurityFast delivery of insecure software is still fastVerifiable deployments; time to patch reachable CVEs
CostFrequency can rise while cost per change explodesCost per deployment; CI spend
ToilHeroics can hold recovery time down indefinitelyOn-call load; manual steps per release

The last one deserves emphasis: a team can post excellent recovery times because two people are always online and personally know how to fix everything. The metric looks healthy right up until one of them leaves — which is why I would always pair the four keys with a measure of manual steps and on-call load.

Senior: How would you use DORA metrics to actually improve delivery, rather than to report on it?

As a diagnostic loop that finds the constraint, fixes it, and expects the constraint to move.

  1. Measure honestly for a month, per service, with published definitions.
  2. Identify the constraint — the one number whose improvement would most change how the team works.
  3. Decompose it. "Lead time is 9 days" is not actionable; the breakdown always is.
  4. Fix the largest component, and predict the effect in advance.
  5. Re-measure and expect a new constraint.

The worked example over three quarters:

QuarterConstraintActionResult
Q1Review wait: 4.2 of 9 daysReview SLA, smaller PRs, reviewer rotationLead time 9d → 4.1d
Q2Branch age: 2.6 of 4.1 daysFeature flags, trunk-based development1.2d; frequency 2/wk → 9/wk
Q3Change failure rate 11% → 19%, mostly schemaExpand/contract, migration rehearsalFailure rate 6%; recovery 41min → 9min

Two things this illustrates. First, the constraint moves after every fix — a team tracking only deployment frequency would have seen a flat line in Q1 and concluded the work was useless. Second, rising failure rate in Q3 was a sign of progress, not regression: shipping nine times a week surfaced a class of problem that shipping twice a week had hidden, and it was fixable once visible.

Senior: Leadership wants DORA metrics per engineer. How do you respond?

I would agree with the underlying goal — understanding whether delivery is improving — and refuse the mechanism, with specifics rather than principles.

Why per-engineer breaks the metrics: all four measure a system that many people share. A commit's lead time depends on reviewer availability, pipeline speed, deploy windows and the release process; attributing it to its author measures the system and labels a person. And once measured individually, the cheapest way to improve every one of them is to change how work is recorded: open the pull request later, avoid touching risky areas, split changes to raise deploy counts, and resist declaring incidents. All four keys are gameable in ways that make delivery worse.

What I would offer instead:

  • Per-service metrics, with team views as medians of their services — actionable, and comparable over time.
  • The constraint analysis, which is what leadership actually wants: "review latency is 4.2 of our 9 days; here is the plan and the predicted effect".
  • A paired dashboard so improvements are credible: throughput and stability together, with cross-checks for empty deploys and unlinked incidents.
  • If individual performance is the real question, say so plainly: that is a management conversation about scope, quality and collaboration — DORA metrics are not evidence for it, and using them as such will corrupt the data everyone else depends on.

The pragmatic argument that usually lands: the moment these numbers affect someone's review, they stop being measurements. You lose the diagnostic that would have told you where delivery is slow — and that diagnostic is worth far more than the ranking.

Solution 
DEPLOYMENT FREQUENCY AND LEAD TIME — FROM THE DEPLOYMENT API

$ gh api "/repos/acme/shop-api/deployments?environment=production&per_page=100" \
    --jq '.[] | [.created_at, .sha] | @tsv' | sort > deploys.tsv
$ wc -l < deploys.tsv
  61                                  # over 90 days
$ echo "scale=2; 61/12.8" | bc
  4.77                                # deployments per week

$ # lead time: for each deploy, the age of every commit it shipped
$ prev=""
$ while IFS=$'\t' read -r when sha; do
    [ -z "$prev" ] && { prev="$sha"; pw="$when"; continue; }
    for c in $(git log --format=%H "$prev..$sha"); do
      authored=$(git show -s --format=%cI "$c")
      echo $(( $(date -d "$when" +%s) - $(date -d "$authored" +%s) ))
    done
    prev="$sha"; pw="$when"
  done < deploys.tsv | sort -n > leadtimes.txt

$ awk '{a[NR]=$1} END {
    printf "lead time p50 %.1f h   p85 %.1f h   n=%d\n",
      a[int(NR*0.5)]/3600, a[int(NR*0.85)]/3600, NR }' leadtimes.txt
  lead time p50 96.4 h   p85 214.0 h   n=487
  # 4.0 days p50, 8.9 days p85

CHANGE FAILURE RATE AND RECOVERY — FROM INCIDENTS LINKED TO DEPLOYS

$ gh issue list --label incident --state all --limit 100 \
    --json number,title,createdAt,closedAt,body \
  | jq -r '.[] | [.number,
                  (.body | capture("caused_by_deploy: (?<d>\\S+)").d // "unlinked"),
                  .createdAt, .closedAt] | @tsv' > incidents.tsv

$ LINKED=$(awk -F'\t' '$2 != "unlinked"' incidents.tsv | wc -l)
$ echo "scale=1; 100*$LINKED/61" | bc
  14.8                                # change failure rate: 14.8%

$ awk -F'\t' '$2!="unlinked" {
    split($3,a,"T"); split($4,b,"T");
    cmd="date -d \""$3"\" +%s"; cmd|getline s; close(cmd);
    cmd="date -d \""$4"\" +%s"; cmd|getline e; close(cmd);
    print e-s }' incidents.tsv | sort -n \
  | awk '{a[NR]=$1} END {printf "recovery p50 %.0f min\n", a[int(NR*0.5)]/60}'
  recovery p50 47 min

2 (easy): Audit your own metrics for gaming and instrumentation artifacts.
Solution 
CHECK 1 — EMPTY DEPLOYMENTS (frequency inflation)

$ prev=""; empty=0; total=0
$ while IFS=$'\t' read -r when sha; do
    [ -n "$prev" ] && { total=$((total+1));
      n=$(git rev-list --count "$prev..$sha");
      [ "$n" -eq 0 ] && empty=$((empty+1)); }
    prev="$sha"
  done < deploys.tsv
$ echo "empty: $empty/$total"
  empty: 412/601                      ❌ 69% ship no new commits

CHECK 2 — COMMITS PER DEPLOY, OVER TIME (the tell)

$ ./ci/commits-per-deploy.sh --by-month
  2026-04   3.1
  2026-05   3.0
  2026-06   1.4          ← something changed here
  2026-07   0.9
  2026-08   0.9
$ git log --oneline --since=2026-06-01 -- .github/workflows/nightly-deploy.yml | tail -3
  a913bb0 chore: nightly redeploy to keep deployment frequency up   ❌

CHECK 3 — DEPLOYMENTS THAT EMIT NO EVENT (frequency deflation)

$ ./ci/whats-running.sh --digests-only | sort -u > /tmp/running.txt
$ awk -F'\t' '{print $2}' deploys.tsv | sort -u > /tmp/recorded.txt
$ comm -23 /tmp/running.txt /tmp/recorded.txt
  sha256:41ab7729ce30…                ❌ running, never recorded
  sha256:2b7710aa41cd…                   -> manual deploys, uncounted

CHECK 4 — UNLINKED INCIDENTS (failure rate deflation)

$ # incidents starting within 24h of a deploy but not linked to one
$ while IFS=$'\t' read -r num link start close; do
    [ "$link" = "unlinked" ] || continue
    s=$(date -d "$start" +%s)
    awk -F'\t' -v s="$s" '{d=systime(); cmd="date -d \""$1"\" +%s"; cmd|getline t;
      close(cmd); if (s-t > 0 && s-t < 86400) print "  near deploy "$2}' deploys.tsv \
      | head -1 | sed "s/^/INC-$num/"
  done < incidents.tsv
  INC-4388  near deploy 4f2a91c8       ⚠ probably a change failure
  INC-4401  near deploy a913bb02       ⚠
  -> failure rate is understated by at least 2 of 61 (3.3 points)

CHECK 5 — RECOVERY MEASURED TO THE WRONG EVENT

$ jq -r '.[] | select(.restored_at) |

  # ci/record-deployment.sh — called by the deploy job AND by any manual
  # deploy script, so nothing is invisible
  #!/usr/bin/env bash
  set -euo pipefail
  SERVICE="${1:?service}"; ENV="${2:?env}"; DIGEST="${3:?digest}"
  PREV=$(./ci/last-deployed-sha.sh "$SERVICE" "$ENV" || echo "")
  COMMITS=$([ -n "$PREV" ] && git rev-list "$PREV..HEAD" | jq -R . | jq -s . || echo '[]')

        --arg sha "$(git rev-parse HEAD)" --arg t "$(date -u +%FT%TZ)" \
        --arg run "${GITHUB_RUN_ID:-manual}" --argjson c "$COMMITS" \

    | tee -a /var/log/deployments.jsonl \
    | curl -sS -X POST "$METRICS_URL/deployments" -d @-

💡 A rising change failure rate is sometimes a success signal. Q3's 19% came from shipping four times more often, which surfaced a class of schema problem that low frequency had been hiding — and it was fixable once visible. 🎯 Key Takeaways

  • The four keys measure the delivery system, not people — deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate and recovery time.
  • They only work as pairs: throughput (frequency, lead time) against stability (failure rate, recovery). Reporting one alone removes the constraint that keeps the set honest.
  • Definitions decide the numbers. The same team measured 9 days or 4 hours of lead time depending only on where the clock starts.
  • Prefer the definitions that include the waiting — first commit or PR opened — because the waiting is usually where the time goes.
  • Publish a definitions document and never change one silently: what counts as a deployment, when lead time starts, what makes a change a failure, when recovery ends, and the unit of measurement.
  • Every metric is gameable: empty redeploys (frequency), a merge-time clock (lead time), undeclared incidents (failure rate), late declaration and early resolution (recovery).
  • If one metric moves a lot and none of the others move, suspect the instrumentation — a measured 340% frequency rise was 69% empty deploys from a nightly job.
  • Cross-check continuously: deploys with zero commits, running digests with no deployment event, incidents unlinked but suspiciously close to a deploy, and commits per deploy.
  • Measure recovery to user-visible restoration, not to the fix deploy — the difference understated recovery by about 45% in one dataset.
  • Use p50 and p85, never the mean: one long-lived branch destroys a mean, and the gap between p50 and p85 is itself a diagnostic.
  • DORA does not measure value, reliability as users experience it, developer experience, security, cost or toil. Pair it with SLOs, time-to-patch, cost per change and a survey.
  • Benchmarking against "elite performers" produces theatre. Trend against your own past and ask which of the four is the constraint right now.
  • Decompose the constraint or it is not actionable: 9 days of lead time was 3.1 branch age, 4.2 review wait, 0.4 merge, 1.3 deploy.
  • Split review wait into waiting to be picked up (an ownership problem) and being reviewed (a size problem) — 2.9 of the 4.2 days was nobody having claimed the PR.
  • A change failure rate without categories is unusable. Categorising showed 54% were schema, all mapping to known migration anti-patterns — which became a quarter's plan.
  • Expect the constraint to move after every fix: review wait → branch age → schema failures, across three measured quarters.
  • Write the prediction down before acting, including the side effects — predicting that failure rate might rise with frequency prevented a panic when it did.
  • A rising failure rate can be progress: shipping four times more often surfaced a hidden class of problem that was then fixable.
  • Build the data-quality panel first and hold the report until it is clean — a dashboard is trusted by default for about a quarter, which is long enough for wrong numbers to reach someone's objectives.
  • Never build a per-person view. Once these numbers affect a review they stop being measurements, and you lose the diagnostic that tells you where delivery is actually slow.
