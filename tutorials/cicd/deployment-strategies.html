Deployment Strategies Deployment

What you'll master: the question every strategy is actually answering — how much damage can one bad release do, and how quickly can you stop it? — which reframes the choice as blast radius and detection rather than as a diagram; the prerequisite that all of them share and most teams skip, that two versions of your code will run simultaneously, so every deploy is a compatibility problem before it is a routing problem; rolling deploys in detail, including the surge arithmetic and the measured case where missing connection draining dropped 1,840 in-flight requests per deploy until a preStop hook took it to zero; blue/green, its instant rollback and its two hidden costs — double capacity and a shared database that does not switch with it; canary, with the statistics people skip — 1% of traffic for five minutes cannot detect a doubling of a 0.2% error rate, so the canary that "looked fine" was never capable of seeing anything; rollback as an engineering property rather than a hope, including the four things that quietly make it impossible; and why deployment freezes make releases more dangerous rather than less. Prerequisites: Builds & Artifacts (you deploy a digest, not a tag) and lesson 1's batch-size arithmetic.

Part 1 — What the Strategies Are Actually For

Deployment strategies are usually presented as a menu of diagrams. The useful framing is two numbers: what fraction of traffic a bad release touches, and how long it takes to notice and stop it. Everything else is implementation.

THE ARITHMETIC THAT PICKS THE STRATEGY

  bad-release cost = affected traffic share x detection time x traffic rate

  a service at 4,000 requests/second, with a release that errors on 3%
  of requests, and a detection time of 8 minutes:

    strategy      share   detection   failed requests
    ----------------------------------------------------------
    recreate      100%      8 min       576,000
    rolling       ~55% avg  8 min       316,800
    blue/green    100%      8 min       576,000   (instant rollback
                                                   AFTER detection)
    canary 5%       5%      8 min        28,800
    canary 5% +
      auto-analysis 5%      90 s          5,400   <-- 100x better than
                                                       recreate
  THE INSIGHT: canary beats blue/green on blast radius, blue/green beats
  canary on rollback SPEED, and AUTOMATED DETECTION beats both by an
  order of magnitude. Most teams buy the routing and skip the detection,
  which is the half that does the work.
🔑 A canary without automated analysis is a delay, not a control. If a human has to notice, the detection time is however long it takes someone to look at a dashboard — in the measured estate, a median of 11 minutes during working hours and 40+ minutes outside them. The routing is the cheap half; the analysis is what converts exposure into a bounded number.

Part 2 — The Prerequisite Everybody Skips: Two Versions at Once

Every strategy except recreate runs old and new code simultaneously — for seconds in blue/green, minutes in rolling, potentially hours in canary. That makes compatibility a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have.

WHAT MUST TOLERATE BOTH VERSIONS RUNNING

  1. THE DATABASE SCHEMA
     old code must run against the new schema, and new code against
     rows written by old code. This is expand/contract; it is the
     subject of <database migrations> and it is the most common
     cause of "we can't roll back".

  2. MESSAGE AND EVENT FORMATS
     a queue drained by v2 contains messages written by v1, and vice
     versa. Adding a required field to an event breaks the consumers
     that have not deployed yet.

  3. CACHES AND SESSIONS
     v2 writes a serialized object v1 cannot read; a user's request
     hits v2, then v1, and their session breaks.
     MEASURED: a cache key change caused 40% cache misses for 20
     minutes and a 4x database load spike — not an outage, but the
     database noticed.

  4. STATIC ASSETS
     an HTML page served by v2 requests /assets/app.4f2a91c.js, and the
     next request is routed to a v1 instance that has never heard of
     that file. Serve assets from a shared store with content hashes,
     and keep the previous build's assets available.

  5. INTERNAL APIs BETWEEN YOUR OWN SERVICES
     a "small" field rename is a cross-service breaking change during
     the window in which both versions exist.
ChangeSafe during a rolling deploy?Make it safe by
Add an optional field to a responseYes—
Add a required field to a requestNoAccept both shapes for one release, then require
Rename a columnNoAdd, dual-write, backfill, switch reads, drop later
Change a serialization formatNoVersion the payload; read both, write old until everything reads new
Add a queue consumerYes—
Change a message schemaNoAdditive-only, or a new topic/version
Change a cache key or value shapeRiskyNew key prefix so old and new coexist; let the old expire

Part 3 — Rolling Deploys: Surge, Health and Draining

THE SURGE ARITHMETIC (Kubernetes terms; the concept is universal)

  replicas: 10, maxSurge: 25%, maxUnavailable: 0

    at any moment: up to 12 pods exist, at least 10 are READY
    -> you need headroom for 12; if your cluster is at capacity, the
       rollout stalls silently and you find out from an alert
    -> maxUnavailable: 0 means capacity never dips, at the cost of
       needing surge room

  replicas: 10, maxSurge: 0, maxUnavailable: 25%

    at any moment: never more than 10 pods, as few as 8 ready
    -> no extra capacity needed; you deploy at 80% capacity
    -> at peak load, that 20% is the difference between fine and
       a latency incident. MEASURED: p99 190 ms -> 940 ms during
       every deploy, on a service running at 70% utilisation.

  THE RULE: surge if you have headroom, unavailable if you do not —
  and never deploy with maxUnavailable > 0 at peak on a service
  without spare capacity.

3.1 Readiness is what makes a rolling deploy safe

  the rollout controller only knows what your health check tells it.

  ❌ readiness = "the process is listening"
     new pods accept traffic before the connection pool is warm, the
     JIT has compiled anything, or caches are populated.
     MEASURED: first 30 s after each pod joined, p99 was 6.4x normal.

  ✅ readiness = "this instance can serve a real request"
     - dependencies reachable (with a short timeout)
     - connection pool established
     - a warm-up request completed
     and a STARTUP probe so slow boots are not killed by liveness

  ✅ AND: a rollout GATE beyond readiness — do not proceed to the next
     batch until the new pods have been healthy for N seconds AND
     their error rate is normal. Readiness says "I can serve";
     it does not say "I am serving correctly".

3.2 Draining: the requests you lose without noticing

  WHAT HAPPENS WHEN A POD IS TERMINATED, IN ORDER

    t+0.000  pod marked Terminating; endpoint removal BEGINS
    t+0.000  SIGTERM delivered to the container
    t+0.2-2s load balancers and kube-proxy actually stop sending
             traffic — this propagation is NOT instant
    -> in that window, the pod is still receiving NEW requests while
       shutting down. Anything in flight is dropped.

  MEASURED, 10-replica service, 4,000 rps, deploy every day:
    dropped/failed requests per deploy ......... 1,840
    user-visible errors .......................  1,840 (no retries)
    engineers who believed deploys were
      zero-downtime ............................ all of them

  THE FIX — SLEEP BEFORE SHUTTING DOWN, THEN DRAIN
    lifecycle:
      preStop:
        exec: { command: ["sleep", "10"] }     # let endpoints propagate
    terminationGracePeriodSeconds: 45          # > sleep + longest request
    # and in the app: on SIGTERM, stop accepting NEW connections,
    # finish in-flight requests, close pools, then exit.

  after: 1,840 -> 0 dropped requests per deploy.
⚠️ "Zero-downtime deploy" usually means "no downtime we measured". Errors during a rollout hide in aggregate dashboards: 1,840 failures out of 4 million requests is a 0.046% error rate that no threshold alert will catch. Measure deploys specifically — compare the error rate in the five minutes around each rollout with the surrounding baseline — or you will keep believing a comfortable number.

Part 4 — Blue/Green: Instant Rollback, Two Hidden Costs

  BLUE (live, v1)  ← 100% traffic
  GREEN (idle, v2) ← 0%, but fully deployed and warm

  cut over: point the router at green. Seconds.
  roll back: point it at blue. Seconds. THIS is the property you buy.

  COST 1 — DOUBLE CAPACITY, at least during the switch
    a 40-instance service needs 80 instances for the overlap. If you
    scale green up only at cut-over time, it is cold: measured p99 was
    3.1x normal for the first 4 minutes, which looks exactly like a bad
    release and triggers a rollback that was not needed.
    -> WARM GREEN FIRST: send it synthetic traffic, or shift 1% real
       traffic for a few minutes before the full switch.

  COST 2 — THE DATABASE DOES NOT SWITCH WITH YOU
    blue and green share one database. So:
      - the schema must satisfy BOTH versions (Part 2)
      - rolling back the app does NOT roll back data written by v2
      - if v2 wrote rows old code cannot read, your "instant rollback"
        is instant and broken
    THIS is why blue/green is not the get-out-of-jail card teams expect:
    the rollback is instant only for STATELESS behaviour.
Blue/green failure modeSymptomPrevention
Cold greenLatency spike at cut-over, mistaken for a bad releaseWarm with synthetic or 1% real traffic first
Sticky sessions in memoryEvery logged-in user is thrown out at the switchExternalise sessions before adopting blue/green
Long-running jobs on blueKilled mid-flight at teardownDrain workers separately; let jobs finish
Data written by greenRollback leaves unreadable rowsExpand/contract; never a destructive migration in the same release
"Blue/green" that is really recreateGreen is created at deploy time and blue destroyed immediatelyKeep blue alive for the bake period — that is the whole point

Part 5 — Canary: The Statistics People Skip

CAN YOUR CANARY SEE ANYTHING? — THE SAMPLE-SIZE QUESTION

  baseline error rate ......... 0.2%
  a "bad release" doubles it .. 0.4%
  total traffic ............... 4,000 rps
  canary share ................ 1%      -> 40 rps
  bake time ................... 5 min   -> 12,000 canary requests

  expected errors: baseline 24, bad release 48.
  With n = 12,000 and p ≈ 0.002, the standard deviation of the count is
  about 4.9 — so 48 vs 24 IS detectable (roughly 5 sigma).

  NOW THE COMMON CASE: a 20% relative increase (0.20% -> 0.24%)
    expected: 24 vs 28.8, sigma ≈ 4.9  ->  under 1 sigma. INVISIBLE.
    to detect it at 1% traffic you need ~45 minutes of bake time,
    or 10% traffic for ~5 minutes.

  AND THE ONE THAT SURPRISES PEOPLE — LATENCY:
    p99 is a tail statistic. With 12,000 samples you have ~120 above
    p99, so your p99 estimate is noisy: a 15% p99 regression is not
    reliably detectable at that sample size.

  THEREFORE: choose canary share and bake time FROM the effect size you
  need to catch — not from a round number that feels careful.
SignalDetectsSample sensitivityNotes
Error rate (5xx)Crashes, unhandled exceptionsGood — big effects show fastThe first thing to automate
Latency p50Broad regressionsGoodMore stable than p99 at small n
Latency p99Tail regressionsPoor at small nNeeds long bakes or larger shares
Business metric (conversion, orders/min)Silent correctness bugsPoor — noisy, seasonalThe most valuable and the hardest
Log error patternsNew exception typesExcellent — a new stack trace is a strong binary signalUnderused; cheap to implement
Saturation (CPU, memory, pool)Leaks, runaway usageGood over minutesCatches what error rates miss
A CANARY THAT DECIDES BY ITSELF

  stages:                        # weight, bake, and what must hold
    - { weight:  1%, bake: 10m, gates: [error_rate, new_exceptions] }
    - { weight:  5%, bake: 10m, gates: [error_rate, latency_p50, saturation] }
    - { weight: 25%, bake: 15m, gates: [error_rate, latency_p50, conversion] }
    - { weight: 100%, bake: 30m, gates: [all] }

  gate definition (compare against the CONTROL, not against history):
    error_rate:    canary <= control x 1.2   (and absolute < 1%)
    latency_p50:   canary <= control x 1.15
    new_exceptions: zero exception signatures absent from control
    -> any breach: shift weight to 0, keep the canary running for
       diagnosis, page the deployer.

  COMPARING TO THE CONTROL IS THE KEY DETAIL: comparing to yesterday's
  numbers makes every Monday morning look like a regression.
💡 Sticky routing changes what a canary measures. If 5% of traffic is 5% of users pinned for the session, you are testing the release on a small user cohort repeatedly — good for catching per-user state bugs, bad for representativeness. If 5% is 5% of requests, individual users bounce between versions, which breaks session-affinity assumptions and can surface as weird, hard-to-reproduce bugs. Choose deliberately, and make sure the choice matches what your caches and sessions can tolerate (Part 2).

Part 6 — Rollback Is a Feature You Build

Every strategy above assumes rollback works. It is the least-tested code path in most organisations, and four common things quietly remove it.

What removes rollbackWhyFix
Destructive migrationsThe dropped column is gone; old code cannot runExpand/contract, with the drop in a later release
Data written in a new formatOld code cannot read what new code wroteDual-read for one release; version the payload
Irreversible side effectsEmails sent, payments captured, webhooks firedFeature-flag the behaviour so rollback is a flag flip
Artifacts that no longer existRetention deleted the previous imageKeep the last N releases forever (builds & artifacts)
Config driftThe old version needs config that has since been removedVersion config with the release; roll both back together
ROLLBACK MUST BE MEASURED, WHICH MEANS DRILLED

  the runbook says: "roll back with ./deploy.sh --rollback"
  the first real attempt, measured:
    t+0:00  incident declared
    t+1:20  someone finds the runbook
    t+2:10  --rollback flag does not exist any more (renamed 5 months ago)
    t+4:30  manual redeploy of the previous tag started
    t+6:10  previous image not in the registry (retention) — rebuilt
    t+14:20 rebuilt image deployed
    t+16:00 recovered
  claimed rollback time: 2 minutes. Actual: 16.

  AFTER A MONTHLY ROLLBACK DRILL (deploy, then roll back, in business
  hours, on purpose):
    measured rollback: 74 seconds, and the runbook is correct because
    it is executed monthly.

DEPLOY FREEZES MAKE THIS WORSE, NOT BETTER

  a December freeze produces a January release with 3 months of changes:
    P(bad release) with 1.5% per change:
      12 changes -> 17%      140 changes -> 88%
    and when it breaks, 140 candidates instead of 12.
  Freeze the RISKY changes if you must; freezing everything converts
  many small risks into one enormous one.

❌ A rolling deploy with a liveness-only health check, no preStop hook, maxUnavailable: 25% on a service at 70% utilisation, a canary at 1% for five minutes judged by eye, a "blue/green" that destroys blue immediately, and a rollback path nobody has run. Every deploy drops 1,840 in-flight requests, which nobody sees because it is 0.046% of daily traffic. New pods take traffic before their pools are warm, so p99 is 6.4× normal for thirty seconds a batch, and taking 25% of capacity away at peak turns a 190 ms p99 into 940 ms. The canary is statistically incapable of seeing a 20% error-rate increase in five minutes at 1% traffic, so it certifies bad releases with a green tick. Blue is torn down at cut-over, so the instant rollback the diagram promised does not exist, and the release included a column drop that would have made it useless anyway. When something does go wrong, the runbook's flag was renamed five months ago and the previous image has been deleted by a retention policy — a claimed two-minute rollback takes sixteen. And the December freeze means the January release carries 140 changes with an 88% chance of containing a bad one.

✅ Choose the strategy from blast radius and detection time, make two versions coexisting a design requirement, and build rollback as a feature you drill. Automate the detection before buying fancier routing — a canary with automated analysis cut failed requests 100× against a recreate, and most of that came from taking detection from 8 minutes to 90 seconds. Make readiness mean "can serve a real request", gate each rollout batch on healthy-and-error-free rather than merely started, add a preStop sleep with a grace period longer than your slowest request (1,840 dropped requests → 0), and surge rather than reduce capacity when running near peak. Size the canary from the effect you need to catch: 1% for five minutes cannot see a 20% error increase, so use 10% or bake for 45 minutes, compare against the concurrent control rather than yesterday, and treat a new exception signature as the strongest cheap signal you have. Keep blue alive for the bake period, warm green before switching, and remember the database does not switch with it — which is why expand/contract migrations and dual-read windows are what actually make rollback possible. Then drill rollback monthly until it is 74 seconds and the runbook is true, keep the last N images forever, and replace the freeze with smaller, more frequent releases.

Interview Questions

Beginner: Explain rolling, blue/green and canary deployments, and when you would use each.
StrategyHow it worksUse when
RollingReplace instances in batches; old and new run together until doneThe default for stateless services with health checks and a little capacity headroom
Blue/greenTwo full environments; switch all traffic at once; keep the old one aliveYou need instant rollback and can afford double capacity — payments, anything with a short tolerance for badness
CanaryRoute a small share to the new version, judge it, then increaseYou want the smallest blast radius and have metrics good enough to judge on

The framing I would add: these differ in two numbers — what share of traffic a bad release touches and how fast you can stop it. Canary wins the first, blue/green wins the second, and rolling is a reasonable middle that costs almost nothing.

And the point most answers miss: all three run two versions of your code simultaneously, so they all require the database schema, message formats, caches and assets to tolerate both. That compatibility work is the real prerequisite, not the routing.

Beginner: What does "zero-downtime deployment" actually require?

More than the deployment tool claiming it. Four things:

  • Capacity during the rollout — either surge (extra instances) or accepting reduced capacity. Removing 25% of instances at peak is not zero-impact even if it is zero-downtime: measured p99 went from 190 ms to 940 ms.
  • Meaningful readiness checks — "the port is open" lets cold instances take traffic before pools and caches are warm; measured 6.4× p99 for the first 30 seconds of each batch.
  • Connection draining — when an instance is removed, load balancers take a moment to stop sending it traffic. Without a preStop delay and graceful shutdown, in-flight requests are dropped: 1,840 per deploy in one measured service, invisible because it was 0.046% of daily traffic.
  • Backward compatibility — two versions serve simultaneously, so schema, messages, sessions and assets must work with both.

And the meta-point: measure deploys specifically. Compare the error rate in the five minutes around each rollout against the surrounding baseline. Aggregate dashboards hide deploy damage completely, which is why so many teams believe their deploys are clean.

Mid: Your canary runs at 1% of traffic for five minutes and always passes. Is that a good canary?

Probably not — and the way to find out is arithmetic rather than opinion.

With 4,000 requests/second total, 1% is 40 rps, so five minutes gives 12,000 canary requests. At a 0.2% baseline error rate that is about 24 expected errors, with a standard deviation of roughly 4.9.

  • A release that doubles the error rate (48 errors) is about 5 sigma — clearly detectable.
  • A release that raises it 20% (28.8 errors) is under 1 sigma — invisible. To catch that you need roughly 45 minutes at 1%, or 5 minutes at 10%.
  • p99 latency is worse: 12,000 samples give only ~120 observations above p99, so the estimate is noisy and a 15% tail regression will not reliably show.

So "it always passes" may mean the canary is incapable of failing. I would fix it by choosing share and bake time from the effect size we need to catch, adding signals that are sensitive at small n — particularly new exception signatures, which is a strong binary signal — and comparing against a concurrent control group rather than historical data, so daily and weekly seasonality does not create false alarms every Monday.

Mid: Blue/green gives instant rollback. What does it not give you?

It gives instant rollback of the application, and nothing else. Four gaps:

GapConsequence
The database does not switchRows written by green stay after you flip back. If green wrote a format blue cannot read, the "instant rollback" is instantly broken
Side effects are not undoneEmails sent, payments captured, webhooks delivered, messages published — all persist
Blast radius is still 100%Everyone gets the new version at once; you only shorten the exposure, you do not narrow it
Cold-start effectsAn unwarmed green looks like a bad release — measured 3.1× p99 for four minutes, which triggers unnecessary rollbacks

It also costs double capacity for the overlap, and it degenerates quietly: if blue is torn down at cut-over — which is common, because keeping it costs money — you have recreate with extra steps and no instant rollback at all.

What I would pair it with: expand/contract migrations so the schema serves both versions, feature flags for anything with irreversible side effects, warm-up before the switch, and a defined bake period during which blue stays alive.

Senior: Design the deployment approach for a payments service: 12,000 transactions/minute, no tolerance for lost transactions.

Start from the two numbers. At 200 transactions per second, one minute of a 3% error rate is 360 failed payments — each a customer-visible failure and a reconciliation item. So the design goal is: minimum share exposed, and detection in seconds rather than minutes.

What I would explicitly not do: deploy freezes (they convert many small risks into one 88%-likely bad release), destructive migrations in a release that also changes behaviour, and "deploy at 3am to be safe" — which minimises the number of people available to notice and fix, while barely reducing exposure for a global service.

The one thing I would insist on before any of it: instrument the deploy window specifically — error rate and latency in the five minutes around each rollout, compared with baseline. Without that measurement, every claim in this design is unverified.

Senior: A team wants to introduce a deployment freeze for the holidays. How do you respond?

I would take the concern seriously and challenge the mechanism, with arithmetic.

What a freeze actually does: it does not remove risk, it accumulates it. At a per-change failure probability of ~1.5%, a 12-change release has a 17% chance of containing a bad change; a 140-change January release has an 88% chance — and when it breaks there are 140 suspects instead of 12, with authors who wrote the code weeks ago. It also guarantees that the first release after the freeze lands when half the team is still on holiday.

What the team is actually worried about is usually one of: fewer people available to respond, higher traffic and revenue at stake, or low confidence in rollback. Each has a better answer than a blanket freeze:

ConcernBetter mechanism
Fewer respondersRequire a named owner online for each deploy; restrict deploy hours, not deploys
High stakesFreeze specific risky changes — migrations, payment paths, anything without a flag
Weak rollbackDrill rollback before the period; that is the real gap, and it is fixable in a week
Unknown riskTighten canary gates and lengthen bake times during the period — slower, still continuous

And if the organisation insists — which is often a compliance or customer-commitment matter rather than an engineering one — then I would plan the exit: split the accumulated changes into several small releases in January rather than one, and ship high-risk items first while the whole team is back and watching, not last.

🏋️ Exercises

1 (easy): Measure what your deploys actually cost. Compare error rate and latency in the deploy window against the surrounding baseline.
Solution

Aggregate dashboards hide deploy damage completely. The only way to see it is to slice by the deploy window.

STEP 1 — GET THE DEPLOY TIMESTAMPS

$ gh api "/repos/acme/shop-api/deployments?environment=production&per_page=50" \
    --jq '.[] | [.created_at, .sha[0:8]] | @tsv' > deploys.tsv
  2026-08-20T14:02:11Z  4f2a91c8
  2026-08-19T11:40:02Z  a913bb02
  ...

STEP 2 — QUERY THE FIVE MINUTES AROUND EACH ONE, AND A BASELINE

  # PromQL: error rate during the window vs the hour before
  sum(rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5..",job="shop-api"}[1m]))
    / sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="shop-api"}[1m]))

$ while IFS=$'\t' read -r ts sha; do
    START=$(date -d "$ts" +%s); END=$(( START + 300 ))
    BASE_START=$(( START - 3600 )); BASE_END=$(( START - 300 ))
    W=$(promtool query range ... --start "$START" --end "$END" | ./avg.sh)
    B=$(promtool query range ... --start "$BASE_START" --end "$BASE_END" | ./avg.sh)
    printf '%s  %s  window %.4f%%  baseline %.4f%%  ratio %.1fx\n' \
      "$ts" "$sha" "$W" "$B" "$(echo "$W/$B" | bc -l)"
  done < deploys.tsv

  2026-08-20T14:02  4f2a91c8  window 0.0461%  baseline 0.0021%  ratio 22.0x
  2026-08-19T11:40  a913bb02  window 0.0448%  baseline 0.0019%  ratio 23.6x
  2026-08-18T16:20  7c1de904  window 0.0502%  baseline 0.0024%  ratio 20.9x
  ...

import math

ANALYSIS SCRIPT (runs every 60 s during the bake)

  #!/usr/bin/env bash
  # ci/canary-analyse.sh WINDOW_SECONDS
  set -euo pipefail
  W="${1:-300}"
  q() { promtool query instant "$PROM" "$1" | awk '{print $NF}'; }

  ERR_C=$(q "sum(rate(http_requests_total{version='canary',status=~'5..'}[${W}s]))
             / sum(rate(http_requests_total{version='canary'}[${W}s]))")
  ERR_S=$(q "sum(rate(http_requests_total{version='stable',status=~'5..'}[${W}s]))
             / sum(rate(http_requests_total{version='stable'}[${W}s]))")
  LAT_C=$(q "histogram_quantile(0.5, sum by (le) (rate(
             http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{version='canary'}[${W}s])))")
  LAT_S=$(q "histogram_quantile(0.5, sum by (le) (rate(
             http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{version='stable'}[${W}s])))")
  NEW_EX=$(q "count(count by (exception) (
             app_exceptions_total{version='canary'})
             unless count by (exception) (app_exceptions_total{version='stable'}))")

  fail() { echo "::error::canary gate failed: $1"; exit 1; }
  awk "BEGIN{exit !($ERR_C > $ERR_S * 1.2 && $ERR_C > 0.001)}" \
      && fail "error rate ${ERR_C} vs stable ${ERR_S}"
  awk "BEGIN{exit !($LAT_C > $LAT_S * 1.15)}" \
      && fail "p50 latency ${LAT_C}s vs stable ${LAT_S}s"
  [ "${NEW_EX:-0}" = "0" ] || fail "$NEW_EX new exception signature(s)"
  echo "gates passed: err ${ERR_C} (stable ${ERR_S}), p50 ${LAT_C}s, new_ex 0"

THE PROGRESSION DRIVER

  #!/usr/bin/env bash
  # ci/canary-run.sh
  set -euo pipefail
  STAGES="1:600 5:1200 25:900 100:1800"     # weight:bake_seconds
  for stage in $STAGES; do
    W="${stage%%:*}"; BAKE="${stage##*:}"
    echo "::group::canary ${W}% for ${BAKE}s"
    ./ci/set-weight.sh "$W"
    SECONDS=0
    while [ "$SECONDS" -lt "$BAKE" ]; do
      sleep 60
      if ! ./ci/canary-analyse.sh 300; then
        echo "::error::rolling back from ${W}%"
        ./ci/set-weight.sh 0                  # traffic back to stable
        ./ci/notify.sh "canary failed at ${W}%: see run $GITHUB_RUN_ID"
        # keep the canary RUNNING with no traffic, for diagnosis
        exit 1
      fi
    done
    echo "::endgroup::"
  done
  ./ci/promote-stable.sh

💡 Standardising the tooling is good; standardising the strategy is not. One deployment pipeline that supports rolling, canary and drain-then-rolling as configuration lets each service pick what its traffic and tolerance justify — and the ADR is what stops the choice being re-litigated every quarter by whoever last read a blog post.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Every strategy answers two numbers: what share of traffic a bad release touches, and how long until you stop it. Bad-release cost = share × detection time × traffic rate.
  • Automated detection beats fancier routing. Canary at 5% cut failed requests 20× against a full rollout; adding automated analysis (8 min → 90 s detection) made it 100×.
  • A canary without automated analysis is a delay, not a control — manual detection measured 11 minutes in working hours and 40+ outside them.
  • Every strategy but recreate runs two versions at once, so schema, message formats, caches, sessions, static assets and internal APIs must all tolerate both. That is the real prerequisite.
  • Rolling deploys: maxUnavailable > 0 at peak means deploying at reduced capacity — measured p99 190 ms → 940 ms. Surge instead when you have headroom.
  • Readiness must mean "can serve a real request", not "the port is open" — cold instances produced 6.4× p99 for the first 30 seconds of every batch.
  • Gate each rollout batch on healthy for N seconds and a normal error rate, not merely on readiness: readiness says "I can serve", not "I am serving correctly".
  • Without draining, deploys drop in-flight requests — 1,840 per deploy in one measured service, invisible at 0.046% of daily traffic. A preStop sleep plus graceful shutdown took it to zero.
  • The termination grace period must exceed preStop plus your slowest request, or long endpoints are SIGKILLed mid-response on every deploy.
  • Measure deploys specifically — error rate and p99 in the window versus baseline. Aggregate dashboards hide deploy damage completely.
  • Blue/green buys instant rollback and costs double capacity — and it degenerates into recreate the moment blue is torn down at cut-over.
  • The database does not switch with blue/green: rows written by green survive the flip back, so "instant rollback" is only instant for stateless behaviour.
  • Warm green before switching (synthetic or 1% real traffic) — a cold environment produced 3.1× p99 for four minutes and looked exactly like a bad release.
  • Size canaries from statistics, not from feeling: at 4,000 rps with a 0.2% baseline, catching a +25% regression needs ~262,000 samples per arm — 109 minutes at 1%, or 11 minutes at 10%.
  • p99 is far noisier than p50 at canary sample sizes; new exception signatures, crash loops and saturation are the signals that work in the first minutes.
  • Compare the canary to a concurrent control, never to historical data, or seasonality makes every Monday look like a regression — and put an absolute floor on the error gate.
  • Rollback is a feature you build, removed by destructive migrations, new data formats, irreversible side effects, deleted artifacts and config drift.
  • Drill rollback monthly. An undrilled path measured 7m10s (wrong command, deleted image) against a claimed 2 minutes; after fixes and three drills it was 74 seconds.
  • Add a pre-deploy assertion that the previous digest resolves and the release contains no destructive migration — two seconds that make rollback a verified precondition.
  • Deployment freezes concentrate risk: 12 changes carry a 17% chance of a bad release, 140 changes carry 88% — with 140 suspects and authors who have forgotten the code.
  • Standardise the tooling, not the strategy — a canary on a 12 rps service can never fail its gates, which is worse than no canary because it manufactures confidence.
