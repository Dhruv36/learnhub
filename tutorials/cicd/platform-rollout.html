Platform Engineering & Rollout Operating & Measuring

What you'll master: the framing that decides whether a platform team succeeds — the platform is a product whose users can refuse it, so adoption is the only honest success metric, and the two failure modes that follow from ignoring that: the queue, where a central team owns everyone's pipeline and becomes the bottleneck, and the mandate, where a standard nobody can escape is circumvented in ways you cannot see; what a paved road actually contains and the property that makes it work — it must be the easiest path, not the required one, measured by voluntary adoption reaching 57 of 60 repositories without a mandate; the rollout playbook for changing 60 repositories at once, where automated migration PRs plus a dated deprecation took adoption from 12 to 57 in twelve weeks and the stragglers turned out to be unowned repositories — the migration's most valuable finding; escape hatches as a feedback channel rather than a loophole, since high opt-out on one feature is information, not disobedience; running the platform with an SLO, an on-call rotation and a roadmap driven by measured constraints; and the funding argument that survives a budget review, in engineer-hours rather than adjectives. Prerequisites: the whole track — this lesson is where reusable workflows, gate rollout and constraint analysis become an operating model.

🔑 Adoption is the metric because it is the only one that cannot be faked. Compliance can be satisfied by a trivially-passing job; ticket throughput measures the bottleneck's width, not its necessity. Voluntary adoption — teams choosing the paved road because it is easier than not using it — is a direct measurement of whether the platform is worth its cost.

Part 3 — Rolling Out a Change to Sixty Repositories

THE PLAYBOOK (the same shape used for gates, flags and migrations)

  1. MEASURE the current state — per repository, from data you have
  2. PILOT with one willing team; publish the before/after
  3. AUTOMATE the migration: a script that edits, a bot that raises PRs
  4. DASHBOARD adoption, visible to everyone
  5. DEPRECATE with a date, warnings, and a migration guide
  6. REMOVE, on the published date

⚠️ Raise the pull requests; do not merge them. A platform team that self-merges into sixty repositories teaches every team that CI belongs to someone else — and the next breaking change lands on people who have never read their own pipeline. The automation removes the tedium; the review keeps the ownership, which is what makes the following migration cheaper rather than identical.

The durable argument is delivery capability — teams shipping daily instead of fortnightly, security fixes reaching production in a day instead of eleven weeks — with the engineer-hours as corroboration rather than as the case.

❌ A central team that owns sixty pipelines, an 84-ticket backlog with a 23-day median age, a mandate that produced 100% compliance and 41 escape scripts, no escape-hatch process, no SLO, and a roadmap built from whatever was loudest last week. Teams are blocked on platform for changes to their own build, so they lose both velocity and any sense of ownership — and when a pipeline breaks at 2am nobody on the owning team knows how it works. The mandate's compliance number is meaningless: seventeen repositories copied the shared workflow and edited it, nine satisfy the required check with a job that does nothing, and the circumvention is invisible by design, so the platform team believes the rollout succeeded. Because leaving is forbidden rather than recorded, the ten genuine platform defects behind those workarounds are never discovered. And the funding case rests entirely on hours saved, which a sharper spreadsheet dismantles in the next budget review.

✅ Build a paved road that is the easiest path, measure voluntary adoption, automate migrations without merging them, treat opt-outs as feedback, and run the platform as a service with an SLO and a constraint-driven roadmap. Own the shared building blocks — reusable workflows, composite actions, CI images, runner pools, scaffolding — and let each team own its own pipeline as a dozen lines calling them, so a new service reaches production in fourteen minutes and a fleet-wide change is one PR and a tag move rather than 61 PRs over eleven weeks. Roll changes out on the pattern this whole track uses: measure, pilot with one willing team, publish that peer team's numbers, automate the migration PRs and let teams merge them, dashboard adoption, then deprecate with a date and a guide — which took adoption from 12 to 57 of 60 in twelve weeks and surfaced two unowned repositories as its most valuable finding. Keep an escape hatch and read it: ten of fourteen opt-outs were platform defects that only surfaced because leaving was allowed. Then publish an SLO, staff a support rota, drive the roadmap from the measured constraints in the four keys, and make the funding case on delivery capability — lead time 9 days to 1.1, deploys 2.1/week to 11 — with the engineer-hours as corroboration rather than as the argument.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is a platform team for, and what should it not do?

A platform team builds the shared capability that lets product teams deliver software safely without each of them solving CI, deployment, observability and security independently. Concretely: reusable workflows, composite actions, CI images, runner pools, scaffolding and documentation.

What it should not do is own everyone's pipeline. That produces the queue: in one measured estate, 84 open tickets with a 23-day median age, eleven teams blocked on platform changes, and no shared improvements shipped in two quarters — because the team spent 70% of its time on bespoke requests.

The right split is: platform owns the building blocks, and each team owns its own pipeline as a dozen lines calling them, plus whatever ci/*.sh is specific to that codebase. Teams keep ownership and can debug their own build; platform ships improvements to everyone at once.

The single best measure of whether this is working is voluntary adoption — how many teams use the shared path because it is easier, not because they were told to.

Beginner: Why is a "paved road" better than a mandated standard?

Because a mandate produces compliance, and compliance can be satisfied without using the thing.

The measured example: an estate mandated the standard pipeline and reported 100% compliance. Underneath it, 41 repositories had added a custom-steps.sh escape, 17 had copied the shared workflow and edited it, and 9 satisfied the required check with a job that did nothing. The number was perfect and meaningless, and — worse — the circumvention was invisible, so the platform team believed the rollout had worked.

A paved road inverts the incentive: it must be easier than not using it. When that is true, adoption happens without enforcement — 57 of 60 repositories in the same estate — and when it is not true, low adoption tells you so immediately, which is a diagnosis rather than a hidden failure.

The corollary people find uncomfortable: you must allow teams to leave. Ten of fourteen recorded opt-outs turned out to be platform defects — a timeout that was too short, missing GPU runners, an unsupported monorepo shape — which were only discoverable because leaving was permitted and recorded.

Mid: How do you roll out a pipeline change across 60 repositories?

The same six-step pattern used for gates, flags and migrations throughout this track:

StepDetail
MeasurePer-repository state from data you already have — it ranks the estate in an afternoon
PilotOne willing team; small enough to abandon cheaply
EvidencePublish the pilot's before/after — 212 lines of workflow to 16, build 34m to 11m. Numbers from a peer team persuade where a mandate does not
AutomateA migration script and a bot that raises PRs — for repositories matching the standard shape. The rest get an issue with a link
DeprecateAn adoption dashboard, warnings on the old path, and a published removal date
RemoveOn the date, with a failure message naming the migration guide

Measured adoption on that schedule: 12 repositories at week 1, 34 at week 4, 57 of 60 at week 12.

Two things I would insist on. Raise the PRs but do not merge them — self-merging into sixty repositories teaches teams that CI is somebody else's, and the next change costs exactly as much. And expect the stragglers to be unowned repositories: in this migration the final two had no team, which was the exercise's most valuable finding and the sort of thing otherwise discovered during a security incident.

Mid: A team refuses to use the shared pipeline. What do you do?

Find out why, and treat the answer as product feedback rather than as a compliance problem.

In a measured year, fourteen teams opted out and the reasons sorted into six categories — of which ten were platform defects:

ReasonCountResponse
Build longer than the shared timeout2Raised the limit; made it an input
Needs GPU runners1Added a runner pool
Monorepo with per-package publishing3Built a new shared shape
"We didn't know it existed"4Scaffolding and docs
"The error messages were unhelpful"2Fixed the messages
Genuinely bespoke needs2Left alone; recorded; revisit annually

So the process is: ask, record the reason in a file with an owner and a revisit date, and look for patterns. Several teams opting out for the same reason is a missing capability, not a discipline problem — build it and re-invite them.

The two genuinely bespoke cases are fine. A paved road that 95% of repositories use is a success; the effort to force the last 5% usually costs more than it returns and damages the relationship that makes the next rollout easy.

Senior: How would you justify a platform team's budget?

With two arguments, in a specific order — because the obvious one is the weaker one.

Lead with delivery capability. Across twelve teams, lead time went from 9 days to 1.1 and deployment frequency from 2.1 to 11 per week, while change failure rate fell from 11% to 6%. That is the organisation shipping roughly five times more often with fewer failures — a product-velocity argument that a finance spreadsheet cannot dismantle, because it is about revenue-affecting speed rather than cost.

Support it with engineer-hours, honestly:

CostBeforeAfter
CI outages561 h/yr44 h/yr
Queue time925 h/yr50 h/yr
Retry waiting (flakiness)2,288 h/yr156 h/yr
Per-repo pipeline maintenance960 h/yr180 h/yr

And be honest about the arithmetic: a $394k saving does not by itself fund four engineers at $560k. Presenting it as though it does invites someone to check — and then the whole case looks inflated. The correct framing is that the saving offsets most of the cost and the delivery improvement is the return.

I would also report adoption alongside both, because it is the leading indicator: if adoption is falling, the outcomes will follow, and it is the number that tells you a quarter early.

Senior: What is the general pattern for introducing any change to an engineering organisation?

Every rollout in this track — quality gates, feature flags, admission policies, shared workflows, schema migrations — is the same algorithm:

Measure → Pilot → Evidence → Automate → Deprecate → Remove.

  • Measure from data you already have, so the case is specific rather than aspirational — "our first-attempt pass rate is 61%", not "CI is flaky".
  • Pilot with one willing team, small enough that abandoning it costs nothing. Expect a good proportion of proposals to die here; that is the cheapest possible outcome.
  • Evidence from that pilot, published. A peer team's numbers persuade where a mandate produces circumvention.
  • Automate adoption so the easy path is the new one — migration PRs, scaffolding, defaults — and let the teams merge, so ownership stays with them.
  • Deprecate with a date, a warning on the old path, and a migration guide.
  • Remove on the date, failing with a message that names the guide.

The two most common ways this fails: skipping the pilot, which turns a cheap abandonment into an expensive one; and skipping the removal, which leaves two supported paths forever and doubles the maintenance cost of everything that follows.

And the one non-negotiable: always leave a way out, and read who takes it. The escape hatch is not a loophole — it is the feedback channel that tells you which parts of your change are wrong, and it is how a platform stays a product rather than becoming a policy.

2 (easy): Build the adoption dashboard, and publish it weekly.
Solution 
  #!/usr/bin/env bash
  # platform/adoption.sh — one command, run weekly by CI
  set -euo pipefail
  TOTAL=0; SHARED=0; VERSION_COUNT=""
  : > /tmp/adoption.tsv

  for r in $(gh repo list acme --limit 200 --json name,isArchived \
             --jq '.[] | select(.isArchived|not) | .name'); do
    WF=$(gh api "/repos/acme/$r/contents/.github/workflows" --jq '.[].name' 2>/dev/null || true)
    [ -z "$WF" ] && continue
    TOTAL=$((TOTAL+1))
    REF=$(for w in $WF; do
            gh api "/repos/acme/$r/contents/.github/workflows/$w" --jq '.content' \
            | base64 -d | grep -oE 'shared-workflows/\.github/workflows/[a-z-]+\.yml@\S+'
          done | head -1)
    if [ -n "$REF" ]; then
      SHARED=$((SHARED+1))
      printf '%s\tshared\t%s\n' "$r" "${REF##*@}" >> /tmp/adoption.tsv
    else
      REASON=$(yq -r ".[] | select(.repo==\"$r\") | .reason // \"\"" platform/opt-outs.yaml)
      printf '%s\town\t%s\n' "$r" "${REASON:-UNRECORDED}" >> /tmp/adoption.tsv
    fi
  done

  echo "adoption: $SHARED/$TOTAL ($((100*SHARED/TOTAL))%)"
  echo "--- by version ---"
  awk -F'\t' '$2=="shared" {print $3}' /tmp/adoption.tsv | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn
  echo "--- not using the shared path ---"
  awk -F'\t' '$2=="own" {printf "  %-24s %s\n", $1, $3}' /tmp/adoption.tsv

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • The platform is a product whose users can refuse it, so voluntary adoption is the only success metric that cannot be faked.
  • Two failure modes: the queue (84 tickets, 23-day median, no shared improvements shipped) and the mandate (100% compliance, 41 escape scripts, 17 copied workflows, 9 trivially-passing checks).
  • Compliance is satisfiable without using the thing; ticket throughput measures the width of a bottleneck. Adoption measures whether the platform is worth its cost.
  • Platform owns the building blocks; teams own their pipeline as a dozen lines calling them, plus repo-specific ci/*.sh.
  • The paved road must be the easiest path, not the required one — new service to production in 14 minutes against 3 days, and a fleet-wide change as one PR and a tag move rather than 61 PRs over 11 weeks.
  • If the paved road is not easier, teams are right to refuse it, and adoption tells you before any survey does.
  • The rollout pattern is always the same: measure → pilot → evidence → automate → deprecate → remove.
  • Publish the pilot team's numbers, not the platform team's claims — evidence from a peer persuades where a mandate produces circumvention.
  • Raise migration PRs; do not merge them. Self-merging into 60 repositories destroys ownership and makes the next change just as expensive.
  • Track cohorts rather than one number: standard, near-standard, custom, no-workflow — 12 → 57 of 60 in twelve weeks.
  • The custom cohort is the most valuable: engaging it produced three new shared inputs that every repository benefited from. Forcing it would have produced sixteen escape scripts instead.
  • Migration stragglers are usually unowned repositories — every rollout doubles as an ownership audit, and that is often its most valuable output.
  • Escape hatches are a feedback channel: 10 of 14 recorded opt-outs were platform defects — a short timeout, missing hardware, an unsupported shape, poor discovery, unhelpful errors.
  • Require a recorded reason, an owner and a revisit date; several teams opting out for the same reason is a missing capability, not indiscipline.
  • Order the roadmap by repositories unblocked per engineering day — scaffolding and docs unblocked four repos in two days; the GPU pool unblocked one in two weeks.
  • Run the platform as a service: published SLIs, a support rota rather than a hero, "asked twice becomes a docs page that day" (support load −37%), and a changelog per release.
  • Drive the roadmap from aggregated constraint analysis — "this addresses the measured constraint for 11 of 12 teams" is a defensible plan.
  • Fund the team on delivery capability, with hours as corroboration: lead time 9d → 1.1d, deploys 2.1/wk → 11/wk, security fixes 11 weeks → 1 day.
  • Volunteer the shortfall — a $394k time saving does not cover a $560k team, and presenting it as though it does costs you every future argument.
  • Always leave a way out, and read who takes it. The escape hatch is what keeps a platform a product rather than a policy — and it is how you find out which parts of your change are wrong.
