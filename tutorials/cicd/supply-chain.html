Supply-Chain Security Security & Supply Chain

What you'll master: the four links where a supply chain actually breaks — source, build, artifact, deploy — and the specific control that closes each, so "supply-chain security" stops being a category and becomes six concrete pipeline changes; provenance, which answers the question no scanner can (where did this artifact come from, built from which commit, by which workflow, with what inputs), and the SLSA levels read as capability tiers rather than a compliance ladder; signing and — the part that matters — verification, because signing without an enforced verification step is theatre: one audited estate signed 100% of images and verified 0%; SBOMs generated from the final image rather than the source tree, and the exposure query that turned a three-day scramble into six minutes; hardening the build itself with ephemeral isolated builders, pinned inputs and no network during compilation; and consuming upstream safely, ending at an admission policy that refuses to run an image that is unsigned, unattested, or not built by your pipeline. Prerequisites: Builds & Artifacts, Secrets & Pipeline Identity and Containers in CI.

THE QUESTION EACH CONTROL ANSWERS — AND WHY SCANNERS DO NOT ANSWER THEM

  "does this image contain known-vulnerable packages?"   -> scanner
  "what is inside this image?"                           -> SBOM
  "who built it, from what source, with what inputs?"    -> PROVENANCE
  "has it been modified since it was built?"             -> SIGNATURE
  "may this specific artifact run in production?"        -> ADMISSION POLICY

  Most organisations buy the first and skip the rest — which detects
  yesterday's public CVEs and is silent about an attacker who builds a
  perfectly clean image containing their own code.
🔑 Scanning finds known-bad; provenance and signing establish known-good. They are complementary and the second is the one that stops a targeted attack, because an attacker who compromises a build step produces an artifact with no CVEs at all. If you can only do one thing this quarter, make it "every deployed image is verifiably from our pipeline".

SLSA levelWhat it means in practiceTypical cost
L1The build is scripted, and provenance is generated and availableTwo lines in the workflow
L2Provenance is signed by a hosted build service, so it cannot be forged by the person publishingUse the platform's built-in generator

Treat the levels as a capability ladder: L1 tells you where an artifact claims to be from; L2 makes the claim trustworthy; L3 makes it trustworthy even if the person who wrote the build steps is hostile. Most teams get real value from reaching L2 across the estate and L3 on the handful of artifacts that matter most — and no value from a spreadsheet asserting a level nobody verifies.

Part 3 — Signing Is Half; Verification Is the Other Half

GENERATING A SIGNATURE (keyless — no key material to manage)

  - uses: sigstore/cosign-installer@v3
  - run: |
      cosign sign --yes "registry/app@${DIGEST}"
      # keyless: identity comes from the CI OIDC token, and the
      # certificate + signature are recorded in a transparency log.
      # There is no private key to store, rotate or leak.

  - run: |
      syft "registry/app@${DIGEST}" -o cyclonedx-json > sbom.json
      cosign attest --yes --type cyclonedx --predicate sbom.json \
        "registry/app@${DIGEST}"

VERIFYING IT — AND VERIFYING **WHO** SIGNED IT

  ❌ cosign verify registry/app@sha256:9e1c…            # meaningless:
                                                        # "signed by someone"
  ✅ cosign verify \
       --certificate-identity-regexp \
         '^https://github\.com/acme/shop-api/\.github/workflows/release\.yml@refs/heads/main$' \
       --certificate-oidc-issuer https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com \
       "registry/app@sha256:9e1c…"

  the identity assertion is the whole point: it says the artifact was
  signed by THAT workflow, on THAT branch, in THAT repository.

THE AUDIT THAT SHOWS WHY THIS SECTION EXISTS

  images published in the last 90 days ................. 1,284
  images with a signature .............................. 1,284  (100%)
  images with provenance ...............................   946  (74%)
  ---------------------------------------------------------------
  places where a signature is VERIFIED before use ......     0
  deployments blocked by a failed verification .........     0

  the organisation had "implemented signing". Nothing in the deploy path
  would have noticed an unsigned image, an image signed by a different
  workflow, or an image built on a laptop and pushed by hand.
⚠️ Signing without enforced verification changes nothing about your security posture. It is the most common outcome of a supply-chain initiative, because generating signatures is a two-line change and enforcing verification means blocking deployments — which requires an owner, a rollout plan and a break-glass path. Plan the verification side first; the signing side is trivial once you know what will check it.

Part 4 — SBOMs, and What They Are Actually For

GENERATE FROM THE IMAGE, NOT FROM THE SOURCE TREE

  ❌ syft dir:.                  # lists what the manifests INTEND to install
  ✅ syft "registry/app@${DIGEST}"   # lists what is ACTUALLY in the layers

  the difference in one measured image:
    from the source tree ....... 214 components
    from the image ............. 389 components
    the extra 175: base-image OS packages, a binary vendored by a
    postinstall script, and two packages installed by a build tool —
    including the one that later had the CVE.

STORE IT WITH THE ARTIFACT, KEYED BY DIGEST

  cosign attest --type cyclonedx --predicate sbom.json "registry/app@${DIGEST}"

  so the SBOM travels with the image, cannot be confused between
  versions, and is retrievable years later:
    cosign download attestation "registry/app@${DIGEST}" \
      | jq -r '.payload' | base64 -d | jq '.predicate'

  FROM node:20-slim@sha256:cb1e5f3ac0e1d4… AS deps

  COPY package.json package-lock.json ./

Part 6 — Consuming Safely, and Enforcing at the Door

UPSTREAM VERIFICATION — CHECK WHAT YOU PULL IN

  # binaries: pin a version AND verify a checksum you recorded
  ARG KUBECTL_VERSION=1.31.2
  ARG KUBECTL_SHA256=aa1b2c…
  RUN curl -fsSLo /tmp/kubectl \
        "https://dl.k8s.io/release/v${KUBECTL_VERSION}/bin/linux/amd64/kubectl" \
   && echo "${KUBECTL_SHA256}  /tmp/kubectl" | sha256sum -c - \
   && install -m 0755 /tmp/kubectl /usr/local/bin/kubectl

  # container base images: by digest, updated deliberately by a bot
  # packages: lockfiles + a private mirror (also a reliability win)
  # actions: SHA-pinned, with the version in a comment

ENFORCE AT ADMISSION — THE STEP THAT MAKES ALL OF IT REAL

  # Kubernetes admission policy (Sigstore policy-controller shape)
  apiVersion: policy.sigstore.dev/v1beta1
  kind: ClusterImagePolicy
  metadata: { name: acme-signed-and-attested }
  spec:
    images:
      - glob: "registry.acme.io/**"
    authorities:
      - keyless:
          url: https://fulcio.sigstore.dev
          identities:
            - issuer: https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com
              subjectRegExp: "^https://github\\.com/acme/[^/]+/\\.github/workflows/release\\.yml@refs/heads/main$"
        attestations:
          - name: must-have-sbom
            predicateType: https://cyclonedx.org/bom
          - name: must-have-provenance
            predicateType: https://slsa.dev/provenance/v1

  WHAT THIS REJECTS
    - an image with no signature
    - an image signed by a different repository or workflow
    - an image built on someone's laptop and pushed manually
    - an image from an untrusted registry
    - an image without an SBOM or provenance attestation

$ kubectl apply -f deploy.yaml
  Error from server: admission webhook "policy.sigstore.dev" denied the
  request: validation failed: no matching signatures: none of the
  expected identities matched what was in the certificate
💡 Roll enforcement out in the observe → warn → block order from quality gates. Run the policy in audit mode first and count what would have been rejected — in one estate that was 61 workloads, of which 44 were legacy images nobody was rebuilding and 3 were genuinely unexplained. Blocking on day one would have been an outage; blocking after six weeks of audit and cleanup was uneventful.

❌ A vulnerability scanner in CI, images signed but never verified, SBOMs generated from the source tree, floating base images, curl | sh in the Dockerfile, and a build job that also holds the publishing credentials. The scanner detects yesterday's public CVEs and is completely silent about an attacker who compromises a build step and produces a clean image containing their own code. All 1,284 images carry signatures and nothing in the deploy path verifies one, so an image built on a laptop and pushed by hand deploys exactly like a pipeline-built one. The SBOM lists 214 components from the manifests while the image actually contains 389 — including the vendored binary that later had the CVE. The base image is a floating tag, so the build changes when someone else publishes; a build step pipes a URL into a shell, so it executes whatever that host serves today; and because build and publish are the same job, every transitive dependency's install script runs with the registry credentials in its environment.

✅ Establish known-good rather than only detecting known-bad: provenance, signatures verified by identity, SBOMs from the image, a hardened build, and an admission policy that enforces it all. Generate signed provenance from the platform's builder so a hostile build step cannot forge its own claim, and verify with --certificate-identity-regexp pinned to the repository, workflow and branch — "signed by someone" is not a check. Produce the SBOM from the final digest so it lists what is really in the layers, attach it to the artifact, and keep the exposure query that turns the next CVE announcement into six minutes of work rather than three days. Harden the build itself: ephemeral isolated builders, base images and actions pinned by digest or SHA, lockfile-honouring installs with --ignore-scripts, checksum-verified downloads instead of curl | sh, no network during compilation, and publishing credentials only in the job that does not run third-party code. Then close the loop at the door with an admission policy requiring a signature from your workflow plus provenance and an SBOM — rolled out audit-first, because the first run will find dozens of workloads nobody has rebuilt in a year.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is a software supply chain, and where can it be attacked?

It is everything that contributes to the artifact you run: your source, your dependencies, the build system, the registry, and the deployment path. Four links, each with a characteristic attack:

LinkAttackControl
SourceA malicious dependency or a taken-over maintainer accountLockfiles, pinned actions, review, no secrets in untrusted jobs
BuildA compromised builder, or a build step that fetches code at build timeEphemeral isolated builders, pinned inputs, no network while compiling
ArtifactA moved tag or a poisoned mirror serving different bytesDeploy by digest; signatures; provenance
DeploySomeone runs an image your pipeline never builtAdmission policy verifying signature and provenance

The point worth making unprompted: a vulnerability scanner addresses none of these directly. Scanning answers "does this contain known-vulnerable packages?" — an attacker who compromises a build step produces an artifact with no known vulnerabilities at all. Scanning finds known-bad; provenance and signing establish known-good.

Beginner: What is provenance, and how does it differ from an SBOM?

They answer different questions about the same artifact.

An SBOM is an inventory: what is inside this image — every package and version. It is what you query when a CVE is announced.

Provenance is a record of origin: where this artifact came from — which commit, which repository, which workflow, which builder, which base image, which run. It is signed, so the person publishing the artifact cannot forge it.

Concretely: an SBOM tells you the image contains log4j-core 2.14.1; provenance tells you the image was built by acme/shop-api/.github/workflows/release.yml at commit 4f2a91c on a hosted runner — and therefore not by someone's laptop.

You want both, attached to the digest, and — critically — something in the deploy path that requires them to be present and correct. Generating them and never checking them is the common failure: one audited estate had signatures on 100% of images and verification in exactly zero places.

Mid: Your organisation signs all its images. Is that enough?

No — signing is the easy half. The questions I would ask are:

  1. Where is a signature verified? If the answer is "nowhere", signing has changed nothing: an unsigned image, or one signed by an attacker, deploys exactly the same way.
  2. Does verification assert who signed? cosign verify without an identity assertion only proves "someone signed this". The check must pin the certificate identity to your repository, workflow and branch, and pin the OIDC issuer.
  3. Is the artifact identified by digest all the way to the runtime? Verifying a digest and then deploying a tag leaves a gap where the tag can move.
  4. Is provenance required too? A signature says the bytes are unmodified since signing; provenance says where they came from. An attacker with publish access can sign their own image.
  5. What happens on failure? If verification is advisory, it is a dashboard, not a control.

The enforcement point is usually an admission controller in the cluster, which rejects images that are unsigned, signed by the wrong identity, or missing attestations. Roll it out in audit mode first — the first run typically finds dozens of legacy workloads nobody has rebuilt, and blocking on day one is an outage.

Mid: How do you make a build harder to compromise?

Reduce what the build can be influenced by, and separate it from what it can influence.

ControlAttack it removes
Ephemeral, isolated builder (one job per machine)State and credentials left by another build
Build job holds no publishing credentialsA dependency's install script stealing registry or cloud access
Base images by digest, actions by SHA, lockfiles honouredSomeone else changing what your build consumes
--ignore-scripts on installArbitrary code execution during dependency installation
No network during compilation (--network=none)Code fetched and executed at build time
Checksum-verified downloads instead of curl \| shA URL serving different content today
Provenance generated outside the build stepsA hostile step forging its own provenance

The one I would emphasise is separating build from publish. The build job executes an enormous amount of code nobody on your team wrote; the publish job executes almost none. Merging them means every transitive dependency effectively holds your registry credentials, and it costs one artifact upload to split.

Reproducibility is the natural next step — if two independent builds of the same commit produce identical bytes, a compromised builder becomes detectable by rebuilding elsewhere.

Senior: Design a supply-chain programme for 60 repositories. What is the order?

Order matters more than completeness, because each step makes the next cheaper and the wrong order produces artifacts nobody checks.

PhaseWorkWhy here
1. Identity and inventoryOIDC everywhere, no static keys; know what is deployed by digestWithout digests and identity, nothing later is enforceable
2. GenerateProvenance + SBOM on every build, via the shared reusable workflowOne change reaches 60 repositories (pipelines as code) and nobody can opt out by copying an old pipeline
3. SignKeyless signing tied to the workflow identityCheap once phase 2 exists
4. Verify in audit modeAdmission policy logging what it would rejectReveals the legacy estate without breaking it — 61 workloads in one measured case
5. Clean upRebuild or retire everything the audit flaggedThe actual work; usually a quarter
6. EnforceBlock unsigned/unattested images, with a documented break-glassOnly now is it uneventful
7. Harden the buildNo network at compile time, --ignore-scripts, split build/publish, pinned inputsRaises the guarantee behind the signature

What I would measure, and report monthly: percentage of deployed digests that verify; percentage of builds emitting provenance and SBOM; number of images deployed that your pipeline did not build (target zero); and CVE exposure query time, which is the capability people actually feel.

What I would avoid: starting with an SBOM mandate (it produces documents nobody queries), buying a scanner and calling it done, and enforcing admission before the audit phase — which is how a security programme becomes the team that caused an outage.

Senior: A critical vulnerability is announced in a widely used library. How does your supply-chain work pay off?

The value shows up entirely in how fast and how completely you can answer "are we exposed, and where?"

With SBOMs attached per digest:

  1. Enumerate the digests actually running in each environment — from the clusters, not from a spreadsheet.
  2. Query each digest's SBOM for the package and version. Measured: six minutes, and complete — including transitive and vendored components that lockfile-grepping misses.
  3. Assess reachability: shipping a vulnerable version is not the same as being exploitable; prioritise internet-facing services that call the affected code path.
  4. Patch and roll out through the normal build-once-promote path; confirm by re-querying the new digests.
  5. Prove it: "these digests contained it, these replaced them, here is the query" — rather than "we think we got them all".

Without them: grep 60 repositories' lockfiles, miss anything vendored or bundled by a build tool, and answer with a caveat. Measured on a real event: three days, and still hopeful. The gap is largest exactly where it hurts — the source tree listed 214 components where the image contained 389, and the vulnerable one was in the difference.

The second-order benefit is provenance: when you patch, the admission policy proves that what is now running was built by your pipeline from the patched commit — so "did the fix actually roll out everywhere?" is also a query rather than an investigation.

2 (easy): Generate signed provenance and an SBOM on every build, and verify them by identity.
Solution 
GENERATE — IN THE SHARED REUSABLE WORKFLOW, SO ALL REPOS GET IT

  jobs:
    publish:
      permissions:
        contents: read
        packages: write
        id-token: write            # required for keyless signing
        attestations: write
      steps:
        - uses: docker/build-push-action@v6
          id: push
          with:
            push: true
            tags: registry.acme.io/app:${{ github.sha }}
            provenance: mode=max     # full provenance, generated by the
                                     # builder — not by our build steps
            sbom: true

        - uses: sigstore/cosign-installer@v3
        - name: Sign and attest
          env: { DIGEST: "${{ steps.push.outputs.digest }}" }
          run: |
            set -euo pipefail
            IMG="registry.acme.io/app@${DIGEST}"
            cosign sign --yes "$IMG"
            syft "$IMG" -o cyclonedx-json > sbom.json
            cosign attest --yes --type cyclonedx --predicate sbom.json "$IMG"
            echo "signed and attested $IMG"

VERIFY — AND ASSERT THE IDENTITY, WHICH IS THE WHOLE POINT

  # ci/verify-image.sh DIGEST
  #!/usr/bin/env bash
  set -euo pipefail
  IMG="registry.acme.io/app@${1:?digest}"
  IDENTITY='^https://github\.com/acme/[^/]+/\.github/workflows/release\.yml@refs/heads/main$'
  ISSUER='https://token.actions.githubusercontent.com'

  cosign verify \
    --certificate-identity-regexp "$IDENTITY" \
    --certificate-oidc-issuer "$ISSUER" \
    "$IMG" > /dev/null
  echo "✅ signature valid, signed by the release workflow on main"

  cosign verify-attestation \
    --type cyclonedx \
    --certificate-identity-regexp "$IDENTITY" \
    --certificate-oidc-issuer "$ISSUER" \
    "$IMG" > /dev/null
  echo "✅ SBOM attestation present and valid"

$ ./ci/verify-image.sh sha256:9e1c4a…
  ✅ signature valid, signed by the release workflow on main
  ✅ SBOM attestation present and valid

💡 Keyless signing removes the worst part of signing: key management. There is no private key to store in a secret, rotate, or leak — the identity comes from the CI OIDC token and the certificate is short-lived and logged. It is strictly better than a long-lived signing key sitting in the same secret store this track spent a lesson trying to empty.

STEP 3 — THE QUERY, WHEN A CVE LANDS

  #!/usr/bin/env bash
  # ci/cve-exposure.sh PACKAGE FIXED_VERSION
  set -euo pipefail
  PKG="${1:?package}"; FIXED="${2:?fixed version}"
  ./ci/whats-running.sh --digests-only | sort -u | while read -r digest; do
    SBOM=$(cosign download attestation "$digest" 2>/dev/null \
           | jq -r '.payload' | base64 -d | jq '.predicate')
    echo "$SBOM" | jq -r --arg n "$PKG" \
        '.components[]? | select(.name==$n) | .version' \
    | while read -r v; do
        if [ "$(printf '%s\n%s\n' "$v" "$FIXED" | sort -V | head -1)" = "$v" ] \
           && [ "$v" != "$FIXED" ]; then
          SRC=$(crane config "$digest" | jq -r \
                '.config.Labels["org.opencontainers.image.source"] // "?"')
          REV=$(crane config "$digest" | jq -r \
                '.config.Labels["org.opencontainers.image.revision"][0:8] // "?"')
          echo "AFFECTED  $SRC@$REV  $PKG $v  ($digest)"
        fi
      done
  done

      npm ci --ignore-scripts                          # 3 lockfile, no

  FROM deps AS build
  COPY . .
  RUN --network=none npm run build                     # 4 no network

  FROM gcr.io/distroless/nodejs20-debian12@sha256:41ab77… AS runtime

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Four links, four attacks: source (malicious dependency), build (compromised builder or build-time fetch), artifact (moved tag, poisoned mirror), deploy (an image your pipeline never built).
  • Scanning finds known-bad; provenance and signing establish known-good. An attacker who compromises a build step produces an image with zero CVEs.
  • Provenance answers what no scanner can: which commit, repository, workflow, builder and base image produced this artifact — signed, so the publisher cannot forge it.
  • Read SLSA as capability tiers: L1 provenance exists, L2 it is signed by the build service, L3 the build is isolated and ephemeral so a hostile build step cannot forge it.
  • Signing without enforced verification changes nothing — one audit found 100% of images signed and verification in exactly zero places.
  • cosign verify with no identity assertion only proves "someone signed it". Pin the certificate identity to repository, workflow and branch, and pin the OIDC issuer.
  • Generate SBOMs from the final image, not the source tree: 214 components listed versus 389 actually present, and the vulnerable one was in the difference.
  • Attach the SBOM to the digest as an attestation so it travels with the artifact and cannot be confused between versions.
  • The SBOM's payoff is the exposure query: 6 minutes and complete, against 3 days and hopeful — including vendored copies no lockfile mentions.
  • Check which cataloguers your SBOM tool runs — nested archives need them enabled, and a missing cataloguer yields a confident, incomplete inventory.
  • Harden the build: ephemeral isolated builders, base images by digest, actions by SHA, lockfile-honouring installs, checksum-verified downloads instead of curl | sh, and no network while compiling.
  • --ignore-scripts removes the standard delivery mechanism for compromised packages — install hooks run automatically with the job's full environment.
  • Split build from publish. The build job runs enormous amounts of third-party code; merging it with publishing gives every transitive dependency your registry credentials.
  • Generate provenance outside the build steps, via the platform's builder, or a hostile step can write its own.
  • Enforce at admission: reject images that are unsigned, signed by the wrong identity, or missing provenance and an SBOM — this is what makes every earlier control real.
  • Roll enforcement out audit-first. The first audit found 61 workloads that would have been rejected: 44 legacy, 9 external, 5 from the wrong workflow, and 3 built by hand.
  • The manually-built images are the real finding — they reveal that the incident path bypasses the pipeline and needs a supported route.
  • Handle external images with a separate allowlist policy that only matches digest-pinned references, so third-party tags cannot run.
  • Design break glass before enforcing, with alerting and a TTL, or the policy gets disabled during the first incident and never restored.
  • Report outcomes, not activities: verifiable deployments, images deployed that you did not build, and CVE query time — SBOMs-generated can reach 100% while nothing verifies anything.
