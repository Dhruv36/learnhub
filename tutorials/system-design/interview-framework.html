Interview Framework Senior / Staff

What you'll master: a repeatable structure for a 45-minute system design interview, the time budget that keeps you out of the two classic traps, what the interviewer is actually scoring, how to recover when you don't know something, and a toolbox indexed to every lesson in this track so an unfamiliar problem is still solvable.

This is the closing lesson. It assumes the rest of the track — the framework is simple; the depth comes from what you plug into each phase.

🎯 What's actually being scored. Not whether your design matches the interviewer's. They're assessing: can you handle ambiguity (do you ask before designing?), do you reason quantitatively (does your architecture follow from numbers?), can you make and defend trade-offs, do you go deep on the part that matters, and do you communicate so a colleague could follow it. A "wrong" architecture with excellent reasoning beats the "right" one recited without justification — because they're hiring someone to face problems that have no known answer.

1. The Five Phases and the Time Budget

45 MINUTES

 1. REQUIREMENTS       5 min   — never skip; everything depends on it
 2. ESTIMATION         5 min   — the numbers that force the design
 3. HIGH-LEVEL DESIGN 10 min   — boxes, arrows, and the data model
 4. DEEP DIVE      15-20 min   — where the interview is actually won
 5. WRAP-UP            5 min   — bottlenecks, trade-offs, what's next

THE TWO CLASSIC FAILURES:
   spending 25 minutes on requirements and estimation, then having
   no time for the deep dive where the signal lives;
   or skipping straight to boxes at minute 2, designing against
   requirements you invented, and being unable to justify anything.

Watch the clock. If you're 15 minutes in without a diagram, move on.

2. Phase 1: Requirements (5 min)

The most-skipped and most-costly phase. You cannot make a trade-off without knowing what you're trading for.

ASK ABOUT:

FUNCTIONAL — the 2-3 core use cases, not a feature list
   "Users post photos; followers see them in a feed. Should I include
    comments and DMs, or focus on the feed?"

NON-FUNCTIONAL — the ones that change the architecture
   scale        "How many users? Posts per day?"
   read/write   "What's the read-to-write ratio?"     ← often decisive
   latency      "Is this a user-facing page load, or a background job?"
   consistency  "Can a user briefly see stale data, or must it be exact?"
   availability "What happens if this is down for 5 minutes?"

OUT OF SCOPE — say it explicitly
   "I'll assume authentication exists and won't design it, unless
    you'd like me to."

STATE YOUR ASSUMPTIONS ALOUD so they become agreed premises:
   "I'll assume 100:1 read-to-write — correct me if that's off."

Two questions with unusually high leverage: the read/write ratio (it decides whether you're solving a caching problem or a sharding problem) and what being wrong costs (which determines your consistency model per operation). Ask those two and much of the design follows.

3. Phase 2: Estimation (5 min)

Not arithmetic for its own sake — each number should eliminate an option.

THE FOUR NUMBERS, and the consequence to state after each:

   WRITE QPS   → does it exceed one primary (~5,000/s)?
                 yes → sharding. no → say so explicitly and move on.
   READ QPS    → does it exceed the DB's capacity?
                 yes → caching is load-bearing, not an optimisation.
   STORAGE     → does it fit one machine? does it fit in RAM?
   BANDWIDTH   → for media, egress often dominates the entire bill.

Round hard: 1 day ≈ 10⁵ seconds. Say "roughly 3,000 per second" and
keep talking. A candidate doing long division has stopped
communicating.

THE SIGNAL IS THE CONSEQUENCE, NOT THE NUMBER:
   "6,000 writes/s exceeds a single primary, so I'll shard by user_id"
is worth far more than a correct calculation with no conclusion.

It's equally strong to conclude that nothing is needed. "33 writes/s is trivial — one database handles this, and the interesting problem is entirely on the read path" demonstrates judgement. Proposing shards for 33 writes/s demonstrates the opposite.

4. Phase 3: High-Level Design (10 min)

Draw the request flow, then the data model.

   Client → LB → API → [services] → data stores
                          ↓
                        cache, queue

FOR EACH BOX, be ready to say why it exists. If you can't justify it,
don't draw it — a simpler design you can defend beats a complex one
you can't.

THEN THE DATA MODEL, which candidates rush and interviewers care
about:
   - what are the entities and their access patterns?
   - what's the primary key / partition key, and WHY?
   - which queries must be fast?

START SIMPLE. One database, one service, a cache. Let the deep dive
justify adding complexity — arriving with microservices, Kafka and
a service mesh before anyone asked is the clearest over-engineering
signal available.

5. Phase 4: Deep Dive (15–20 min) — Where It's Won

The interviewer steers here, toward whatever they want to probe. This is where the track's content gets used, so here it is indexed as a toolbox:

If they ask…Reach for
"How do you scale reads?"Caching (hit-rate arithmetic), read replicas
"How do you scale writes?"Sharding, consistent hashing, async processing
"What if a node/region fails?"Failover & fencing, failure detection, RPO/RTO
"How do you stay consistent?"CAP, per-operation consistency, quorums and consensus
"How do you avoid double-charging / overselling?"Atomic conditional writes, idempotency keys
"How do you keep the search index in sync?"CDC, outbox
"One key is much hotter than the rest"Hot keys — replicate, don't shard
"How do you handle a huge fan-out?"Push/pull hybrid, batching, sampling
"How would you know it's working?"SLIs/SLOs, cardinality, alerting on symptoms
"How do you deploy this change safely?"Expand/contract, shadow reads, flags
"What does this cost?"Unit economics, utilisation, TCO

Go deep on one thing rather than shallow on five. If they ask about the database, don't give three sentences and move on — take it through sharding strategy, shard key choice, hot-partition risk, and what breaks at 10×. Depth on one topic is a much stronger signal than breadth across many.

6. Phase 5: Wrap-Up (5 min)

Volunteer, before being asked:

   BOTTLENECKS   "The first thing to break at 10x is the single
                  primary at ~5,000 writes/s. I'd shard by user_id,
                  which costs me cross-shard queries for X."
   TRADE-OFFS    "I chose availability over consistency for the cart
                  because a rejected write costs a sale. For
                  inventory I inverted that."
   WHAT'S MISSING "I haven't covered monitoring, the migration path
                  from an existing system, or abuse prevention —
                  happy to go into any of those."

Naming your own design's weaknesses is a STRONG signal. Refusing to
identify one reads as either dishonesty or a lack of self-review,
and both are worse than a known, understood limitation.

7. Deep Dive: Recovering When You Don't Know

You will be asked something you haven't seen. How you handle it is itself a strong signal — arguably a stronger one than a topic you happen to have memorised.

❌ Bluffing 
"Yes, we'd use a CRDT for
that."

...followed by inability to
say which CRDT, what it
guarantees, or what its merge
bias is.

The interviewer now doubts
everything else you said,
including the parts you did
know. Bluffing is the most
expensive possible error.
✅ Reasoning from principles 
"I haven't used those directly.
Let me reason about it: the
problem is concurrent writes
with no leader, so we need a
merge that's order-independent.

For a set, union works —
commutative and idempotent.
The trade is that deletes can
lose to concurrent adds.

Is that the direction you're
looking for?"

The recovery pattern: say what you don't know, state the underlying problem in terms you do understand, reason toward an answer out loud, and check whether you're on the right track. That demonstrates exactly what the interview is for — how you approach unfamiliar problems, since the job consists mostly of those.

And ask for help when it's efficient: "Is that the part you wanted to explore, or would you rather I go deeper on the storage layer?" Interviewers are usually happy to steer, and a candidate who checks direction wastes less of the 45 minutes.

9. The Meta-Lesson of the Whole Track

Every case study here — TinyURL, the rate limiter, chat, Uber, Amazon, the feed — was built from the same small set of primitives, recombined according to each system's requirements. That recombination is the transferable skill; the twelve architectures are not.

THE PATTERNS THAT RECURRED ACROSS UNRELATED PROBLEMS:

   read-decide-write is a race           → fold the check into the write
   hot keys can't be sharded             → replicate, or don't do the work
   requiring ALL of N multiplies failure → any-of-N, or quorums
   persist before you acknowledge        → an ack is a promise
   estimate first — it inverts answers   → same question, opposite answers
   jitter anything synchronised          → herds are self-inflicted
   push safety to the resource           → actors can't police themselves
   approximate where nobody acts on it   → exactness has a price
   broad-cheap then narrow-precise       → funnels beat brute force
   decide degradation in advance         → config, not panic

A problem you have never seen is solvable with these. That is the
point of the track — not the twelve designs.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — What should you do in the first five minutes?

Ask questions and write down the answers. Nothing else.

Functional scope — establish two or three core use cases rather than a feature list. "Users post photos and followers see them in a feed — should I include comments and messaging, or focus on the feed?" This prevents designing something enormous and shallow.

Non-functional requirements, which are what actually change the architecture:

scale         how many users, how many posts/day?
read/write    what's the ratio?           ← often the decisive one
latency       user-facing, or background?
consistency   can data be briefly stale, or must it be exact?
availability  what breaks if this is down for 5 minutes?

Explicit out-of-scope: "I'll assume authentication exists and won't design it unless you'd like me to." This prevents both sprawl and the impression you forgot about it.

State assumptions aloud so they become agreed premises: "I'll assume 100:1 read-to-write — correct me if that's wrong." If the interviewer disagrees with an unstated assumption later, your whole design is retroactively wrong; if they agreed to it, it isn't.

Why this phase is worth 5 of 45 minutes: every subsequent decision is a trade-off, and you cannot make a trade-off without knowing what you're trading for. Candidates who skip this design against requirements they invented — and then can't justify any choice, because the justification depends on requirements nobody stated.

🟢 Beginner — What is the interviewer actually assessing?

Not whether your architecture matches theirs. There is no answer key. They're assessing how you think, across five dimensions:

1. Handling ambiguity. Do you ask clarifying questions, or design against invented requirements? Real problems arrive underspecified, and this is the closest proxy for that.

2. Quantitative reasoning. Does your architecture follow from numbers? "6,000 writes/s exceeds a single primary, so I'll shard" is engineering. "I'll use Cassandra because it scales" is recitation.

3. Trade-off judgement. Can you name options, their costs, why you chose one, and what would change your mind? This is the core skill the whole track builds toward.

4. Depth. When they steer toward a topic, can you go several levels down, or do you have three sentences and nothing more?

5. Communication. Could a colleague follow your reasoning? Much of the job is explaining technical decisions to people who need to act on them.

The practical implication: a "wrong" architecture with excellent reasoning scores better than the "right" one recited without justification — because they're hiring someone to face problems with no known answer, and reasoning transfers where memorised designs don't.

It also means narrating matters. Thinking silently and announcing a conclusion gives them almost nothing to score.

🟡 Mid — How do you budget 45 minutes, and what happens if you overrun? 
requirements       5 min
estimation         5 min
high-level design 10 min
DEEP DIVE      15-20 min   ← most of the signal is here
wrap-up            5 min

The two classic overruns:

Spending 20 minutes on requirements and estimation. Thorough, and it leaves no time for the deep dive — so the interviewer never sees whether you can go deep on anything, which is what they most need to know. Being exhaustive early is a common way for careful candidates to score poorly.

Skipping to boxes at minute two. Now you're designing against invented requirements and can't justify anything, because justification depends on requirements nobody stated.

Managing it in practice:

• Check the clock at each transition, and move on deliberately: "I could ask more here, but let me move to estimation so we have time for the interesting parts."
• Timebox estimation ruthlessly. Four numbers with their consequences; don't compute anything that won't change a decision.
• Let the interviewer steer the deep dive — they know which part they want to probe, so asking "would you like me to go deeper on storage or on the fan-out?" saves minutes.
• Protect the wrap-up. Reserve the last five minutes to name bottlenecks and trade-offs. It's a disproportionately strong closing signal and it's the first thing people cut.

If you do overrun, say so and prioritise: "I'm conscious of time — the most important remaining piece is how this behaves at 10×, so let me cover that." Managing the clock visibly is itself a positive signal; it's the same judgement you'd apply to a real design review.

🟡 Mid — The interviewer asks about something you've never used. What do you do?

Say so, then reason from principles out loud. This is one of the highest-value moments in the interview, because it shows exactly what they're trying to assess: how you handle unfamiliar problems.

What not to do: bluff. "Yes, we'd use a CRDT" followed by an inability to say which one, what it guarantees, or what its merge bias is, causes the interviewer to doubt everything else you said — including the parts you genuinely knew. Bluffing is the most expensive error available, because it's retroactive.

The recovery pattern:

1. Name the gap plainly.
   "I haven't worked with those directly."
2. Restate the problem in terms you DO understand.
   "The underlying issue is concurrent writes with no leader, so we
    need a merge that doesn't depend on ordering."
3. Reason toward an answer.
   "For a set, union works — it's commutative, associative and
    idempotent. The trade is that a delete racing an add loses, so
    removed items can reappear."
4. Check direction.
   "Is that the direction you were looking for?"

Why this scores well: most of the job is problems with no known answer. Demonstrating that you can decompose an unfamiliar problem into principles you know is more valuable than having memorised a technology — and interviewers deliberately probe past your comfort zone precisely to see this.

It also opens a collaborative exchange. Interviewers frequently fill in the gap and ask you to continue reasoning, which is a much better use of the time than watching you struggle silently.

🔴 Senior — What separates a senior answer from a mid-level one?

Not more technologies. Six specific behaviours:

1. Naming the deciding variable. Mid-level: "I'd use eventual consistency." Senior: "The product requirement is that add-to-cart never fails, which prioritises availability — so eventual consistency with a merge strategy. If this were inventory, I'd invert it."

2. Knowing your own breaking points. Being able to say what number invalidates each choice — a single primary at ~5,000 writes/s, push fan-out in the tens of thousands of followers. That's what lets you re-derive under pushback rather than defend.

3. Volunteering weaknesses. "The weakest part is the hot-partition risk if one tenant dominates — I'd mitigate with a composite key, and I'd monitor per-tenant volume." Refusing to name a weakness reads as either dishonesty or lack of self-review.

4. Recognising when the answer is "don't build it". Use Elasticsearch rather than building search; a modular monolith rather than microservices at eight engineers. Weighing engineering cost against benefit rather than demonstrating you can build things.

5. Renegotiating impossible requirements. Exact global rank for 10M concurrent players, recalling a sent email — both requests for something that can't be built. Finding what the requirement is for and delivering that is frequently the highest-leverage move available.

6. Treating business concerns as in-scope. Cost, degradation policy, oversell tolerance, the RPO/RTO conversation. Recognising which decisions belong to the business — and surfacing them rather than deciding silently — is a large part of what "senior" means.

The unifying thread: mid-level answers describe what; senior answers explain why, name what would change it, and acknowledge what they gave up.

🔴 Senior — You're asked to design something you've never seen. How do you approach it?

Fall back on the patterns rather than searching for a remembered architecture. The whole point of the track is that unfamiliar problems decompose into familiar shapes.

1. Run the framework anyway. Requirements, estimation, high-level design, deep dive. The structure works regardless of domain, and it stops you freezing.

2. Let the estimate tell you what kind of problem it is.

read-heavy with a small working set   → a caching problem
write-heavy beyond one primary        → a partitioning problem
huge fan-out                          → a push/pull decision
large media                           → an egress and CDN problem
strict correctness on shared state    → a concurrency problem

Every problem in this track reduced to one or two of these, and the numbers identify which — which is why estimation comes before design rather than after.

3. Ask which known problem it resembles. A ride-hailing dispatch is a proximity-search problem; a ticketing system is a flash-sale inventory problem; a collaborative editor is a concurrent-write convergence problem. Naming the analogy out loud is useful in itself — and if the analogy is imperfect, explaining why is exactly the reasoning being assessed.

4. Apply the recurring patterns. Is there a read-decide-write race? A hot key? An all-of-N availability chain? An acknowledgement made before persistence? These appear in nearly every system, and spotting them in an unfamiliar one is the transferable skill.

5. Say what you're unsure about. "I don't know this domain well, so I may be wrong about the access patterns — I'm assuming reads dominate. Does that match?" That invites correction early rather than building on a false premise for twenty minutes.

The framing to hold onto: you are not being asked to recall an architecture. You're being asked to derive one from requirements using tools you have — and being visibly systematic about it under uncertainty is a better signal than fluency on a problem you happened to have studied.

🏋️ Graded Exercises

Exercise 1 (Easy): You're asked to "design Twitter". List the first five questions.
Solution 
1. "Which features are in scope? I'd focus on posting and the
    home timeline — should I include DMs, search and trends, or
    leave those out?"
    → prevents a shallow design spread across ten features.

2. "What scale? Daily active users, and posts per day?"
    → every later number derives from this.

3. "What's the read-to-write ratio?"
    → THE decisive question. Heavily read-dominated means this is a
      caching and fan-out problem, not a write-scaling one.

4. "How fresh must the timeline be? Is a few seconds of delay
    acceptable?"
    → determines whether you can precompute, and how aggressively.

5. "Do we need to handle accounts with very large follower counts?"
    → this single requirement decides push vs pull vs hybrid, and
      it's the crux of the whole design.

Question 5 is the highest-value one and the one candidates most often skip. Without celebrities, fan-out-on-write is trivially correct and the problem is easy. With them, pure push is infeasible and you need a hybrid. Asking it early shows you know where the difficulty lives.

Then state assumptions aloud: "I'll assume 300M daily actives, 100:1 read/write, and that a few seconds of timeline staleness is fine — correct me if any of that's off." Now they're agreed premises rather than guesses you can be blamed for.

And explicitly scope out: "I'll assume authentication, media storage and abuse detection exist unless you'd like me to cover them."

Exercise 2 (Easy): Why is stating a number without its consequence a weak answer?
Solution

Because the arithmetic isn't the signal — what the number rules out is.

WEAK:   "We'd have about 6,000 writes per second."
        → correct, and it tells the interviewer nothing about
          whether you know what that implies.

STRONG: "About 6,000 writes per second. That exceeds what a single
         relational primary handles — roughly 5,000 — so replication
         won't help and I'll need to shard, probably by user_id.
         That costs me cross-shard queries for anything spanning
         users."

The strong version does three things: states the number, compares it to a known ceiling, and names the architectural consequence and its cost. That's a complete piece of reasoning the interviewer can probe.

It's equally strong to conclude nothing is needed: "33 writes per second is trivial — one database handles that comfortably, so I won't shard. The interesting problem here is entirely on the read path." Explicitly declining to add complexity, with the number to justify it, is a good signal — over-engineering is the more common failure.

The habit to build: after every estimate, ask "so what?" If the answer is "nothing changes", you computed the wrong number and should move on. Estimation exists to eliminate options, not to demonstrate arithmetic.

Exercise 3 (Medium): The interviewer says "your design won't handle 10× traffic". Respond.
Solution

Treat it as an invitation to re-derive, not a challenge to defend. They're testing whether your reasoning was derived from numbers or retrieved from memory — a derived answer can be recomputed, a memorised one can only be defended.

1. AGREE AND RECOMPUTE.
   "You're right, let me work out what breaks first. At 10x, writes
    go from 6,000/s to 60,000/s."

2. IDENTIFY THE FIRST FAILURE.
   "That's well past a single primary, so sharding becomes mandatory
    rather than optional."

3. NAME THE NEW COST.
   "Sharding by user_id means cross-shard queries for anything
    spanning users, and no cross-shard transactions — so checkout
    would need a saga."

4. STATE THE NEXT BREAKING POINT.
   "That holds until we need cross-shard consistency, or until one
    tenant becomes a hot shard."

What a poor response looks like: "My design already handles that, it scales horizontally." Rigid, usually untrue, and it signals the original answer wasn't reasoned — because a reasoned design knows its own limits.

It's also valid to conclude nothing changes: "At 10× reads this is still a caching problem — I'd add cache nodes and the architecture is unchanged." Said confidently, with the arithmetic, that's a strong answer too. The failure is not having a basis for either conclusion.

The preparation this implies: for every significant choice, know the number that invalidates it. That makes the re-derivation fast and confident rather than improvised under pressure.

Exercise 4 (Medium): With ten minutes left, the interviewer asks you to go deep on one area. How do you choose?
Solution

Ask them. "Is there a particular area you'd like me to explore, or shall I go into the part I think is riskiest?" They usually have something specific in mind, and guessing wastes minutes you don't have.

If they leave it to you, choose the part where the design is genuinely hardest — which is usually one of:

• The bottleneck your estimate identified. If writes exceed a single primary, sharding strategy and shard-key choice is the substantive discussion.
• The correctness-critical path. Anything involving money, inventory or uniqueness — the concurrency discussion is where real difficulty lives and where mistakes are expensive.
• The requirement that breaks the obvious design. Celebrity fan-out, a hot key, a strict latency budget. Whatever made the problem non-trivial in the first place.

What to avoid choosing: the part you find most comfortable, if it isn't the hardest. Spending ten minutes on a straightforward caching layer when the design has an unaddressed hot-partition problem suggests you can't identify risk — which is worse than not knowing a technology.

How to structure ten minutes of depth:

state the problem precisely      (1 min)
options with trade-offs          (3 min)
choose, with justification       (2 min)
failure modes of that choice     (2 min)
what would change the decision   (2 min)

That structure demonstrates every dimension being assessed — depth, trade-off reasoning, awareness of failure, and knowing your own limits — in a single answer.

Exercise 5 (Hard): You realise 25 minutes in that an early assumption was wrong. What now?
Solution

Say so immediately and re-derive. This is uncomfortable and it's a genuinely strong signal — catching your own error and correcting it publicly is what you'd want a colleague to do in a design review.

"I want to revisit something. I assumed a 100:1 read/write ratio,
 but from what you just described about the ingestion path, this is
 actually write-heavy. That changes the design materially — let me
 rework it.

 With writes dominating, the caching layer I emphasised isn't the
 main concern. The bottleneck moves to write throughput, so I'd
 shard earlier and reach for a write-optimised store — an LSM-based
 engine rather than a B-tree, since writes become sequential appends."

Why this scores well rather than badly:

• It shows you're tracking the reasoning, not executing a script.
• It shows you can re-derive, which is the same capability pushback tests.
• It shows intellectual honesty — and an engineer who silently proceeds on an assumption they know is wrong is a much bigger risk than one who says so.

Manage the time: you likely can't redo everything, so state what changes and what doesn't. "The connection layer and the API design are unaffected. What changes is the storage engine and where I'd shard." That's efficient and shows you understand which decisions were coupled to the bad assumption and which weren't.

The alternative is worse. Continuing on a known-wrong assumption means the interviewer either notices — and now doubts your judgement — or doesn't, and you've spent the remaining time building on sand. Neither outcome is recoverable.

And it's worth noting the meta-point: this is exactly why you state assumptions aloud in phase one. If the assumption had been agreed explicitly, the interviewer would likely have corrected it at minute three rather than you discovering it at minute twenty-five.

Exercise 6 (Hard): Design a system you've never seen — a real-time collaborative spreadsheet — using only the patterns from this track.
Solution

Run the framework; the patterns supply the content.

1. Requirements. How many concurrent editors per sheet (10 or 10,000 — completely different problems)? Must formulas recalculate live? Is offline editing required? I'll assume ~50 concurrent editors, live recalculation, and offline support.

2. Estimation. 50 editors × ~2 edits/second = 100 ops/s per sheet, fanning out to 49 peers each — so ~5,000 messages/s per active sheet. That number says the fan-out, not the storage, is the problem.

3. Which known problem is this? It's the collaborative editing shape: concurrent writes to shared state with no lock. The convergence question dominates.

4. Apply the patterns:

CONCURRENT WRITES, NO LEADER
   → CRDT or OT. For a spreadsheet, cells are largely independent,
     so a per-cell last-write-wins register with logical timestamps
     is much simpler than text-editing CRDTs — the structure of the
     data makes the hard part easy.

FAN-OUT
   → route all editors of one sheet to the SAME node (consistent
     hashing on sheet_id), so fan-out is in-process rather than
     through a broker. Same move as the chat/document design.

MESSAGE VOLUME
   → BATCH edits into 50-100ms windows and COALESCE cursor
     positions. Turns 5,000 msg/s into a few hundred.

PERSISTENCE
   → persist-before-broadcast, with a per-sheet sequence number.
     Clients reconnect with ?after=<seq>. The socket is a latency
     optimisation, not the source of truth.

STATE SIZE
   → snapshot + operation log, so opening a sheet doesn't replay
     three years of edits (the event-sourcing snapshot pattern).

FORMULA RECALCULATION
   → a dependency graph; recompute only the affected subgraph.
     This is the one genuinely domain-specific piece, and it's
     worth saying so.

5. Name what's uncertain: "I don't know how spreadsheet formula engines handle circular references or very large dependency graphs — that's the part I'd need to research, and it may dominate the design at scale."

The point of the exercise: almost every component came from a pattern in this track, and the one genuinely novel piece (formula recalculation) was identified and flagged rather than bluffed. That combination — systematic reuse plus honest gap-naming — is what the whole framework exists to produce.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Five phases, budgeted: requirements 5, estimation 5, design 10, deep dive 15–20, wrap-up 5. Protect the deep dive.
  • Never design before clarifying. You cannot make a trade-off without knowing what you're trading for.
  • State assumptions aloud so they become agreed premises rather than guesses you can be blamed for.
  • Every number needs a consequence — the arithmetic isn't the signal, what it eliminates is.
  • Start simple. Arriving with microservices and Kafka before anyone asked is the clearest over-engineering signal.
  • Show the data model — entities, access patterns, and a justified partition key.
  • Go deep on one thing rather than shallow on five; depth is what's being scored.
  • Narrate your reasoning — unspoken thinking cannot be assessed.
  • Treat pushback as an invitation to re-derive, and know the number that invalidates each choice.
  • Never bluff. Name the gap, reason from principles, check direction — that's what the interview is actually for.
  • Volunteer your design's weaknesses and what you'd do next; refusing to name one is a worse signal than the weakness.
  • The patterns transfer; the twelve architectures don't. An unfamiliar problem is solvable with the same small toolbox.
