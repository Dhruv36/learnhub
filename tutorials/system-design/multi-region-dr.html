Multi-Region & Disaster Recovery Senior / Staff

What you'll master: deriving an architecture from stated RPO/RTO rather than choosing the most impressive-sounding option, the cost arithmetic that makes active-active a business decision, why cross-region latency forces a consistency choice, the failure modes of failover itself (which is where most real DR incidents originate), and why an untested DR plan is a liability rather than a safety net.

Prerequisites: Replication & Failover, CAP, Consensus, Estimation (latency numbers). Pairs with Cost & Capacity.

🎯 What this topic is really testing. Whether you treat resilience as a cost decision with stated targets rather than a technical aspiration. "Multi-region active-active" is not the right answer — it's the most expensive answer, and it's correct only when the stated RPO/RTO require it. The mature response derives the cheapest architecture that meets the numbers, and asks what those numbers should be if nobody has set them.

1. RPO and RTO: The Two Numbers That Decide Everything

RPO — Recovery Point Objective: how much DATA may we lose?
   "RPO = 5 minutes" → after a disaster, up to 5 minutes of writes
   may be gone.
   → determined by REPLICATION LAG. Async replication means your RPO
     IS your lag; only synchronous replication gives RPO = 0.

RTO — Recovery Time Objective: how long may we be DOWN?
   "RTO = 1 hour" → service must be restored within an hour.
   → determined by how much must be provisioned, restored, and
     redirected before traffic can flow.

BOTH ARE BUSINESS DECISIONS. An internal reporting tool might accept
RPO = 24h, RTO = 3 days. A payments ledger may require RPO = 0.
The architecture follows from the numbers — not the reverse.

If nobody has stated them, that's the first thing to establish. Asking "what does an hour of downtime cost, and what does losing five minutes of writes cost?" converts an unbounded engineering question into a bounded one — and frequently reveals that the business would accept far more than engineers assumed, or far less.

2. The Strategy Spectrum, With Costs

Backup & restoreNothing — backups in object storageHours / hours~5% — storage only

THE COST ARITHMETIC, made concrete:

   Single region baseline:          $100,000/month
   Warm standby (50% capacity):     $150,000/month  → +$600k/year
   Active-active (2× full):         $220,000/month  → +$1.44M/year
                                     (plus cross-region data transfer)

Now weigh it against the loss avoided:
   revenue $50M/year ≈ $5,700 per hour
   regional outages: assume one 4-hour event every 2 years
      → expected loss ≈ $11,400/year from downtime alone

Naively, $1.44M/year to avoid $11,400/year of downtime is absurd —
BUT that calculation is incomplete. It ignores reputational damage,
SLA penalties, customer churn, and regulatory obligations, which for
some businesses dwarf the direct revenue loss.

The point is not the number. It is that this is a BUSINESS
calculation, and presenting it that way is the senior move.
⚠️ Active-active is not "the best" — it is the most expensive and the most complex. Running two live regions means solving cross-region consistency continuously, not only during a disaster. You inherit conflict resolution, cross-region latency on every coordinated operation, and a permanently harder mental model. Most systems don't need it; choosing it reflexively is over-engineering that also increases the chance of an outage caused by the complexity itself.

3. Deep Dive: Cross-Region Replication Forces a Consistency Choice

The physics, from the estimation lesson:
   same datacenter:   ~0.5 ms round trip
   US ↔ Europe:      ~150 ms round trip   (300x)

SYNCHRONOUS cross-region replication
   ✓ RPO = 0 — no acknowledged write is ever lost
   ✗ EVERY write pays 150 ms. A checkout doing five sequential
     writes now takes 750 ms of pure network time.
   ✗ Availability COUPLES: per the replication lesson, requiring
     both regions makes writes LESS available than one region.

ASYNCHRONOUS cross-region replication
   ✓ local write latency, no coupling
   ✗ RPO = replication lag. A region failure loses whatever hadn't
     shipped — typically seconds, but minutes under load.

CONSENSUS ACROSS REGIONS (Spanner, CockroachDB)
   ✓ strong consistency without a single point of failure
   ✗ a write needs a quorum, which may span regions → 100 ms+
   → viable when you place the quorum thoughtfully (e.g. two replicas
     in the primary region, one remote) so most commits stay local.

The design move that dissolves most of this tension: partition by geography. If each user's data is written only in their home region, cross-region writes essentially disappear — you replicate asynchronously for DR without needing cross-region coordination on the hot path. This is what makes "active-active" tractable for real products: the regions are active for different users, not contending over the same records.

4. Deep Dive: Failover Is Where DR Actually Fails

Most DR incidents are not caused by the disaster — they're caused by the failover mechanism. It's the least-exercised path in the system:

MechanismSpeedFailure mode
DNS failoverMinutesTTLs are advisory — clients, resolvers and JVMs cache aggressively, some ignoring TTL entirely. A "60-second TTL" can mean 30 minutes of stragglers.
Anycast / global LBSecondsFaster and network-layer, but the control plane itself becomes a dependency — and it may live in the failing region.
Client-side failoverImmediateClients try region A, fall back to B. Fastest, but requires shipping logic to clients you can't easily update.
WHAT ELSE BREAKS DURING FAILOVER:

1. THE SECOND REGION HAS NEVER TAKEN FULL LOAD.
   Warm standby runs at 30% capacity. On failover it receives 100%
   and falls over — you've replaced one outage with two.
   → the reason to run periodic full-traffic drills.

2. COLD CACHES. The DR region's caches are empty, so the database
   takes 100% of read traffic at the exact moment it's already
   under stress — the avalanche from the caching lesson.

3. AUTOSCALING CAN'T KEEP UP. Provisioning takes minutes; the
   traffic arrives in seconds.

4. DEPENDENCIES DIDN'T FAIL OVER. Your app moved; the third-party
   API allow-list, the licence server, or the secrets store still
   points at the old region.

5. IN-FLIGHT REQUESTS ARE LOST. Clients must retry against the new
   region — which is safe ONLY if operations are idempotent.

6. SPLIT BRAIN. If the "failed" region is actually alive but
   partitioned, and it keeps accepting writes, two regions diverge
   with no correct merge.

Point 6 is the one with permanent consequences. A regional partition is exactly the situation where you cannot tell "the region is down" from "we can't reach the region" — the failure-detection limit again. If both regions accept writes to the same records, you get divergence that no automated merge can resolve. The defences are the familiar ones: a quorum-based decision to promote (not one observer's opinion), fencing so the old primary's writes are rejected, and — pragmatically — a human in the loop for a decision this consequential.

5. Deep Dive: Data Residency Changes the Problem

GDPR and similar regimes may require that personal data stays within
a jurisdiction. That CONSTRAINS the architecture — and often
SIMPLIFIES it:

   ✗ you cannot replicate EU personal data to a US region for DR
   ✓ you replicate EU data to a SECOND EU region

The constraint forces geographic partitioning, which — as noted
above — removes cross-region write conflicts entirely. What looks
like a compliance burden hands you the property that makes
multi-region tractable.

WHAT CAN BE GLOBAL:
   routing metadata (user → home region), reference data, feature
   flags, aggregated/anonymised analytics
WHAT MUST STAY REGIONAL:
   personal data, message content, documents, activity records

The keystone is a small global routing directory mapping users to home regions. It's tiny, rarely changes, contains no personal data, and lets any edge accept a request and forward it appropriately. Treat a stale entry as "look up authoritatively" rather than "guess", and version it.

6. Testing: An Untested DR Plan Is a Liability

A DR plan that has never been executed is a DOCUMENT, not a
capability. Every real failover reveals something the plan missed.

TESTING LADDER:
   1. Tabletop  — walk through the runbook. Finds missing steps and
                  unclear ownership. Costs an hour.
   2. Component — fail over the database alone, in a maintenance
                  window.
   3. Full drill — shift ALL production traffic to the DR region and
                  run there for a period. The only test that proves
                  capacity, cold caches and dependencies.
   4. Unannounced — the team doesn't know in advance. Tests the
                  humans and the alerting, not just the systems.

Netflix's Chaos Monkey exists because scheduled tests only prove the
system works under conditions you anticipated.

The strongest form is running production in the DR region regularly — some organisations alternate monthly. That guarantees the standby is genuinely capable, because it is production part of the time, and it eliminates the "never taken full load" failure entirely. It also forces the two regions to stay configuration-identical, which is where drift silently accumulates otherwise.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — What are RPO and RTO, and why do they come first?

RPO (Recovery Point Objective) is how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. "RPO = 5 minutes" means a disaster may cost you the last five minutes of writes. It's determined almost entirely by replication lag — with asynchronous replication, your RPO is your lag, and only synchronous replication gives RPO = 0.

RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is how long you can be down. "RTO = 1 hour" means service must be restored within an hour. It's determined by how much has to be provisioned, restored and redirected before traffic flows again.

Why they come first: without them there's no criterion for choosing an architecture, so it gets chosen by aspiration ("active-active sounds robust") or by cost anxiety ("backups are fine"). With them, the choice is mechanical — pick the cheapest strategy that meets both numbers.

backup & restore  → RTO hours,   RPO hours    ~5% extra cost
pilot light       → RTO tens of min, RPO minutes  ~25%
warm standby      → RTO minutes, RPO seconds  ~50%
active-active     → RTO seconds, RPO ~0       200%+

And they are business decisions, not engineering ones. The right question is "what does an hour of downtime cost us, and what does losing five minutes of writes cost us?" An internal reporting tool might genuinely accept RPO of a day; a payments ledger may require zero. Presenting the options with their costs and letting the business choose is the correct move — deciding unilaterally means an engineer has made a financial judgement they weren't asked to make.

🟢 Beginner — Why isn't active-active always the right answer?

Because it's the most expensive and the most complex option, and complexity itself causes outages.

The cost: full duplicate capacity means 200%+ of single-region infrastructure spend, plus continuous cross-region data transfer charges. On a $100k/month baseline that's over $1.4M a year.

The complexity, which is the bigger issue: running two live regions means solving cross-region consistency all the time, not just during a disaster. If both regions can write the same records you need conflict resolution, and per CAP there's no universally correct merge for concurrent writes. You've taken on a permanent distributed-systems problem to solve an occasional availability one.

And the complexity can reduce reliability. More moving parts, more failure modes, and a harder mental model for everyone operating it — so the probability of a self-inflicted outage rises. It's entirely possible to be less available with active-active than with a well-tested warm standby.

When it genuinely is right: when RTO is measured in seconds and RPO near zero (payments, trading); when you need low latency for users on multiple continents anyway, so the second region is earning its cost serving traffic rather than idling; or when regulation requires in-region processing.

The tractable form is geographic partitioning — each user's data is written only in their home region, so the regions are "active" for different users and never contend over the same records. That gets you the availability and latency benefits without the conflict-resolution problem, and it's how real products do it.

🟡 Mid — Your RPO is 5 minutes and RTO is 30 minutes. Which strategy, and why not the others?

Warm standby — and the reasoning is elimination against the numbers.

Backup & restore is ruled out by both. Restoring a large database from object storage takes hours, blowing the 30-minute RTO; and backups taken every few hours give an RPO of hours, not minutes.

Pilot light is marginal. The database is already replicated, so RPO of minutes is achievable — but RTO depends on provisioning and warming an entire compute tier from nothing. Under disaster conditions, with everyone else in the region doing the same thing, 30 minutes is optimistic.

Warm standby meets both comfortably. A reduced-scale but complete stack is already running, so failover means redirecting traffic and scaling up — minutes, not tens of minutes. Async replication with a few seconds of lag comfortably satisfies a 5-minute RPO.

Active-active is over-engineering here. It would deliver RTO in seconds and RPO near zero — far better than required — at 200%+ cost and permanent consistency complexity. Paying four times as much to exceed a requirement is not a good outcome.

What I'd verify before committing:

• Measured replication lag under peak load, not average — lag spikes exactly when you're busiest, and the RPO must hold then.
• That the standby can actually take full load. A 30% standby receiving 100% of traffic fails, turning one outage into two. This needs a full-traffic drill to prove.
• Cache warming in the plan, or the DR database takes 100% of reads cold.
• Every dependency — secrets, third-party allow-lists, licence servers — actually works from the second region.

🟡 Mid — What actually goes wrong during a real failover?

Most DR incidents are caused by the failover itself, not the original disaster — because failover is the least-exercised path in the entire system. The recurring failures:

1. The standby has never taken full load. A warm standby running at 30% capacity receives 100% and collapses. You've converted one region's outage into a total outage. This is the single most common failure, and only full-traffic drills catch it.

2. Cold caches. The DR region's caches are empty, so the database receives 100% of read traffic — the avalanche scenario — at the precise moment it's already stressed and there's no headroom.

3. DNS doesn't propagate as fast as the TTL suggests. TTLs are advisory: resolvers, JVMs and client libraries cache aggressively, and some ignore TTL entirely. A "60-second" failover can leave stragglers on the old address for half an hour.

4. Dependencies didn't move. The application failed over; the secrets store, licence server, or a partner's IP allow-list still points at the dead region. These are only found by testing the full path.

5. Autoscaling can't react fast enough. Traffic arrives in seconds; provisioning takes minutes. Pre-scale before cutting over.

6. In-flight requests are lost and clients retry against the new region — safe only if operations are idempotent. Without idempotency keys, a failover can double-charge people.

7. Split brain. If the region is partitioned rather than dead and keeps accepting writes, two regions diverge with no correct merge — the most damaging outcome, and the reason promotion needs quorum agreement and fencing rather than one observer's judgement.

The through-line: every item is discovered by testing and invisible in a document. That's why an untested DR plan is a liability — it creates confidence without capability.

🔴 Senior — A region appears down but may be partitioned. Do you fail over?

This is the hardest decision in DR, and it's the failure-detection problem at continental scale: you cannot distinguish "the region is down" from "we cannot reach the region".

IF IT IS TRULY DOWN and you don't fail over → prolonged outage.
IF IT IS PARTITIONED and you DO fail over → two regions accepting
   writes to the same data. Divergence with no correct merge, and
   the damage is PERMANENT rather than merely a longer outage.

Gathering evidence before deciding:

• Check from multiple vantage points — if monitoring in three other regions and an external provider all report it unreachable, that's much stronger than one observer.
• Check whether the region is serving users, not just whether you can reach it. Client-side telemetry arriving from users in that region means it's alive and you are the one partitioned.
• Check the cloud provider's status and whether the control plane itself is affected.

The architectural answer is to make being wrong survivable, since certainty is unavailable:

1. Fencing. Promotion issues a new epoch/token, and storage rejects writes bearing an older one. The old primary's writes are refused even if it believes it's still primary — the same mechanism as split-brain prevention, applied across regions. This converts a catastrophic wrong decision into a recoverable one.

2. Quorum-based promotion. Require agreement from a majority of observers spread across regions, so no single partitioned observer can trigger a failover.

3. A human gate for consequential cutovers. At this blast radius, a 5-minute page-and-confirm is cheap insurance. Automate detection and preparation — stage the new primary, verify replication position — and require approval for the final switch.

4. Degrade instead, if possible. Often the best move is neither: put the system into read-only mode globally. Users can still read; nothing diverges; and you buy time to determine what's actually happening. Changing the question from "fail over or not?" to "how do we reduce the cost of waiting?" is frequently the senior answer.

What I'd push back on: if a wrong failover would be catastrophic, that fragility is itself the problem. Fencing, idempotency and the ability to reconcile after divergence reduce the stakes by orders of magnitude — and investing there beats trying to perfect a detector that can never be perfect.

🔴 Senior — How do you justify DR spend to a business that sees it as insurance?

Translate it into their terms and let them choose — this is a business decision with a technical mechanism, and presenting it as purely technical takes the decision from the people entitled to make it.

1. Quantify the exposure.

Revenue $50M/year ≈ $5,700/hour of downtime.
Historical regional outages: ~one 4-hour event every 2 years.
   → expected direct loss ≈ $11,400/year.

But direct revenue is usually the SMALLEST component:
   - SLA credits owed to enterprise customers
   - churn: customers who leave after a visible outage
   - reputational cost and press coverage
   - regulatory penalties in some sectors
   - the engineering time consumed by the incident and its aftermath

2. Present the options with prices, not a recommendation:

backup & restore  +$5k/yr    RTO hours    RPO hours
pilot light       +$300k/yr  RTO ~30 min  RPO minutes
warm standby      +$600k/yr  RTO ~5 min   RPO seconds
active-active     +$1.44M/yr RTO seconds  RPO ~0

3. Frame it as choosing an acceptable loss, not buying insurance. "Which of these outcomes is acceptable when — not if — we lose a region?" That's a question a business leader is equipped to answer, whereas "should we do multi-region?" is not.

4. Name the non-financial drivers that often decide it regardless of the arithmetic: enterprise customers' procurement requiring documented DR; regulatory obligations; and the fact that a second region frequently serves double duty as latency reduction for users on another continent — which moves it from pure insurance to a product improvement that also provides resilience.

5. Offer the cheap wins first. Tested backups, a documented runbook and a rehearsed restore cost very little and remove the worst outcomes. Full active-active can wait until the numbers justify it. Showing a staged path is more persuasive than a single large request.

What I would not do is argue for the most resilient architecture on principle. Engineering judgement here means finding the cheapest option that meets a target the business has agreed to — and being equally willing to say "backups are sufficient for this system" when they are.

🏋️ Graded Exercises

Exercise 1 (Easy): Your replication lag averages 30 seconds and spikes to 5 minutes under load. What is your RPO?
Solution

Your RPO is 5 minutes, not 30 seconds — RPO must be stated against the worst case, because disasters don't wait for quiet periods.

With asynchronous replication, RPO = replication lag at the moment
of failure. Anything not yet shipped to the second region is lost.

Average lag 30 s   → the typical loss
Peak lag 5 min     → the WORST-CASE loss, and the number you must
                      quote

Worse: lag spikes correlate with high load, and regional failures
often happen during high load (thermal events, power draw, cascading
capacity problems). You should expect to fail over at the WORST
moment, not the average one.

What to do about it:

• If 5 minutes exceeds the required RPO, you must reduce the lag or change the replication mode. Options: increase replication bandwidth, use parallel apply on the replica, reduce write amplification, or move to semi-synchronous — which gives RPO = 0 at the cost of write latency.
• Alert on lag as a first-class metric, with the threshold set at your RPO. If lag exceeds it, you are currently out of compliance with your own DR target and should know immediately.
• Investigate the spikes. A 10× lag increase usually has a specific cause — single-threaded apply, a long analytics query blocking replay, or a bulk backfill — and fixing it is often cheaper than changing the architecture.

The general lesson: SLOs and DR targets are worst-case statements. Quoting averages produces a number that's comfortably wrong exactly when it matters.

Exercise 2 (Easy): Why is "we have backups" not a disaster recovery plan?
Solution

Because backups address only one part of recovery, and the untested parts are where failures happen.

What backups don't tell you:

1. Can you restore them? An unrestored backup is a hypothesis. Corruption, missing encryption keys, incompatible versions and incomplete snapshots are all discovered during the first real restore — under pressure.

2. How long does restoring take? A 5 TB database restored from object storage can take many hours. If your RTO is one hour, backups alone cannot meet it regardless of how good they are.

3. What about everything that isn't the database? Application deployment, configuration, secrets, DNS, TLS certificates, third-party integrations, message queues mid-flight. A restored database with nothing to serve it is not a recovered system.

4. Where do you restore to? If the region is gone, you need compute, networking and storage provisioned elsewhere — during a regional outage when everyone else is doing the same thing and capacity is contended.

5. Who does it, and how? An undocumented process executed by whoever happens to be on call, at 3am, under pressure, is not a plan.

What makes it a plan: a documented runbook with named owners; regular tested restores (proving both integrity and duration); infrastructure-as-code so the environment can be rebuilt; backups stored in a different region and account (so the disaster or a compromise can't destroy them); and a measured RTO from the drill rather than an estimate.

The framing: backups protect against data loss. Disaster recovery protects against unavailability. They're related but different problems, and conflating them is how organisations discover during an outage that their RTO is a day.

Exercise 3 (Medium): Design DR for a system with RPO=0 and RTO=1 minute. What does that force?
Solution

These are extremely demanding targets that jointly force active-active, and it's worth stating what each one eliminates.

RPO = 0 forbids asynchronous replication entirely. No acknowledged write may be lost, so every write must be durably present in more than one failure domain before acknowledgement. That means synchronous replication or consensus.

RTO = 1 minute forbids anything requiring provisioning. No time to scale a pilot light, no time for DNS propagation. The second region must already be running at full capacity and already receiving traffic — otherwise you're relying on it working correctly the first time it ever takes load.

ARCHITECTURE
   - both regions live, serving production traffic
   - consensus-based replication (Raft/Paxos per shard) with replicas
     spanning regions, so a committed write is in a quorum before ack
   - quorum placement matters: 2 replicas in the primary region +
     1 remote means most commits stay local-latency, and losing the
     primary region still leaves a quorum
   - traffic routed by anycast or a global LB (seconds, not DNS
     minutes)
   - health checks from multiple vantage points; automated withdrawal

The costs to state honestly:

• Write latency rises to include a cross-region round trip for any operation needing the remote replica — often 100 ms+.
• Cost is 200%+ of single-region.
• Complexity is permanent, not disaster-only.

The move that makes it tractable: partition by geography. If each user's data is written only in their home region, you avoid cross-region write contention entirely — the regions are active for different users. RPO = 0 is then achieved with a quorum within a region plus one remote replica, and most writes never pay the cross-region cost.

And I'd challenge the requirement, because these numbers are expensive: does all data need RPO = 0, or only the financial ledger? Often the honest answer is that a small critical subset needs it while the rest tolerates seconds — in which case a hybrid is dramatically cheaper. Questioning the scope of a demanding requirement is frequently better value than meeting it uniformly.

Exercise 4 (Medium): Your DR region has 30% of production capacity. Traffic fails over to it. What happens, and what should you have done?
Solution

It collapses, and you now have two outages instead of one.

t=0    failover: 100% of traffic arrives at a region sized for 30%.
t+seconds
       - application tier saturates; latency climbs past timeouts
       - CACHES ARE COLD → the database receives 100% of reads with
         no cache absorption — an instant avalanche
       - connection pools exhaust; requests queue
t+1min - health checks fail; instances are pulled from rotation,
         concentrating load on the survivors → cascade
       - clients retry, amplifying load further
t+2min - autoscaling begins provisioning, but takes minutes, and
         everyone else in the region is scaling simultaneously
       - the DR region is now effectively down too

What should have been done:

1. Pre-scale before cutting over. If the failover is deliberate (or detection gives you a minute), scale the DR region first, then shift traffic. This alone prevents most of the above.

2. Warm the caches before or during the ramp, so the database isn't taking 100% of reads cold.

3. Ramp traffic gradually — 10%, 25%, 50%, 100% — watching error rates and latency at each step, rather than an instantaneous cutover.

4. Shed load deliberately. Better to serve 40% of users correctly and reject the rest with a clear message than to accept everything and fail everyone. Rate limiting at the edge makes the degradation graceful.

5. Degrade features — recommendations, search facets, personalisation — decided in advance, so the reduced capacity serves the core path.

6. Test with real full-traffic drills, which is the only way to discover this before it matters.

The strategic question this raises: a 30% standby doesn't meet an RTO that assumes full capacity on failover. Either size it closer to 100% (cost), or accept a longer RTO that includes scale-up time and state that honestly. Claiming a 5-minute RTO with a 30% standby is a plan that will fail its own target — and discovering that during an incident is the worst possible time.

Exercise 5 (Hard): Design a failover drill that would actually find problems. What do you test, and how do you limit blast radius?
Solution

A drill that can't fail teaches nothing. The goal is to find problems, which means testing under conditions close enough to real that the failures are real too — while keeping the damage bounded.

The escalation ladder:

1. TABLETOP (1 hour, zero risk)
   Walk the runbook aloud with the team. Finds missing steps,
   unclear ownership, stale contact details. Cheapest possible
   value, and usually finds something.

2. COMPONENT FAILOVER (maintenance window, low risk)
   Fail over the database alone, or one service. Verifies the
   mechanism and measures actual duration.

3. FULL TRAFFIC SHIFT (scheduled, moderate risk)
   Move 100% of production traffic to the DR region and RUN THERE
   for hours or days. This is the only test that proves capacity,
   cold-cache behaviour, and that every dependency works.

4. UNANNOUNCED (highest value)
   The on-call team doesn't know it's a drill. Tests alerting,
   escalation and human response — not just the systems.

What to test that people usually skip:

• Every external dependency — secrets stores, licence servers, partner IP allow-lists, payment providers, email delivery. These are the most common surprise.
• The actual RTO, measured with a stopwatch, not estimated.
• Data integrity after failover — reconcile record counts and checksums, since RPO = 0 is a claim worth verifying.
• Failing back, which is often harder than failing over and rarely rehearsed.
• Whether monitoring works from the DR region — if observability lives in the failed region, you're flying blind.

Limiting blast radius: schedule during low traffic; keep a tested, practised rollback that can be executed in minutes; ramp traffic rather than switching instantly; feature-flag anything risky; and have the whole team present with a clear abort criterion agreed in advance ("if error rate exceeds 2%, we roll back") so the decision isn't made under pressure.

The strongest form: some organisations run production from the alternate region on a rotation — monthly, say. That eliminates the "never taken full load" failure entirely, because the standby is production part of the time, and it stops configuration drift between regions, which is where silent incompatibility accumulates.

Exercise 6 (Hard): GDPR requires EU personal data to stay in the EU. How does that change your multi-region design?
Solution

The constraint forces geographic partitioning — and in doing so removes the hardest technical problem in multi-region design. That inversion is the interesting part of the answer.

What the constraint forbids: you cannot replicate EU personal data to a US region for DR, nor process it there, nor route an EU user's data through a US service. So the usual "replicate everything everywhere" pattern is simply unavailable.

The resulting architecture:

PER-REGION (never leaves the jurisdiction)
   personal data, message content, documents, activity records
   → EU data replicates to a SECOND EU REGION for DR
   → US data replicates to a second US region

GLOBALLY REPLICATED (safe to hold anywhere)
   user_id → home_region  ROUTING DIRECTORY  ← the keystone
   reference data: plans, pricing, feature flags, config
   aggregated / anonymised analytics (with k-anonymity thresholds)

Why this simplifies things: because each user's data is written only in their home region, two regions can never receive concurrent writes to the same record. Cross-region write conflicts — the problem that makes active-active genuinely hard, requiring conflict resolution with no correct merge — cannot arise. You get active-active behaviour globally with single-writer semantics per record.

What the routing directory needs: it's small, rarely changes and contains no personal data, so it can be replicated everywhere. Version it, and treat a miss as "look up authoritatively" rather than "guess" — a stale entry sending a user to the wrong region is the main failure mode.

What still costs you:

• Latency for travelling users — an EU user in Singapore pays ~150 ms per write because their writes must return to the EU. Mitigate with edge caching for reads and optimistic UI; do not "solve" it by replicating their data locally, which would be a compliance breach.
• Duplicated infrastructure per jurisdiction, since each needs its own DR pair.
• Right to erasure must propagate to replicas, backups and derived stores — crypto-shredding (encrypt per user, delete the key) is the practical mechanism, since backups can't be rewritten.

The framing worth stating: a compliance constraint that looked like a burden handed us the partitioning property that makes the distributed-systems problem tractable. Constraints that partition your write set are gifts — and recognising that is more useful than treating regulation as an obstacle to engineer around.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • RPO and RTO come first — they're business decisions, and without them the architecture has no selection criterion.
  • With async replication, your RPO is your replication lag — and you must quote the peak, not the average.
  • Pick the cheapest strategy that meets the numbers. Active-active is 200%+ cost plus permanent complexity, not a default.
  • Cross-region latency (~150 ms) forces a consistency choice — synchronous replication couples availability and slows every write.
  • Partition by geography so each record has one writing region; that removes cross-region conflicts entirely and makes active-active tractable.
  • Failover is where DR fails — untested standbys, cold caches, DNS TTLs, and dependencies that didn't move.
  • A standby that has never taken full load will fail, turning one outage into two.
  • You cannot distinguish "region down" from "region unreachable" — use quorum promotion, fencing, and a human gate for consequential cutovers.
  • Consider read-only mode instead of failing over when the situation is ambiguous — it reduces the cost of waiting.
  • Data residency constrains and simplifies — it forces the partitioning that removes conflict resolution.
  • An untested DR plan is a liability, because it creates confidence without capability. Drill, escalating to unannounced full failovers.
  • Present DR spend as a business choice with priced options, not an engineering recommendation.
