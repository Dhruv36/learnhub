Design Amazon / E-Commerce Case Study

What you'll master: the read/write asymmetry that makes browsing and checkout completely different systems, why inventory is a genuine distributed-systems race rather than a business rule, the reservation model that makes checkout recoverable, surviving a flash sale where one database row becomes a serialisation point, and building a payment path where a network timeout can't double-charge anyone.

Prerequisites: Concurrency Control (the overselling race), Microservices (sagas), Caching, CAP (per-operation consistency). Pairs with Search Engine.

🎯 What this problem is really testing. Whether you notice that one product contains two systems with opposite requirements. Browsing is ~99% of traffic, tolerates seconds of staleness, and must never go down — a caching and CDN problem. Checkout is a fraction of a percent of traffic, involves money and inventory, and must be exactly right — a consistency and transaction problem. Candidates who design one architecture for both either make product pages needlessly slow, or oversell inventory.

2. Estimation: The Asymmetry Is the Whole Story

Assume 100M DAU, 1% conversion.

BROWSE / SEARCH
   100M users × 20 page views = 2B views/day
   2 × 10⁹ / 10⁵ s = 20,000 req/s   (Black Friday ×10 ≈ 200,000/s)
   → CONSEQUENCE: this is a caching and CDN problem. Product data
     changes rarely and is identical for everyone — near-perfect
     cacheability.

CHECKOUT
   100M × 1% = 1M orders/day
   1 × 10⁶ / 10⁵ s = 10 orders/s   (peak ×20 for a sale ≈ 200/s)
   → CONSEQUENCE: 200 writes/s is TINY. A single well-provisioned
     database handles it. The difficulty is not throughput —
     it is CORRECTNESS.

THE RATIO
   20,000 browse : 10 checkout  =  2,000 : 1
   → Optimise browse for scale and availability.
   → Optimise checkout for correctness. They are different systems.

CATALOGUE
   500M products × 5 KB = 2.5 TB of metadata (cacheable, shardable)
   images: 500M × 5 × 200 KB = 500 TB → object storage + CDN, never
   in a database.

The 2,000:1 ratio is the number to lead with. It explains why the answer isn't "shard the database" — checkout doesn't need it — and why almost all the engineering effort belongs on the read path.

4. The Core Problem: Not Overselling

❌ Check then decrement 
SELECT stock WHERE id=5;   -- 1
-- both customers read 1
UPDATE products
   SET stock = stock - 1
 WHERE id=5;
-- both succeed → stock = -1

Two customers bought the last
item. Somebody's order cannot
be fulfilled.

No isolation level below
SERIALIZABLE prevents this.
✅ Atomic conditional decrement 
UPDATE products
   SET stock = stock - 1
 WHERE id = 5
   AND stock >= 1;

0 rows → out of stock.

The check is PART of the write,
so the database evaluates the
predicate against the current
committed value. No window
exists between check and act.

This is the same shape as the rate limiter's check-then-decrement and the driver claim in Uber: read, decide, write is always a race unless the decision is folded into the write. Recognising the pattern across problems is worth more than memorising any single fix.

5. Deep Dive: Reserve, Don't Decrement

A raw decrement at checkout is still wrong, because checkout isn't instantaneous — payment can take seconds and can fail:

RESERVATION MODEL:

  reservations(id, sku, qty, order_id, state, expires_at)

  1. CHECKOUT START → create a reservation, decrement available stock
        UPDATE inventory
           SET available = available - $qty, reserved = reserved + $qty
         WHERE sku = $sku AND available >= $qty;
        INSERT INTO reservations(..., expires_at = now() + 15 min);

  2. PAYMENT SUCCEEDS → confirm: reserved -= qty (the units are sold)
  3. PAYMENT FAILS    → release: available += qty, reserved -= qty
  4. NEITHER HAPPENS  → the reservation EXPIRES and a sweeper releases it

Step 4 is the one that matters. If the process crashes between reserve
and confirm, an un-expiring reservation strands stock forever —
"phantom inventory" that exists but can never be sold. The TTL is what
makes the system self-healing rather than requiring perfect cleanup.

Reserve at checkout, not at add-to-cart. If adding to a cart reserved stock, a handful of users could hold the entire catalogue hostage, and abandoned carts (the large majority) would strand inventory for hours. The trade is that a user can have an item in their cart and find it unavailable at checkout — universally accepted behaviour, and far better than the alternative.

Also re-validate price at checkout. The cart holds a cached price from browsing; the authoritative price must be re-read before charging. Honour the displayed price if it changed (a small goodwill cost) but never charge a price the user never saw.

6. Deep Dive: Flash Sales — One Row as a Bottleneck

100,000 concurrent requests for ONE SKU.

Even with a correct atomic UPDATE, that single row is a serialisation
point: every transaction takes a row lock, so they queue. At ~1 ms per
transaction, throughput is ~1,000/s and 100,000 requests take
100 seconds — during which the database is saturated and every OTHER
product's checkout suffers.

Correctness is fine. Throughput is not.
TechniqueMechanismTrade-off
Redis counter frontDECR flash:sku5 — atomic, ~100K ops/s; only winners touch the databaseThe fast path rejects 99% of requests without a DB round trip. Needs reconciliation with the durable record.
Sharded countersSplit 1,000 units into 10 buckets of 100; each request hits a random bucket10× the throughput. Slight inaccuracy at the boundary — one bucket can empty while others have stock, so try a second bucket before declaring sold out.
Queue the requestsAdmit to a queue, process at a sustainable rate, tell users their positionHonest and calm; adds latency. Used by ticketing sites for exactly this.
Admission controlOnce N requests exceed remaining stock, reject the rest immediatelyProtects the whole system. If 1,000 units remain and 100,000 people are queued, the last 99,000 cannot win — telling them instantly is better than making them wait.
The Redis-front design in full:

  DECR flash:sku5:stock
     result >= 0  → WON a unit → create the order asynchronously
     result <  0  → sold out → reject immediately (and INCR back
                    to avoid drifting far negative)

  Reconciliation: the durable inventory is updated from the winners'
  orders. Redis holds the allocation; the database holds the truth.

Failure mode to state: if Redis dies mid-sale, the allocation state is
lost. Mitigate with Redis persistence plus a durable record of each
winner written before confirming to the user — you can then rebuild
the count from the orders.

7. Deep Dive: Checkout as a Saga

Checkout spans three services with no shared transaction:

  1. Inventory: reserve      → compensate: release
  2. Payment:   charge       → compensate: refund
  3. Order:     create       → compensate: cancel

ORDER THE STEPS SO THE EXPENSIVE COMPENSATION COMES LAST:
   reserve inventory FIRST (cheap to release, instant)
   charge payment LAST (refunds take days, cost fees, generate
   support tickets)
→ the common failure (out of stock) never touches the customer's card.

Every step must be idempotent, because every step will be retried. The payment step is the one that matters most:

The dangerous scenario: charge the card, then the network drops before
the response arrives. Did it succeed? The service cannot know.

  - retry blindly     → possible double charge
  - don't retry       → possible lost order, customer charged nothing
                        or charged with no order created

FIX: an idempotency key per checkout attempt, passed to the payment
provider. A retry with the same key returns the ORIGINAL charge rather
than creating a second one. The provider is the only participant that
can resolve this, which is why the safety check must live there —
the same push-safety-to-the-resource principle as fencing tokens.

Plus: write the intent locally BEFORE calling the provider, so a crash
leaves a reconcilable record rather than a mystery.

Use an orchestrated saga, not choreography. When a customer contacts support asking why their order is stuck, "let me query the saga state" beats correlating logs across three services. Model the order as an explicit state machine (pending → reserved → paid → confirmed, with cancelling/refunded branches) persisted durably, so a crash mid-checkout leaves a resumable state rather than an ambiguous one.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — How do you prevent overselling the last item?

Make the stock check part of the write, so there's no window between deciding and acting:

❌ SELECT stock WHERE id=5;          -- both customers read "1"
   UPDATE products SET stock = stock - 1 WHERE id=5;
   → both succeed, stock = -1, oversold

✅ UPDATE products SET stock = stock - 1
    WHERE id = 5 AND stock >= 1;
   → 0 rows affected means out of stock

The atomic version works because a single UPDATE takes a row lock and evaluates its predicate against the current committed value. The second transaction blocks briefly, re-evaluates stock >= 1 against 0, matches nothing, and safely reports out of stock.

The general pattern to name: read, decide, write is always a race under concurrency unless the decision is folded into the write. It's the same bug as the rate limiter's check-then-decrement and the driver-claim race in the Uber design — recognising the shape is worth more than memorising any one fix.

The refinement I'd add: in a real checkout you shouldn't decrement at all — you should reserve. Payment happens after inventory and can fail, so you need a state that can be safely undone. A reservation with a TTL gives you that, and the TTL means a crashed checkout releases the stock automatically instead of stranding it.

🟢 Beginner — Why should browsing and checkout be designed differently?

Because they differ by roughly 2,000× in volume and have opposite requirements:

Browse:   ~20,000 req/s (200,000 on Black Friday)
Checkout: ~10 orders/s (200 during a sale)

Browsing is read-heavy, the data is identical for every user, and it changes rarely — so it's a near-perfect caching workload. CDN for images, Redis for product data, read replicas behind that. Seconds of staleness are invisible: nobody notices a product description that's 30 seconds old. And it must never go down, because it's the entire revenue funnel.

Checkout is the opposite. The volume is trivial — 200 writes/s is nothing for a single well-provisioned database — but every operation involves money and inventory and must be exactly right. Here you want transactions, strong consistency, idempotency and durable saga state.

The consequence for how you'd answer: almost all the scaling effort belongs on the read path, and almost all the correctness effort on the write path. A candidate who proposes sharding the orders database has misread the problem — checkout doesn't need throughput, it needs to never be wrong. Conversely, applying transactional rigour to product browsing makes it slow and fragile for no benefit.

One thing that spans both: price. It's cached for display and must be re-validated against the source of truth at checkout, because charging a price the user never saw is unacceptable.

🟡 Mid — Design the inventory reservation flow. What happens if the process crashes mid-checkout? 
reservations(id, sku, qty, order_id, state, expires_at)

1. CHECKOUT START (atomic, one transaction):
     UPDATE inventory
        SET available = available - $qty, reserved = reserved + $qty
      WHERE sku = $sku AND available >= $qty;      -- 0 rows → out of stock
     INSERT INTO reservations(..., state='held',
                              expires_at = now() + interval '15 min');

2. PAYMENT SUCCEEDS → state='confirmed', reserved -= qty  (units sold)
3. PAYMENT FAILS    → state='released', available += qty, reserved -= qty
4. CRASH / ABANDON  → the reservation EXPIRES; a sweeper releases it

Step 4 is the answer to the crash question, and it's the important one. If the process dies between reserving and confirming, nothing in the running system knows to release that stock. Without an expiry, those units are phantom inventory — they exist physically but can never be sold, and the leak accumulates silently with every crashed checkout.

The TTL makes the system self-healing: a sweeper releases anything past expires_at, so a crash costs at most 15 minutes of one item's availability. This is the same reasoning as expiring holds in the trip-booking saga and TTL'd driver claims — never rely on cleanup code running.

Why reserve at checkout rather than add-to-cart: most carts are abandoned, so reserving at add-to-cart would strand inventory for hours and let a few users hold the entire catalogue. The accepted trade is that an item in your cart may be unavailable at checkout.

Operational detail worth adding: monitor the total reserved quantity. If it drifts steadily upward, reservations are leaking — a sweeper failure or a bug — and you'd otherwise discover it only when items mysteriously show as out of stock.

🟡 Mid — A flash sale puts 100,000 requests on one SKU. The atomic UPDATE is correct — what still breaks?

Throughput. Correctness is fine; the single row is now a serialisation point.

Every transaction takes a row lock on that SKU, so they queue.
At ~1 ms per transaction → ~1,000/s throughput.
100,000 requests take ~100 seconds, during which the database is
saturated and checkout for EVERY OTHER product degrades.

Adding database capacity doesn't help — it's one row, so it lives on one node and no amount of sharding moves it. This is the hot-key problem again.

Fixes, layered:

1. A Redis counter in front (the main fix). DECR flash:sku5:stock is atomic and handles ~100K ops/s. A negative result means sold out, rejected immediately without touching the database. Only the winners — bounded by the actual stock, say 1,000 — proceed to create orders. The database sees 1,000 writes instead of 100,000.

2. Sharded counters if one Redis key is still hot: split 1,000 units across 10 buckets and send each request to a random one. Try a second bucket before declaring sold out, since one bucket can empty while others have stock.

3. Admission control. Once the number of in-flight requests exceeds remaining stock, reject the rest instantly. If 1,000 units remain and 100,000 people are waiting, 99,000 of them cannot win — telling them immediately is both honest and protective.

4. A queue with position feedback for high-profile sales — what ticketing sites do. Adds latency but keeps the system calm and the user informed.

The failure mode to name: if Redis dies mid-sale the allocation state is lost. Mitigate with persistence and by writing a durable record for each winner before confirming to the user, so the count can be rebuilt from orders.

🔴 Senior — Payment succeeds but order creation fails. Walk through the recovery.

The customer has been charged and has no order — the worst outcome in the system, because it's both a financial and a trust failure. It must be detectable and automatically recoverable, since it will happen.

Why it happens: payment and order creation are separate services with no shared transaction. A crash, timeout or deploy between them leaves the two systems disagreeing.

Prevention first — reorder the steps. Reserve inventory, create the order in pending state, then charge. Now the order already exists when payment happens, and the failure mode inverts to "order exists, payment unknown" — far easier to resolve, because you have a record to reconcile against.

The durable-intent pattern:

BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO orders(id, state='pending_payment', ...);
  INSERT INTO payment_intents(order_id, idempotency_key, state='pending');
COMMIT;                    -- durable BEFORE any external call

→ call the payment provider with that idempotency_key

BEGIN;
  UPDATE payment_intents SET state='captured' ...;
  UPDATE orders SET state='confirmed' ...;
COMMIT;

A crash anywhere leaves a pending row naming exactly what was in flight. Nothing is ever unknown — only unfinished.

Recovery: a reconciliation job. Periodically find payment intents stuck in pending beyond a few minutes and ask the provider what happened, using the idempotency key:

• Provider says charged, order not confirmed → complete the order (re-drive forward). This is the right resolution: the customer paid and wants their goods.
• Provider says not charged → release the reservation, cancel the order.
• Provider is ambiguous → escalate to a human. Rare, and better than guessing with someone's money.

Why re-driving forward beats refunding: a refund takes days, costs fees, and gives the customer a bad experience for something that was our fault. Completing the order they paid for is almost always correct.

The general principle: in a distributed transaction you cannot prevent partial failure — you can only ensure every partial state is recorded, detectable and resolvable. The reconciliation job is not an afterthought; it's the mechanism that makes the saga trustworthy.

🔴 Senior — Would you ever accept overselling? Justify either way.

Yes, in specific circumstances — and being able to reason about it as a business trade rather than a technical absolute is the point of the question.

The case for accepting a small oversell rate:

Strict inventory correctness requires serialising on a per-SKU row, which caps throughput and creates hot rows exactly during high-demand events. Relaxing it — optimistic decrement with periodic reconciliation, or allowing a small buffer — removes that bottleneck entirely.

If overselling 0.1% of orders costs:
   an apology email, a refund, occasional expedited restock
and strict correctness costs:
   a checkout bottleneck during every sale, lost conversions from
   timeouts, and much higher engineering complexity
then the relaxed design can be clearly better in expectation.

Many large retailers do exactly this — which is why you occasionally
receive "we're sorry, your item is unavailable" after ordering.

When it's clearly unacceptable: unique or high-value items (a single artwork, a specific seat, a serialised product — there's no "we'll restock"); regulated goods; anything where the customer has made dependent plans (event tickets, travel); and where the reputational cost is disproportionate, such as a limited-edition launch where overselling becomes a public story.

The nuanced design that follows: vary the strictness per SKU class rather than globally.

Commodity items, deep stock  → optimistic; reconcile asynchronously
Low stock (< 10 remaining)   → strict atomic decrement
Unique / serialised items    → strict, with a hard reservation
Flash sale                   → Redis allocation, exact within the
                               allocated pool

And if you accept oversells, build for them properly: detect promptly, notify the customer quickly (not at the shipping deadline), offer a substitute or expedited restock, refund without friction, and measure the rate as an SLI with an alert threshold. An accepted oversell rate is a managed parameter, not a bug you tolerate — and that distinction is what separates a deliberate design from a sloppy one.

Exercise 2 (Easy): Why reserve inventory at checkout rather than at add-to-cart?
Solution

Because most carts are abandoned — typically 60–80% — so reserving at add-to-cart would strand the large majority of reserved stock.

Reserve at add-to-cart:
   - stock is held for hours by users who never buy
   - a handful of users could hold the entire catalogue
   - you'd need short cart TTLs, so items silently vanish from carts
   - inventory appears unavailable while sitting physically in a
     warehouse — lost revenue on a large scale

Reserve at checkout:
   - the hold lasts minutes, not hours
   - it's held only by users demonstrating intent to buy
   - abandoned carts cost nothing

The accepted trade: a user can add an item to their cart and find it unavailable at checkout. That's universally understood behaviour — every e-commerce site works this way — and it's far better than the alternative of unsellable inventory.

What good products do about the trade: show a live "only 2 left" indicator on the cart page (from the cached count, which is fine for display), and re-check availability when the cart is opened so the user learns early rather than at payment. The goal is to make the disappointment happen sooner, not to eliminate it by holding stock.

Note the same reasoning applies to the reservation TTL itself: 15 minutes is enough to complete a payment but short enough that a crashed checkout doesn't strand stock for long.

Exercise 3 (Medium): Implement the flash-sale allocation with Redis. Handle the sold-out case and Redis failure.
Solution 
-- Atomic allocation. KEYS[1]=stock key, ARGV[1]=qty, ARGV[2]=order_id
local remaining = tonumber(redis.call('GET', KEYS[1]) or '0')
if remaining < tonumber(ARGV[1]) then
  return {0, remaining}                      -- sold out; no mutation
end
local left = redis.call('DECRBY', KEYS[1], ARGV[1])
-- Record the winner durably-ish so the count can be rebuilt.
redis.call('SADD', KEYS[1] .. ':winners', ARGV[2])
return {1, left}
def allocate(redis, sku, qty, order_id):
    won, left = redis.eval(ALLOC_SCRIPT, 1, f"flash:{sku}:stock",
                           qty, order_id)
    if not won:
        return SoldOut()                    # rejected WITHOUT touching the DB

    try:
        # Durable record BEFORE confirming to the user.
        db.execute("INSERT INTO orders(id, sku, qty, state) "
                   "VALUES (%s,%s,%s,'pending_payment')",
                   (order_id, sku, qty))
    except Exception:
        # Give the unit back — otherwise it is lost for the whole sale.
        redis.incrby(f"flash:{sku}:stock", qty)
        redis.srem(f"flash:{sku}:winners", order_id)
        raise
    return Allocated(remaining=left)


def demo():
    # The invariant: total allocations never exceed initial stock.
    stock = 1000
    allocated = 0
    for _ in range(100_000):                 # simulate the stampede
        if stock <= 0:
            continue                          # sold out, rejected fast
        stock -= 1
        allocated += 1
    assert allocated == 1000, allocated
    assert stock == 0
    print("ok — allocation bounded by initial stock")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    demo()

Why the check and decrement are in one Lua script: Redis executes scripts atomically, so there's no window where two requests both see the last unit. A GET followed by a DECR from the application would be the same check-then-act race the whole lesson is about.

Why DECRBY guarded by a read rather than checking the result for negative: letting the counter go negative and incrementing back works, but under a 100,000-request stampede the counter can drift far below zero before corrections land, and concurrent reads see nonsense. Guarding inside the atomic script avoids it entirely.

Redis failure handling: persistence (AOF) plus the durable order row means the allocation can be rebuilt — count the orders for that SKU and reset the counter to initial - count. The winners set gives a cross-check. Without a durable record written before confirming to the user, a Redis crash would leave you unable to tell who legitimately won.

The rollback path matters: if the database insert fails after winning a unit, that unit must go back. Otherwise every such failure permanently reduces the sale's inventory.

Exercise 4 (Medium): A user checks out, the payment call times out, and they click "Pay" again. Design so they can't be charged twice.
Solution

A timeout means the outcome is unknown — the charge may have succeeded with the response lost. The client can't tell, so the server must make the retry safe.

1. GENERATE the idempotency key when checkout begins, not per attempt:
      idempotency_key = hash(order_id)      -- stable across retries

2. WRITE THE INTENT before calling the provider:
      BEGIN;
        INSERT INTO payment_intents(order_id, idempotency_key,
                                    amount, state='pending');
      COMMIT;

3. CALL the provider WITH that key:
      stripe.charge(amount, idempotency_key=key)
      → a retry with the same key returns the ORIGINAL charge,
        it does not create a second one.

4. RECORD the outcome:
      UPDATE payment_intents SET state='captured', charge_id=...;
      UPDATE orders SET state='confirmed';

Why the provider must enforce it: the external call cannot be inside your transaction, so your database alone can't prevent a double charge — you might call successfully and crash before recording it. Only the provider knows what it has already done, which is why the idempotency key is passed outward. This is the same "push safety to the resource" principle as fencing tokens in replication.

What the second click actually does: the server finds an existing payment_intents row for that order. If captured, return the existing result — the user sees their confirmed order. If pending, either wait briefly or query the provider by key to discover the true state. Either way, no second charge is attempted.

Also handle it in the UI: disable the button after the first click and show a clear pending state. That prevents most double-submits, but it is not sufficient on its own — a refresh, a flaky network, or an impatient user on a second device all bypass it. Client-side prevention reduces frequency; server-side idempotency provides the guarantee.

And the reconciliation backstop: a job that finds intents stuck in pending and asks the provider what happened, so no charge is ever left in an unknown state.

Exercise 5 (Hard): Design multi-warehouse inventory. A customer in London orders an item stocked in Manchester and Berlin.
Solution

"Available" stops being a single number and becomes a question about location, shipping time and cost — which changes both the data model and the reservation flow.

inventory(sku, warehouse_id, available, reserved)
   -- stock is PER WAREHOUSE, not global

Global availability = SUM(available) across warehouses that can
                      serve this customer.

The allocation decision: for a London customer with stock in Manchester and Berlin, prefer Manchester — faster and cheaper delivery, no customs. So allocation ranks warehouses by delivery time, shipping cost, and stock balancing (don't drain one warehouse while another is overstocked).

The reservation is per warehouse, which keeps the atomic pattern intact:

UPDATE inventory
   SET available = available - $qty, reserved = reserved + $qty
 WHERE sku = $sku AND warehouse_id = $chosen AND available >= $qty;

0 rows → that warehouse just sold out; try the next in the ranked list.

The genuinely hard case — a multi-item order that no single warehouse can fill. Three options, and the choice is a business decision:

• Split the shipment — reserve each item from its best warehouse and ship separately. Higher cost, multiple deliveries, and the order isn't complete until all parts ship.
• Transfer stock between warehouses first — slower, but one delivery.
• Partial fulfilment — ship what's available now, backorder the rest.

Whichever you choose, the reservation must be all-or-nothing across warehouses if you promise the whole order: reserve every line item, and if any fails, release all the successful ones. That's a saga across warehouses, and the TTL is what protects you if it fails halfway.

Consistency consideration: if warehouses are in different regions with their own databases, cross-region reservation adds latency to checkout. Better to keep the inventory ledger centrally (it's small and low-volume) even when the physical goods are distributed — the correctness requirement is on the count, not on where the boxes are.

Display simplification: show a single "in stock" indicator with an estimated delivery date computed from the best-available warehouse. Users shouldn't see warehouse topology; they should see when it arrives.

Exercise 6 (Hard): Black Friday brings 10× traffic. What degrades gracefully, and what must not?
Solution

The value of this question is forcing the degradation decisions before the incident, so they're configuration rather than panic.

Must not degradeWhy
Checkout and paymentDirectly revenue. A failed checkout is a lost sale and a lost customer
Inventory correctnessOverselling during the highest-volume day multiplies the damage
Cart persistenceLosing a cart at peak is both revenue loss and a trust failure
Order confirmationA customer charged with no confirmation generates a support contact — and support is already saturated
Can degradeHow
RecommendationsServe a cached generic "popular items" list, or omit the section entirely
ReviewsServe stale, or show only the rating summary
PersonalisationFall back to non-personalised pages — which are far more cacheable, so this helps under load
Search relevanceSimpler ranking, fewer facets, cached popular queries
Real-time stock counts"In stock" instead of "only 3 left" — cache more aggressively, since the display count doesn't need to be exact
Order history / trackingLonger cache TTLs; slightly stale is fine
Email and notificationsQueue them; a confirmation email arriving 10 minutes late is acceptable

Preparation, in order of value:

• Feature flags for every degradation above, tested in advance. The ability to shed recommendations in one click during an incident is worth more than any amount of extra capacity.
• Pre-scale — Black Friday is a known date, so don't rely on reactive autoscaling that takes minutes to respond.
• Pre-warm caches with the expected hot products, so the sale doesn't begin with a cold cache and a stampede.
• Load-test the checkout path specifically at 10×, since it's the path that must not fail and the one least exercised in normal traffic.
• Queue rather than fail for flash-sale items, with honest position feedback.
• Bulkhead checkout so a recommendation-service failure cannot consume the thread pool that checkout depends on.

The framing: graceful degradation is a product decision made in advance about what users can lose without losing the sale. A site that shows plain product pages and completes every purchase has had a good Black Friday; one that shows beautiful personalised pages and times out at payment has had a catastrophic one.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Browse and checkout are two systems — 2,000:1 in volume, with opposite consistency needs. Scale the reads; make the writes correct.
  • Checkout throughput is trivial (~200 writes/s). Don't shard it; the difficulty is correctness.
  • Fold the stock check into the write — UPDATE ... WHERE stock >= 1. Read-decide-write is always a race.
  • Reserve with a TTL, don't decrement. Payment can fail after inventory, and a crash must not strand stock forever.
  • Reserve at checkout, not add-to-cart — most carts are abandoned.
  • Order saga steps so expensive compensation comes last: reserve inventory before charging, so the common failure never touches the card.
  • Idempotency keys passed to the payment provider — only the provider can resolve "did my charge succeed?" after a timeout.
  • Write durable intent before any external call, so a crash leaves a reconcilable record rather than an unknown.
  • A flash sale makes one row a serialisation point — correctness holds, throughput collapses. Redis counter front, sharded counters, admission control.
  • Never let a cache decide whether a sale is allowed — cache "in stock" for display, hit the source of truth for the decrement.
  • Re-validate price at checkout, and honour what the user was shown.
  • Decide degradation in advance — recommendations, reviews and personalisation can go; checkout, inventory and cart cannot.
