Security (TLS, Encryption-at-Rest) Deep Dive

What you'll master: how to reason from a threat model rather than a checklist, what TLS actually guarantees (and the three things it doesn't), why "we encrypt at rest" is the most over-claimed control in the industry, envelope encryption and key hierarchies, how to store passwords correctly in 2026, why parameterised queries — not WAFs — stop injection, and how to design defence in depth so one compromised component isn't total compromise.

Prerequisites: Authentication, REST & API Contracts. Pairs with Microservices (internal trust) and Observability (audit logging).

🎯 The framing that separates a real answer from a checklist: security controls are meaningless without a stated threat model — who the adversary is, what they can reach, and what you're protecting. "We use AES-256" answers nothing on its own. "Encryption at rest protects against a stolen disk or a leaked backup; it does not protect against a compromised application, because the app decrypts as part of normal operation" is a real answer. Always say which attack a control stops, and — just as importantly — which it doesn't.

1. TLS: What It Guarantees, And What It Doesn't

TLS gives you three properties on data in transit:

PropertyMeaningMechanism
ConfidentialityAn eavesdropper can't read the trafficSymmetric encryption (AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305)
IntegrityTraffic can't be altered undetectedAEAD authentication tag
AuthenticationYou're talking to who you think (server side, at least)X.509 certificate chain to a trusted CA

The handshake, and why it's hybrid:

TLS 1.3 (one round trip):
  1. ClientHello  — supported ciphers + a key-share (ephemeral ECDHE public key)
  2. ServerHello  — chosen cipher + its key-share + certificate + signature
  3. Both sides derive the SAME shared secret from the two key-shares
     (Diffie–Hellman: neither secret ever crosses the wire)
  4. All application data is encrypted with the fast symmetric key

Why hybrid: asymmetric crypto solves "two strangers agree a secret over
a hostile network", but it is orders of magnitude too slow for bulk
data. Symmetric crypto is fast but needs a pre-shared key. TLS uses
asymmetric ONLY to establish the symmetric key, then switches.

FORWARD SECRECY: the key-shares are EPHEMERAL — discarded after the
session. So an attacker who records traffic today and steals the
server's private key next year still cannot decrypt it. This is why
TLS 1.3 removed static RSA key exchange entirely.

The three things TLS does not do — each a common misconception:

1. It doesn't authenticate the client unless you deploy mutual TLS. A valid TLS connection tells the server nothing about who is calling.
2. It doesn't protect data at the endpoints. Once decrypted at your load balancer, the data is plaintext in memory, in logs, and on any onward hop that isn't re-encrypted.
3. It doesn't hide metadata. An observer still sees the destination IP, the packet sizes and timings, and — unless Encrypted Client Hello is in use — the SNI hostname. Traffic analysis remains possible.

Where to terminate TLS is a genuine design decision:

PatternMeaningUse when
Terminate at the edgeLB decrypts; internal traffic is plaintextSimple, and acceptable only if the internal network is genuinely isolated. Increasingly considered inadequate.
Terminate and re-encryptLB decrypts (to route, inspect, add headers), then opens a fresh TLS connection to the backendThe usual right answer. You get L7 routing and WAF inspection while internal hops stay encrypted.
PassthroughLB forwards encrypted bytes; the backend terminatesEnd-to-end confidentiality is paramount and you don't need L7 features. Loses routing by path/header.
mTLS everywhere (service mesh)Every hop authenticated in both directionsZero-trust internal networking — services prove identity to each other, not just encrypt.

3. Deep Dive: Key Hierarchies And Envelope Encryption

Encrypting data is easy; managing keys is the actual problem. Two questions decide the design: how do you rotate a key without re-encrypting everything, and where does the root key live?

ENVELOPE ENCRYPTION — the standard answer:

  Data  ──encrypted with──▶  DEK (Data Encryption Key, per record/file)
  DEK   ──encrypted with──▶  KEK (Key Encryption Key, in a KMS/HSM)
  KEK   ──never leaves the KMS──

Store alongside the ciphertext:  { ciphertext, encrypted_DEK, key_id }

To read:   send encrypted_DEK to the KMS → get the plaintext DEK back
           → decrypt locally → discard the DEK from memory.
To rotate: re-encrypt only the DEKs with a new KEK — the DATA is
           untouched. Rotating a KEK over a petabyte costs kilobytes
           of work instead of re-encrypting the petabyte.

Why not encrypt data with the KEK directly? Every read and write would
call the KMS with the full payload — a throughput bottleneck, a hard
dependency, and a large bill.

Crypto-shredding falls out of this design and is the practical answer to "right to erasure" (GDPR Article 17): encrypt each user's data with a per-user DEK, and to delete that user, destroy the key. Every copy — including backups, replicas and archived snapshots you cannot practically rewrite — becomes permanently unreadable. This is how mature systems satisfy deletion requirements without editing immutable backups.

Key management ruleWhy
Root keys live in a KMS/HSM and never leave itThe KMS performs operations with the key rather than handing it out, so a compromised app server cannot exfiltrate the root key
Authenticate to the KMS with workload identity, not a static secretOtherwise you've just moved the secret problem one level down — a credential to fetch credentials
Version keys and record key_id with the ciphertextRotation must be gradual; you need to decrypt old data with old keys while writing new data with the new one
Separate keys by data class and tenantLimits blast radius and enables per-tenant crypto-shredding
Audit every key useKey access logs are often the only signal that data was read at scale

4. Deep Dive: Password Storage (Get This Exactly Right)

This is a question with a correct answer, and getting it wrong is a serious, common failure.

NEVER: plaintext · encryption (reversible — a key compromise reveals
       every password) · MD5/SHA-1/SHA-256 alone (designed to be FAST,
       so a GPU tries billions of candidates per second)

ALWAYS: a SLOW, SALTED, memory-hard password hash.

  Preferred (2026):   Argon2id
                      memory ≈ 19–64 MiB, iterations ≥ 2, parallelism 1
  Also acceptable:    scrypt · bcrypt (cost ≥ 12) · PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256
                      (≥ 600,000 iterations) where FIPS compliance
                      requires it

Why memory-hard matters: bcrypt and PBKDF2 are slow for a CPU but
parallelise well on GPUs and ASICs. Argon2id deliberately consumes
memory, which is expensive to replicate across thousands of cores —
so it degrades an attacker's hardware advantage rather than just
their clock speed.

The salt is a unique random value per password, stored alongside the hash (it is not secret). It ensures two users with the same password get different hashes, which defeats precomputed rainbow tables and stops an attacker from cracking many accounts with one effort. Modern libraries generate and encode it for you — $argon2id$v=19$m=65536,t=3,p=1$<salt>$<hash> contains the parameters and salt, so verification and future upgrades work without a schema change.

A pepper is an optional secret added to every password, stored outside the database (in a KMS or app config). It means a database dump alone is insufficient to crack anything. The cost is that rotating it requires re-hashing on next login, so treat it as defence in depth rather than a substitute for a strong hash.

Also required, and frequently missed: compare hashes in constant time to avoid timing side channels (libraries do this); rate-limit and lock out login attempts per account and per IP; check candidate passwords against a breached-password list (credential stuffing is the dominant real-world attack, not cracking); and never reveal whether the username or the password was wrong.

5. Deep Dive: Injection — Why Parameterised Queries, Not WAFs

Injection persists because it has one real fix that people substitute weaker ones for.

❌ Escaping / blocklists / WAF 
query = "SELECT * FROM users " +
        "WHERE name = '" +
        escape(name) + "'"

Fails because escaping is
CONTEXT-dependent: rules differ
inside quotes, in a LIKE pattern,
in an identifier, per charset.
Attackers find the context you
forgot.

A WAF blocks known PATTERNS. It's
a useful speed bump, never a
boundary — encodings, comments and
novel payloads get past it. Never
present a WAF as the fix.
✅ Parameterised queries 
cur.execute(
  "SELECT * FROM users "
  "WHERE name = %s", (name,))

The SQL text and the DATA travel
to the database SEPARATELY. The
query is parsed and planned first;
the parameter can never become
syntax.

This isn't "escaping done well" —
it removes the possibility
structurally. There is no payload
that works.

The general principle, which extends well beyond SQL: separate code from data. Command injection → pass an argument array, never a shell string. XSS → contextual output encoding, plus a Content-Security-Policy so injected script can't execute. Template injection → never build templates from user input. LDAP, XPath, NoSQL — the same shape every time.

What parameterisation can't cover: identifiers (table and column names) cannot be parameters. If a sort column comes from user input, validate it against an allowlist of known columns — never interpolate it. This is the residual case where injection still occurs in otherwise careful codebases.

6. Defence In Depth And Least Privilege

Internet
  → CDN / DDoS absorption          volumetric attacks
  → WAF                            known patterns (a speed bump, not a boundary)
  → API gateway                    TLS termination, authN, rate limiting
  → Network segmentation           DB in a private subnet, unreachable from the internet
  → Service authN (mTLS)           services prove identity to each other
  → Least-privilege IAM            each service reaches only what it needs
  → Parameterised queries          injection stopped at the source
  → Field-level encryption         the crown jewels stay ciphertext in the DB
  → Audit logging                  detection, and evidence after the fact

No layer is assumed sufficient. Breaching one must not be total
compromise — that is the entire definition of defence in depth.

Least privilege is the highest-leverage control and the most neglected. Concretely: the application's database user should not own DROP TABLE; the read path should use a read-only account; a service that only publishes to one queue should hold exactly that permission. This is what converts "attacker got into the reporting service" from a breach into an inconvenience.

Secrets management follows the same logic. Never commit credentials; fetch them at runtime from a secrets manager that authenticates the workload by its cloud identity (so there's no static credential needed to obtain credentials); prefer short-lived, automatically rotated secrets. For rotation without downtime, issue the new credential while the old one remains valid, let instances pick it up on their refresh cycle, then revoke the old one — the same overlap-window discipline as API expand/contract.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — What does TLS protect against, and what doesn't it protect against?

It protects data in transit with three properties: confidentiality (an eavesdropper can't read it), integrity (it can't be altered undetected), and server authentication (the certificate proves you reached the real example.com, not an impostor). It's what makes a hostile network — public Wi-Fi, an untrusted ISP — safe to transmit over.

What it doesn't do:

• Authenticate the client. Standard TLS proves the server's identity, not yours. Client identity needs mutual TLS or an application-layer credential.
• Protect data at the endpoints. Once decrypted at your load balancer, it's plaintext in memory, in logs, and on any onward hop that isn't re-encrypted. TLS secures the wire, not the systems at either end.
• Hide metadata. An observer still sees the destination IP, packet sizes and timings, and usually the hostname via SNI — so traffic analysis can reveal a great deal without decrypting anything.
• Make an insecure application secure. TLS delivers a SQL injection payload just as reliably as legitimate traffic.

Worth adding: modern TLS 1.3 provides forward secrecy — session keys are ephemeral, so an attacker who records traffic today and later steals the server's private key still can't decrypt it. That's why TLS 1.3 removed static RSA key exchange.

🟢 Beginner — How should passwords be stored?

With a slow, salted, memory-hard password hash — Argon2id is the current recommendation (roughly 19–64 MiB of memory, ≥2 iterations). bcrypt (cost ≥12), scrypt, or PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA256 with ≥600,000 iterations are acceptable, the last mainly where FIPS compliance demands it.

What not to do, and why:

• Plaintext — a database leak is a total, immediate compromise, made far worse by password reuse across sites.
• Encryption — reversible, so one key compromise exposes every password. You never need to recover a password, only verify one, so hashing is strictly correct.
• MD5, SHA-1 or SHA-256 alone — these are designed to be fast, which is exactly wrong here. A GPU tests billions of candidates per second against a fast hash.

The salt is a unique random value per password, stored with the hash (not secret). It ensures identical passwords produce different hashes, defeating rainbow tables and preventing one cracking effort from breaking many accounts at once.

Why memory-hard specifically: bcrypt and PBKDF2 are slow on a CPU but parallelise well on GPUs and ASICs. Argon2id deliberately consumes memory, which is expensive to replicate across thousands of cores — so it attacks the adversary's hardware advantage, not just their clock speed.

And beyond hashing: rate-limit login attempts per account and per IP, check passwords against known breach lists (credential stuffing is the dominant real attack), and return a single generic error so you don't leak which usernames exist.

🟡 Mid — Your database is encrypted at rest. An attacker exploits SQL injection. What do they get?

Plaintext — all of it. Volume encryption and TDE are transparent to the database engine: the engine decrypts on read because that is precisely their design. A SQL injection payload runs as a legitimate authenticated query, so the attacker receives decrypted results through an entirely normal path. The encryption never engages, because the attack never touches the storage layer.

This is the most over-claimed control in the industry. Encryption at rest protects against storage-level compromise: a stolen drive, an improperly decommissioned disk, a leaked backup file, a cloud provider swapping hardware. It does nothing against application-level compromise.

What actually stops this attack:

1. Parameterised queries — the real fix. SQL text and data travel to the database separately, so a parameter can never become syntax. Not escaping, which is context-dependent and easily wrong; not a WAF, which blocks known patterns rather than the attack class.
2. Least-privilege database user — the app account shouldn't be able to read tables it never needs, and the read path should be read-only. This bounds what a successful injection reaches.
3. Field-level encryption for the crown jewels. If SSNs are encrypted by the application, the database stores ciphertext it cannot decrypt, so injection returns unreadable bytes. This is the layer that would actually have helped here.
4. Detection — query monitoring and alerting on anomalous volume, since assuming prevention always works is how breaches go unnoticed for months.

The general lesson: name the threat each control addresses. Stacking controls that all defend the same narrow threat while leaving another wide open is the most common failure in security design.

🟡 Mid — Explain envelope encryption and why you'd use it.

Envelope encryption uses a two-level key hierarchy: data is encrypted with a per-record/per-file Data Encryption Key (DEK), and that DEK is itself encrypted by a Key Encryption Key (KEK) that lives in a KMS or HSM and never leaves it. You store the ciphertext alongside the wrapped DEK and a key identifier.

write: generate DEK → encrypt data locally → ask KMS to wrap the DEK
       → store { ciphertext, encrypted_DEK, key_id }
read:  send encrypted_DEK to KMS → receive plaintext DEK → decrypt
       locally → discard the DEK from memory

Why not just encrypt with the KMS key directly? Three reasons, and they're decisive: every read and write would send the full payload to the KMS (a throughput bottleneck and a hard dependency on the hot path), it would be expensive at per-request pricing, and KMS APIs cap payload sizes anyway.

The rotation benefit is the big one. To rotate the KEK you re-encrypt only the DEKs — the data itself is never touched. Rotating a key protecting a petabyte costs kilobytes of work. Without this, key rotation means re-encrypting everything, which is why systems that skip envelope encryption tend never to rotate keys at all.

Crypto-shredding is the other major benefit: give each user their own DEK, and deleting that key renders every copy of their data permanently unreadable — including backups and archived snapshots you can't practically rewrite. That's the standard mechanism for satisfying GDPR erasure obligations, and it's hard to achieve any other way.

Operationally: version keys and store key_id with each record so old data stays readable during gradual rotation; authenticate to the KMS with workload identity rather than a static secret; and audit key usage, since KMS access logs are often the only signal that data was read at scale.

🔴 Senior — Design the security architecture for a system handling payment card data.

The dominant design goal is reducing scope. Under PCI DSS, every system that stores, processes or transmits card data falls under audit, so the highest-leverage architectural decision is ensuring almost nothing does.

1. Don't handle card data at all, if possible. Use the payment provider's hosted fields or an iframe/SDK so the PAN goes directly from the customer's browser to the processor and never touches your servers. You receive an opaque token. This removes the majority of your infrastructure from PCI scope and eliminates most of the risk — and recognising it as the first move is the senior answer.

2. If you must store card references, tokenise. Store a provider-issued token, not the PAN. A stolen token is useless outside your merchant account, unlike a card number.

3. If you genuinely must store PANs (rare — usually only processors and large merchants):

- Isolate into a dedicated CARDHOLDER DATA ENVIRONMENT: separate
  VPC/subnet, separate accounts, strict ingress/egress allowlists.
- FIELD-LEVEL encryption with envelope encryption; KEK in an HSM.
  The database stores ciphertext it cannot decrypt.
- Never store the CVV after authorisation — prohibited outright.
- Mask on display and in every log: 411111******1111.
- Separate duties: whoever can access the ciphertext must not be able
  to access the keys. That separation is what makes an insider attack
  require collusion.

4. Defence in depth around it: TLS 1.2+ externally with mTLS between internal services; parameterised queries everywhere; least-privilege IAM per service; no direct human access to production (break-glass only, time-boxed and audited, per the auth lesson); MFA for all administrative access.

5. Detection and audit, which regulators weight heavily: append-only, tamper-evident logs of every access to cardholder data; alerting on anomalous query volume; file integrity monitoring; and quarterly vulnerability scanning with annual penetration testing.

6. Key management lifecycle: documented rotation schedule, split-knowledge/dual-control for key ceremonies, and a tested procedure for emergency key rotation on suspected compromise.

The framing I'd close on: most of the security value here comes from architecture, not cryptography. Choosing to never hold the PAN eliminates whole categories of risk that no amount of encryption strength would address. Compliance frameworks are a floor, not a ceiling — and I'd design around the threat model of "assume the application will eventually be compromised; what does the attacker actually reach?"

🔴 Senior — An attacker has compromised one of your 30 microservices. Assess the blast radius and design to limit it.

The question behind the question: does one compromised component mean total compromise? In many architectures the honest answer is yes, and that's the failure to fix.

Assessing the blast radius — what I'd enumerate:

1. IDENTITY: what credentials does this service hold? If it has a
   broad database user or a wildcard IAM role, the attacker inherits
   all of it immediately.
2. NETWORK: what can it reach? With a flat network and no egress
   policy, it can reach all 29 other services and every datastore.
3. TRUST: do other services trust its assertions? If they accept
   forwarded headers like X-User-Id, the attacker can impersonate
   ANY user platform-wide (see the auth lesson).
4. DATA: what is readable through legitimate paths it already has?
   Encryption at rest does not help here at all.
5. PERSISTENCE: can it modify its own image, write to shared storage,
   or alter CI/CD? That converts a breach into a foothold.

Design changes that bound it:

1. Least privilege, per service. The highest-leverage control. Each service gets its own database user restricted to the tables it needs, read-only where possible, and an IAM role scoped to specific resources. "Attacker compromised the reporting service" should mean they can read reporting data — not issue refunds.

2. Network segmentation with default-deny egress. A service should reach only its declared dependencies. This alone prevents lateral movement to the 28 services it has no business calling, and it's what turns an SSRF from a platform-wide problem into a local one.

3. mTLS plus signed internal tokens. Never trust headers. Services authenticate each other cryptographically, and user identity travels in a gateway-signed, audience-scoped token the compromised service cannot forge.

4. Short-lived credentials. Static secrets are permanent once stolen; 15-minute workload-identity credentials bound the window and force the attacker to maintain access noisily.

5. Field-level encryption for the crown jewels, so even legitimate query paths return ciphertext for the data that matters most.

6. Detection, assuming prevention failed. Anomaly alerting on unusual query volume, unexpected egress destinations, or a service suddenly calling endpoints it never calls. The realistic goal is a short dwell time, not zero breaches — most damage comes from months of undetected access.

The mental test I'd apply to any architecture: pick each component in turn and ask "if this is fully owned, what do we lose?" If the answer is ever "everything", that component has too much trust concentrated in it. Security architecture is fundamentally about ensuring no single compromise is catastrophic — the same blast-radius reasoning as consistent hashing or bulkheads, applied to trust rather than load.

🏋️ Graded Exercises

Exercise 1 (Easy): A team hashes passwords with SHA256(password). List three problems and the fix.
Solution

1. Too fast. SHA-256 is designed for speed — a modern GPU computes billions of hashes per second, so an offline attack against a leaked table cracks common passwords almost immediately. Password hashing needs deliberate slowness.

2. No salt. Identical passwords produce identical hashes, so an attacker sees which users share a password and can use precomputed rainbow tables to reverse common ones instantly. One cracking effort breaks every account using that password.

3. Not memory-hard. Even iterated, SHA-256 parallelises beautifully on GPUs and ASICs, so the attacker's hardware advantage is enormous.

Fix — Argon2id with a per-password salt:

from argon2 import PasswordHasher
ph = PasswordHasher(memory_cost=65536, time_cost=3, parallelism=1)
stored = ph.hash(password)     # salt generated and encoded automatically
ph.verify(stored, candidate)   # constant-time comparison

Migrating without forcing a reset: you can't recover the plaintext, so re-hash opportunistically. Store the algorithm with each record; on successful login (verifying with the old scheme), immediately re-hash with Argon2id and replace. Alternatively, wrap the existing hashes (argon2(sha256(password))) to gain the protection immediately, then migrate to clean hashes at next login. Flag any account not migrated after a long window for a forced reset.

Exercise 2 (Easy): Where would you terminate TLS for a system with a load balancer, API gateway, and 10 backend services? Justify it.
Solution

Terminate at the load balancer / API gateway, then re-encrypt to the backends — the "TLS termination and re-encryption" pattern.

Why terminate at the edge: you need plaintext to do L7 routing (path- and header-based), WAF inspection, request logging, header injection for authenticated claims, and centralised certificate management. Certificate renewal in one place rather than eleven is a genuine operational benefit.

Why re-encrypt rather than sending plaintext internally: the internal network is not trustworthy. A compromised pod, a misconfigured network policy, or an attacker with any foothold can observe unencrypted east-west traffic — which now includes authenticated requests and their responses. Perimeter-only encryption assumes an inside/outside boundary that container networks and cloud VPCs don't really provide.

Better still, at this size: mTLS between services, typically via a service mesh. That adds mutual authentication — each service proves its identity to the other — so encryption and service identity come together, and certificate rotation is automated. With 10 backend services, the mesh overhead is justified.

When passthrough would be right instead: if end-to-end confidentiality is a hard requirement (the LB must never see plaintext, e.g. for regulatory reasons), terminate at the backend and accept losing L7 routing and inspection. That's a deliberate trade, not a default.

Exercise 3 (Medium): Write a secure database query for a search endpoint with user-supplied filters and a user-chosen sort column. Explain what parameterisation can and can't cover.
Solution 
ALLOWED_SORT = {"created_at", "name", "price"}   # allowlist
ALLOWED_DIR  = {"ASC", "DESC"}

def search(conn, name_filter, min_price, sort_by, direction, limit=50):
    # Identifiers CANNOT be parameterised — validate against an allowlist.
    if sort_by not in ALLOWED_SORT:
        raise ValueError("invalid sort column")
    if direction.upper() not in ALLOWED_DIR:
        raise ValueError("invalid sort direction")

    # Values ARE parameterised — never interpolated.
    sql = f"""
        SELECT id, name, price
          FROM products
         WHERE name ILIKE %s
           AND price >= %s
         ORDER BY {sort_by} {direction.upper()}    -- safe: allowlisted
         LIMIT %s
    """
    with conn.cursor() as cur:
        cur.execute(sql, (f"%{name_filter}%", min_price, min(limit, 200)))
        return cur.fetchall()

What parameterisation covers: every value. The SQL text and the data travel to the database separately, so the query is parsed and planned before the parameter is bound. A parameter can never become syntax — there is no payload that works. Note the ILIKE case: the wildcards are added to the value, not the SQL, so the pattern stays data.

What it can't cover: identifiers — table names, column names, sort direction, and structural keywords. These are part of the query's syntax, so the database offers no placeholder for them. This is precisely where injection survives in otherwise careful codebases, because developers reasonably assume "we use parameterised queries" covers everything.

The fix for identifiers is an allowlist, never escaping. Map user input to a known-safe set; reject anything else. Mapping from an external name to an internal column ({"newest": "created_at"}) is even better, since it decouples your schema from the API.

Two other defences present above: a bounded limit (min(limit, 200)) to prevent resource exhaustion via limit=99999999, and running this on a least-privilege read-only account, so even a novel injection can't write.

Exercise 4 (Medium): Design key management for a system encrypting 500M user records, with per-user deletion required within 30 days.
Solution

The deletion requirement drives the whole design — it's what makes per-user keys necessary rather than merely convenient.

Three-level hierarchy:

  Root KEK        in an HSM/KMS, never leaves it, rotated annually
      ↓ wraps
  Per-user DEK    one AES-256 key per user
      ↓ encrypts
  User's records  { ciphertext, wrapped_DEK, key_id, key_version }

Storage: 500M users × ~100 bytes of wrapped key + metadata ≈ 50 GB
         of key material. Store in its own keystore table/service,
         separate from the data, with distinct access controls.

Deletion via crypto-shredding: destroy the user's DEK and every copy of their data becomes permanently unreadable — including replicas, snapshots, and backup archives you cannot practically rewrite. This is the only realistic way to guarantee 30-day deletion across an immutable backup estate; attempting to locate and excise rows from months of backups is neither reliable nor verifiable.

Performance: don't call the KMS per record. Unwrap a user's DEK once per session/request and cache it in memory briefly (seconds to minutes), then discard. With envelope encryption the KMS handles wrap/unwrap of small keys, not bulk data, so it stays off the hot path.

Rotation: record key_version with every record so old data remains readable during gradual rotation. Rotating the root KEK re-wraps 500M DEKs — meaningful work, but kilobytes per user rather than re-encrypting all the data. Rotating an individual user's DEK does require re-encrypting that user's records, so do it only on suspected compromise.

The failure mode to design against: losing a DEK is indistinguishable from deleting it — the data is gone forever. So the keystore needs stronger durability guarantees than the data itself: replicated, backed up, with its own tested restore procedure. And deletion must be deliberate and audited, ideally with a short tombstone period before irreversible destruction, since an accidental key deletion is unrecoverable in a way that an accidental row deletion is not.

Finally, verify the shredding actually works: a test that confirms a deleted user's ciphertext is genuinely undecryptable, run regularly, because "we deleted the key" is a claim you may need to demonstrate to a regulator.

Exercise 5 (Hard): A pentest finds your internal admin API is reachable from any pod in the cluster with no authentication, on the assumption that "it's internal". Assess and remediate.
Solution

Assessment: this is a critical finding, because it means the security of every administrative action depends on nobody ever reaching the pod network. That assumption fails to an SSRF in any service, a compromised dependency, a misconfigured ingress, a malicious insider, or a single vulnerable sidecar. In a Kubernetes cluster with a flat pod network by default, "internal" is a very weak boundary — any workload can reach any other unless a NetworkPolicy says otherwise.

Realistic exploitation path: attacker finds an SSRF in a public-facing service → makes it POST to http://admin-api.internal/users/1/promote → gains admin. No credentials needed at any step. The admin API can't distinguish that request from a legitimate one, because it authenticates nothing.

Remediation, in priority order:

1. Authenticate the admin API immediately (the actual fix). Require a signed token with an admin scope, validated per request — the same mechanism as any other endpoint. "Internal" must never be a substitute for authentication; this is the core zero-trust principle.

2. Add NetworkPolicy default-deny so only explicitly authorised sources can even open a connection to the admin service. Defence in depth beneath the authentication fix, and it also limits reconnaissance.

3. Require mTLS so callers prove service identity cryptographically, not by network position.

4. Separate the admin surface entirely — a distinct service, ideally a distinct cluster or namespace with its own ingress, reachable only via VPN/bastion with MFA. Administrative capability should not sit one HTTP call away from user-facing workloads.

5. Require step-up authentication for dangerous operations — recent MFA for destructive or privilege-granting actions, so a stolen session alone is insufficient.

6. Audit every admin action to an append-only log: who, what, when, from where. Then review the existing logs — you must determine whether this was already exploited, and the absence of logging may mean you cannot answer that, which is itself a finding worth reporting.

Then generalise: audit every service for the same assumption. Where else does the code say "it's internal, so it's safe"? This is rarely an isolated instance — it's a design habit, and the remediation that matters is establishing that every service authenticates its callers regardless of network position.

Exercise 6 (Hard): Design security for a multi-tenant SaaS where tenant A must never access tenant B's data. Name the failure modes.
Solution

Tenant isolation is the entire security model here, and a single missed WHERE tenant_id = ? is a cross-tenant breach — the defining risk of multi-tenant SaaS.

Isolation models, strongest to cheapest:

ModelIsolationCost
Database per tenantStrongest — a query cannot cross a connection boundaryExpensive beyond a few hundred tenants; migrations and connection pooling get hard
Schema per tenantStrong — separate namespaces in one databaseMigration complexity grows with tenant count
Shared tables + tenant_idWeakest — depends entirely on every query being correctCheapest and most scalable; the common choice

If using shared tables (usually the practical choice), defend in layers:

1. Row-Level Security in the database — the highest-value control, because it makes isolation structural rather than dependent on developer discipline:

ALTER TABLE documents ENABLE ROW LEVEL SECURITY;
CREATE POLICY tenant_isolation ON documents
  USING (tenant_id = current_setting('app.tenant_id')::bigint);

-- The app sets app.tenant_id per connection/transaction from the
-- authenticated session. A forgotten WHERE clause now returns
-- NOTHING rather than everything — the failure mode inverts from
-- catastrophic to merely broken.

2. Derive tenant_id from the session, never from the request. If the client supplies it, an attacker changes it. It must come from the validated token, and it should never be a settable parameter on any endpoint.

3. Enforce at the data-access layer too — a repository/ORM base class that injects the tenant scope, so no individual query author can omit it.

4. Per-tenant encryption keys for sensitive fields, giving cryptographic isolation plus per-tenant crypto-shredding on offboarding.

The failure modes to name explicitly:

• The forgotten WHERE clause — the classic. RLS is the mitigation.
• Cross-tenant IDs in URLs — GET /documents/12345 where the handler fetches by primary key without checking ownership (IDOR). Always filter by tenant, never trust that an ID belongs to the caller.
• Cache key collisions — caching under user:profile instead of tenant:42:user:profile serves one tenant's data to another. A recurring real-world breach, and it bypasses every database control.
• Background jobs and admin tooling — batch processes often run with elevated credentials and no tenant context, and they're where RLS gets bypassed "temporarily".
• Search indexes, analytics, exports and logs — derived data stores frequently lack the tenant filtering the primary database has. Elasticsearch indexes are a common leak path.
• Noisy neighbours — not confidentiality, but one tenant exhausting shared capacity is an availability failure; per-tenant rate limits and quotas are needed.

Verification, because this cannot rest on review alone: automated tests that authenticate as tenant A and attempt to read tenant B's resources across every endpoint, run in CI. Cross-tenant access is the one bug class in this system that must be impossible to ship, so it deserves a test suite rather than a code-review convention.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • State the threat model, not a checklist. Every control answers a specific attack — say which, and say what it doesn't cover.
  • TLS protects the wire, not the endpoints. It doesn't authenticate the client, doesn't protect decrypted data, and doesn't hide metadata.
  • Terminate and re-encrypt rather than leaving internal traffic plaintext; mTLS when you need service identity too.
  • "Encrypted at rest" stops stolen disks and leaked backups — not SQL injection. The engine decrypts for any authenticated query, which is exactly what the attacker uses.
  • Field-level encryption is what protects data from the database itself, and from most injection.
  • Envelope encryption (DEK wrapped by KMS-held KEK) keeps the KMS off the hot path and makes rotation cost kilobytes instead of petabytes.
  • Crypto-shredding is how you really satisfy deletion requirements across backups you can't rewrite — but the keystore then needs stronger durability than the data.
  • Argon2id, salted, for passwords. Fast hashes (SHA-256, MD5) are the wrong tool; encryption is reversible and therefore wrong too.
  • Parameterised queries, not escaping or WAFs. Separating code from data removes the attack structurally — but identifiers need an allowlist, and that's where injection survives.
  • Least privilege is the highest-leverage control — it decides whether one compromised service is an inconvenience or a total breach.
  • "It's internal" is not authentication. Flat pod networks mean any workload can reach any other unless you say otherwise.
  • Test every component against "if this is fully owned, what do we lose?" If the answer is ever "everything", trust is too concentrated.
