Design Instagram News Feed Case Study

What you'll master: the fan-out decision in its purest form — why push works for most users and breaks catastrophically for celebrities, the hybrid that every large social platform converges on, why the media pipeline costs far more than the feed itself, and how ranking turns a chronological list into a product (with the consequences that follow).

Prerequisites: Estimation, Caching, Pub/Sub, Recommendation (the ranking funnel). The fan-out trade also appears in Chat and Notifications — this is its clearest instance.

🎯 What this problem is really testing. Whether you can identify that one requirement — a celebrity with 100M followers — invalidates the design that works perfectly for everyone else, and whether you reach for a hybrid rather than picking a side. The second signal, which candidates routinely miss, is that the feed is not the expensive part: media storage and delivery dominate the cost by an order of magnitude, and a design that ignores the image pipeline has optimised the cheap half.

1. Requirements

FunctionalNon-functional
Post photos and videosFeed loads in <200 ms — users scroll instantly
Follow accounts; see their posts in a feedSupport accounts with 100M+ followers
Ranked feed, not purely chronologicalSupport users following thousands of accounts
Likes and commentsMedia delivery at global scale

The question that determines the architecture: "what's the follower distribution?" If everyone had 500 followers, fan-out-on-write would be trivially correct. The existence of a long tail — most users with hundreds, a handful with hundreds of millions — is what makes this a design problem at all.

2. Estimation: The Feed Is Not the Expensive Part

Assume 500M DAU, 100M posts/day, average 500 followers.

FAN-OUT WRITES
   100M posts × 500 followers = 50B feed insertions/day
   5 × 10¹⁰ / 10⁵ s = 500,000 writes/s
   → CONSEQUENCE: substantial but feasible for Redis (each write is a
     small sorted-set insert). This is what makes push viable at all.

FEED READS
   500M users × 10 feed loads = 5B reads/day = 50,000 reads/s
   → CONSEQUENCE: with precomputed feeds, each read is one sorted-set
     range query — trivially fast.

FEED STORAGE
   500M users × 500 cached post IDs × 8 B = 2 TB
   → CONSEQUENCE: fits in a Redis cluster. Feed metadata is CHEAP.

MEDIA — where the money actually goes
   100M posts × 3 MB original = 300 TB/day ingested
   with 4 resized variants (~1.5 MB total) = +150 TB/day
   viewing: 5B feed loads × ~10 images × 200 KB = 10 PB/day EGRESS
   → CONSEQUENCE: ~115 Gbps sustained, and egress is typically the
     largest line on the bill — often 10-40x the cost of everything
     else in this design combined.

THE RATIO TO STATE:
   feed metadata: 2 TB.   media: 300 TB/day and 10 PB/day of egress.
   Optimising the feed while ignoring media is optimising the cheap half.

3. The Core Decision: Fan-Out on Write vs Read

Fan-out on write (push)Fan-out on read (pull)
On postInsert into every follower's feed listNothing
On readOne range query — trivialQuery every followed account, merge, sort
Write costO(followers)O(1)
Read costO(1)O(accounts followed)
Breaks whenAn account has millions of followersA user follows thousands of accounts
Wasted workWriting to feeds of users who never open the appRecomputing the same feed on every load
THE CELEBRITY PROBLEM — why pure push fails:

   One post by an account with 100M followers
      = 100,000,000 feed insertions for ONE action.

   At 50,000 writes/s per Redis node, that is 2,000 node-seconds —
   over half an hour of one node's full capacity for a single post.
   Meanwhile the post is "live" but most followers cannot see it yet,
   so engagement is skewed by propagation order.

   And the waste compounds: only ~10-20% of followers open the app
   that day, so 80-90% of those 100M writes are for feeds nobody reads.
💡 The hybrid is the expected answer, and the reasoning is what matters. Use push for normal accounts (the overwhelming majority, cheap to fan out) and pull for accounts above a follower threshold. At read time, merge the user's precomputed feed with a live query for the few celebrity accounts they follow. It works because the asymmetry is exploitable in both directions: celebrities are few (so the read-side merge is small) but have enormous reach (so avoiding their fan-out saves the most). You get O(1)-ish reads for 99% of content and O(1) writes for the content that would otherwise dominate.
def get_feed(user_id, limit=50):
    # 1. Precomputed portion — pushed by normal accounts
    feed = redis.zrevrange(f"feed:{user_id}", 0, limit)

    # 2. Pull portion — celebrities this user follows (typically < 50)
    celebs = get_followed_celebrities(user_id)          # cached
    recent = []
    for c in celebs:
        recent += redis.zrevrange(f"posts:{c}", 0, limit)

    # 3. Merge, rank, truncate
    return rank(merge(feed, recent))[:limit]

4. Deep Dive: Choosing the Threshold, and the Other Optimisations

The threshold isn't a magic number — it's the point where the fan-out cost exceeds the read-merge cost, and it should be derived and tuned:

Push cost for one post  ≈ followers × cost_per_write
Pull cost              ≈ (readers of this account per day)
                          × cost_per_extra_query

Push is worse when followers ≫ daily readers — i.e. exactly when the
follower list is large and mostly inactive.

Typical thresholds land in the 10,000-100,000 range, and should be
TUNED from measurements rather than assumed. Also make it dynamic:
an account crossing the threshold switches mode, which needs care so
posts near the transition aren't duplicated or lost.

Two further optimisations that matter as much as the hybrid:

Don't fan out to inactive users. If 80% of followers haven't opened the app in 30 days, writing to their feed is pure waste. Fan out only to active followers and generate feeds on demand for returning users. This alone can cut fan-out volume several-fold, and it's cheaper to implement than the celebrity hybrid.

Cap and trim feed lists. A feed only needs the most recent few hundred entries — nobody scrolls to post 5,000. Trimming keeps the 2 TB figure bounded regardless of how long the service runs.

5. Deep Dive: The Media Pipeline (The Expensive Half)

UPLOAD
   client → pre-signed URL → object storage DIRECTLY
   (never through the app tier — 3 MB through your servers wastes
    bandwidth and ties up request handlers)
   → publish MediaUploaded event

PROCESSING (async, never blocking the post)
   → generate variants: thumbnail 150px, small 320, medium 640,
     large 1080, plus WebP/AVIF alternates
   → strip EXIF (privacy: photos carry GPS coordinates)
   → run moderation/classification
   → mark the post as ready

DELIVERY
   → CDN with long TTLs; media is IMMUTABLE, so cache forever
   → serve the smallest variant the client needs
     (a phone feed does not need a 1080px image)
DecisionImpact
Transcode to modern formatsWebP/AVIF at ~30% of JPEG size. On 10 PB/day of egress, that's a multi-million-dollar annual difference — the highest-value optimisation in the entire design.
Serve size-appropriate variantsSending 200 KB thumbnails instead of 2 MB originals in the feed is a 10× egress reduction, and the user cannot tell.
CDN with immutable URLsContent-addressed filenames mean infinite TTLs and no invalidation problem — most traffic never reaches origin.
Async processingA post appears immediately; variants fill in within seconds. Blocking the post on transcoding would make uploads feel broken.
Lifecycle tieringAccess is heavily recency-biased — move originals older than 30 days to archival storage at ~5× lower cost.

6. Deep Dive: Ranking

Modern feeds are ranked by predicted engagement, not recency — which is the same retrieval-then-ranking funnel applied to a much smaller candidate set:

RETRIEVE  the hybrid feed (pushed + pulled) → a few hundred candidates
RANK      score each by predicted engagement:
             recency, relationship strength (do they interact?),
             content type affinity, author quality, diversity
TRUNCATE  return the top ~50

The candidate set is bounded by "accounts this user follows", so
ranking is cheap here compared to recommendation systems that must
retrieve from a 10M-item catalogue.

Ranking creates product consequences worth naming: a chronological feed is predictable and fair; a ranked feed is engaging and opaque. Ranking by engagement introduces the same feedback loops as recommendations — popular content gets shown, gets more engagement, gets shown more — so it needs diversity constraints and exploration, or the feed collapses onto a narrow set of creators and users perceive it as broken. And optimising raw engagement rewards outrage, which is a product decision disguised as a metric choice.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — Explain fan-out on write versus fan-out on read.

Fan-out on write (push): when someone posts, immediately insert that post into every follower's precomputed feed list. Reading a feed is then a single range query — trivially fast.

Fan-out on read (pull): store the post once. When a user opens their feed, query every account they follow, merge the results, and sort. Writes are O(1); reads do all the work.

PushPull
Write costO(followers)O(1)
Read costO(1)O(accounts followed)
Breaks onCelebritiesUsers following thousands

Push is the better default because feeds are read far more often than posts are written — a user might post once a day and check their feed ten times. Doing the work once at write time and serving many cheap reads is the right trade for the common case.

Where push breaks is the celebrity: one post by an account with 100M followers means 100 million feed insertions for a single action. That's the problem the hybrid exists to solve, and it's why "just pick push" isn't a complete answer.

Worth noting the same trade appears in group chat and notifications — and interestingly, chat's answer is the opposite (fan-out on read for message bodies), because a user has tens of conversations rather than thousands of followees.

🟢 Beginner — Why is the media pipeline more expensive than the feed itself?

Because the numbers differ by orders of magnitude:

FEED METADATA
   500M users × 500 cached post IDs × 8 B = 2 TB total
   → fits in a Redis cluster. Cheap.

MEDIA
   ingest:  100M posts × 3 MB = 300 TB/DAY
   egress:  5B feed loads × 10 images × 200 KB = 10 PB/DAY
            ≈ 115 Gbps sustained

Egress is typically the single largest line on the infrastructure bill for a media product — often 10–40× everything else combined. So a design that carefully optimises feed generation while treating images as an afterthought has optimised the cheap half.

The highest-value optimisations are all in the media path:

• Modern formats — WebP or AVIF at roughly 30% of JPEG size. On 10 PB/day that's a multi-million-dollar annual saving for a transcoding job that costs a fraction of it.
• Size-appropriate variants — serving 200 KB thumbnails in the feed rather than 2 MB originals is a 10× reduction the user cannot perceive.
• CDN with immutable URLs — content-addressed filenames mean infinite cache TTLs, so most traffic never reaches origin at all.

The general lesson: estimate before optimising. The instinct is to focus on the algorithmically interesting part (fan-out), but the arithmetic says the bytes are where the money is.

🟡 Mid — Design the hybrid. How do you choose the threshold?

The hybrid: push for normal accounts, pull for celebrities, merged at read time.

def get_feed(user_id, limit=50):
    feed  = redis.zrevrange(f"feed:{user_id}", 0, limit)   # pushed
    celebs = get_followed_celebrities(user_id)             # usually < 50
    recent = [p for c in celebs
                for p in redis.zrevrange(f"posts:{c}", 0, limit)]
    return rank(merge(feed, recent))[:limit]

Why it works: the asymmetry is exploitable in both directions. Celebrities are few, so the read-side merge stays small (a user follows maybe 20 of them). But they have enormous reach, so removing them from fan-out eliminates most of the write cost. You get cheap reads for 99% of content and cheap writes for the content that would otherwise dominate.

Choosing the threshold — derive it rather than guessing:

Push cost per post ≈ followers × cost_per_write
Pull cost          ≈ daily_readers × cost_per_extra_read_query

Push becomes worse when followers ≫ daily active readers — precisely
when the follower list is large and mostly inactive.

In practice thresholds land in the 10,000-100,000 range, and should
be tuned from measured write load rather than assumed.

The transition is the fiddly part. When an account crosses the threshold, posts near the boundary must not be duplicated (pushed and pulled) or lost. Handle it by recording the mode at post time on the post itself, so read-time merging knows whether a given post was already pushed.

The optimisation I'd pair with it, because it's cheaper to build and often saves more: don't fan out to inactive followers at all. If 80% haven't opened the app in 30 days, those writes are pure waste — push to active users and generate feeds on demand for returners.

🟡 Mid — A user follows 5,000 accounts. Which model, and what breaks?

Push handles this well; pull breaks. The two models fail at opposite ends of the distribution, and this user is the pull-model's worst case.

PULL for this user:
   5,000 accounts × 1 query each = 5,000 queries per feed load
   even at 1 ms each and heavy parallelism, that's a large fan-out
   with severe tail-latency exposure (see the search engine lesson:
   with 5,000 parallel queries you hit SOMEONE's p99 essentially
   every time)

PUSH for this user:
   their feed was precomputed as those 5,000 accounts posted
   → one sorted-set range query. ~1 ms.

So the hybrid handles both ends: the pushed portion covers the thousands of normal accounts they follow, and only the handful of celebrities among those 5,000 are pulled. If they follow 20 celebrities, the read is one range query plus 20 small queries — entirely manageable.

What still needs attention for this user:

• Their feed list fills quickly — following 5,000 active accounts means a high insertion rate. Capping at a few hundred entries and trimming keeps it bounded.
• Ranking matters more. With 5,000 followed accounts, a chronological feed is dominated by whoever posts most frequently. Ranking is what makes a high-follow-count feed usable at all.
• Their fan-in is a write hotspot in the sense that many posts converge on one feed key — worth watching, though far less severe than celebrity fan-out.

The general observation: push and pull fail at opposite ends of the follower/followee distribution, which is exactly why neither alone is sufficient and why the hybrid isn't a compromise but the correct answer.

🔴 Senior — A celebrity with 100M followers posts. Trace the cost of pure fan-out and design around it. 
PURE FAN-OUT ON WRITE:
   100,000,000 feed insertions for ONE post.
   At ~50,000 writes/s per Redis node: 2,000 node-seconds
   → over half an hour of one node's FULL capacity, for one post.

Consequences:
   - propagation takes minutes; followers see it at wildly different
     times, which skews engagement by position in the fan-out queue
   - 80-90% of those writes target users who won't open the app today
   - if the account posts several times an hour, fan-out never
     catches up
   - it competes with every other account's fan-out on the same
     infrastructure

The design: hybrid push/pull. Accounts above a tuned follower threshold are not fanned out. Their posts are stored once, and at read time each follower's feed merges the precomputed portion with a live query for the celebrities they follow. The merge is cheap because a user follows few celebrities, even though each has enormous reach.

Additional measures for the largest accounts:

• Cache the celebrity's recent posts aggressively — the same small list is read by millions of followers, so it's a textbook hot key served well from an in-process L1 cache.
• Prioritise fan-out by recency of activity if you do partially push: reach users who opened the app in the last hour first, so the people most likely to see it get it soonest.
• The media is the harder hot spot — one viral post's image is requested by tens of millions of clients. The CDN absorbs this naturally, which is precisely why immutable content-addressed URLs matter.

The recurring pattern to name: this is the hot-key problem again, and the same rule applies as with a viral TinyURL link or a flash-sale SKU — a hot key cannot be solved by distributing it, only by replicating it or by not doing the work at all. Here the answer is the latter: don't fan out, and let readers pull.

🔴 Senior — Chat uses fan-out on read; feeds use fan-out on write. Why the opposite defaults?

Because the ratio between recipients and reads is inverted, and that ratio is what decides the answer in both cases.

Group chatSocial feed
Recipients per writeTens (group members)Hundreds to millions (followers)
Sources per readTens (conversations)Hundreds to thousands (followees)
Read frequencyReads and writes are comparableReads ≫ writes (post once, check ten times)
Message volumeVery high (2B/day)Lower (100M posts/day)

Chat: fan-out on read for message bodies. A user has tens of conversations, so merging at read time is cheap. And with 2B messages/day, storing one copy per conversation rather than N copies per recipient is an enormous storage saving. Chat only fans out the delivery signal — a small pointer — not the content.

Feeds: fan-out on write. Feeds are read far more often than written, so precomputing once and serving many cheap reads is right. And a user may follow thousands of accounts, making read-time merging expensive.

The unifying principle: do the work on the less frequent operation, and avoid duplicating large payloads. Both designs follow it and reach opposite conclusions because their parameters differ.

And both converge on hybrids at the extremes: feeds pull for celebrities; chat pushes delivery signals but pulls bodies, and treats a 100,000-member broadcast channel as a feed rather than a conversation. Recognising that these are the same trade with different inputs — rather than two unrelated patterns to memorise — is the point of the question.

🏋️ Graded Exercises

Exercise 1 (Easy): 100M posts/day, average 500 followers. Compute fan-out write volume and say whether push is viable.
Solution 
100,000,000 posts × 500 followers = 50,000,000,000 insertions/day
5 × 10¹⁰ / 10⁵ s = 500,000 writes/s   (peak ×3 ≈ 1.5M/s)

Each write is a small sorted-set insertion (~8 B payload).
Redis handles ~50,000-100,000 writes/s per node.
   500,000 / 50,000 = ~10 nodes sustained
   peak: ~30 nodes

Verdict: push is viable for the average case. Ten to thirty Redis nodes for feed fan-out is entirely reasonable at this scale, and it buys O(1) feed reads for 500M daily users — an excellent trade given reads outnumber writes ten to one.

But the average hides the problem. The 500-follower figure is a mean over a heavily skewed distribution: most accounts have far fewer, and a handful have millions. A single 100M-follower account posting once generates 100M insertions — 200× the entire per-second average — which is why the average is the wrong number to design against.

The refinement worth proposing immediately: don't fan out to inactive followers. If only 10–20% of followers open the app on a given day, the majority of those 50B daily insertions land in feeds nobody reads. Pushing only to recently-active users cuts the volume several-fold and is cheaper to implement than the celebrity hybrid.

Exercise 2 (Easy): Why cap precomputed feeds at a few hundred entries?
Solution

Because nobody scrolls that far, and uncapped lists grow without bound.

UNCAPPED
   an active user following 1,000 accounts that post daily accumulates
   ~1,000 entries/day → 365,000/year → 3.65M after a decade
   500M users × 3.65M × 8 B = 14.6 PB of feed metadata.

CAPPED at 500
   500M × 500 × 8 B = 2 TB. Bounded forever, regardless of how long
   the service runs.

Why 500 is enough: users scroll a few dozen posts per session. A feed is a recent-content view, not an archive — and the ranking layer only ever considers the top few hundred candidates anyway, so entries beyond that are unreachable even in principle.

What happens if someone scrolls past the cap: fall back to a pull query for older content — generating that portion on demand. It's slower, but it happens rarely, so paying the cost only for the rare case is exactly the right trade.

Implementation: Redis sorted sets make this trivial — ZADD then ZREMRANGEBYRANK feed:{user} 0 -501 keeps the newest 500 and discards the rest. Trim on write so the list never exceeds the cap.

This is the same bounding instinct as capping catch-up queries in chat and aggregating rather than storing raw analytics events: anything that grows per-user-per-day needs an explicit bound, or it becomes an unplanned storage problem years later.

Exercise 3 (Medium): Design the media upload and delivery pipeline. Where does the money go?
Solution 
UPLOAD
   1. client requests a PRE-SIGNED URL from the API
   2. client uploads DIRECTLY to object storage (never through the
      app tier — 3 MB per upload would waste bandwidth and occupy
      request handlers)
   3. API records the post as "processing" and publishes MediaUploaded

PROCESSING (async — the post appears immediately)
   4. generate variants: 150 / 320 / 640 / 1080 px,
      each in JPEG + WebP/AVIF
   5. strip EXIF (photos carry GPS coordinates — a privacy leak)
   6. moderation classification
   7. mark the post ready; variants are content-addressed

DELIVERY
   8. CDN, immutable URLs, effectively infinite TTL
   9. serve the smallest variant the client needs

Where the money goes — egress, overwhelmingly:

ingest:  100M × 3 MB           = 300 TB/day
storage: +150 TB/day of variants, accumulating
egress:  5B feed loads × 10 images × 200 KB = 10 PB/day

At typical CDN rates, egress dominates everything else by an order of
magnitude or more.

So the highest-value optimisations are all on egress:

• WebP/AVIF at ~30% of JPEG size — the single biggest lever, and transcoding compute costs a small fraction of the savings.
• Right-sized variants — a phone feed needs 320px thumbnails, not 1080px originals. A 10× reduction users cannot perceive.
• CDN hit rate — immutable content-addressed URLs mean infinite TTLs and no invalidation, so origin traffic approaches zero.
• Lifecycle tiering — access is heavily recency-biased, so archive originals older than 30 days at ~5× lower storage cost.

Why processing is async: blocking the post on transcoding would make uploads feel broken. Show the post immediately using the original (or a fast client-side thumbnail) and swap in variants as they complete.

Exercise 4 (Medium): An account crosses the celebrity threshold mid-day. Handle the transition without duplicating or losing posts.
Solution

The hazard: the read path merges pushed and pulled content. If a post was pushed and the account is now treated as pull-mode, that post appears twice. If the switch happens mid-fan-out, some followers have it pushed and others don't — so skipping the pull would lose it for the latter group.

The fix: record the mode on the post itself, not on the account.

posts(post_id, author_id, created_at, fanout_mode)
                                        -- 'pushed' | 'pull'

At post time, decide once and record it.
At read time:
   pushed portion → from the precomputed feed
   pull portion   → query celebrity accounts, but ONLY include posts
                    where fanout_mode = 'pull'
→ a post is never both. Deduplication becomes unnecessary rather
  than something to handle.

Handling an in-flight fan-out when the threshold is crossed mid-job: let it complete. Cancelling halfway leaves some followers with the post and others without, and the post is marked pushed so the pull path will skip it — meaning the unreached followers would never see it. Finishing the job is cheaper than reconciling a partial one.

Add hysteresis so an account hovering near the boundary doesn't flip modes repeatedly: switch to pull above 100,000 followers, but only back to push below 80,000. Flapping between modes makes behaviour hard to reason about and complicates debugging.

The reverse transition (a celebrity losing followers) is easier — new posts start being pushed, old ones remain marked pull, and both paths coexist correctly because the mode is per-post.

The general principle: when a system has two modes, record which mode each item was created under rather than inferring it from current state. Current state changes; historical facts don't.

Exercise 5 (Hard): Feed ranking is down. What do you serve, and what does that reveal about the architecture?
Solution

Serve a chronological feed. The retrieval layer — the pushed feed plus the celebrity pull — is independent of ranking, so the candidate set is still available. Sort by recency and return it.

try:
    candidates = get_hybrid_feed(user_id)
    return rank(candidates, timeout=50ms)[:50]
except (RankerUnavailable, Timeout):
    return sorted(candidates, key=recency)[:50]    # degraded, not broken

What this reveals about the architecture — and it's the point of the question:

1. Retrieval and ranking must be separable. If ranking were fused into retrieval, there would be no fallback at all. The funnel structure isn't only a performance optimisation — it's what makes graceful degradation possible.

2. The feed is the product, so it must always render. An empty feed is indistinguishable from a broken app, and users who see one may not come back. Chronological is worse than ranked; it is enormously better than nothing.

3. Ranking is enhancement, not correctness. Nothing is wrong about a chronological feed — it's what these products originally were. That makes it a safe fallback in a way that, say, falling back on inventory checks in e-commerce would not be.

Additional degradation layers: cache the last ranked feed per user with a short TTL and serve that if it's recent; fall back to a simple heuristic ranking (recency plus author affinity) that needs no ML service; and apply a strict timeout so a slow ranker degrades to chronological rather than making the feed slow — since slow is often worse than degraded.

The general principle: for every component, ask "if this is unavailable, what do we serve?" Deciding in advance turns an incident into a configuration change. The components with no acceptable fallback — inventory correctness, payment idempotency — are the ones deserving the most engineering rigour.

Exercise 6 (Hard): Users complain the feed shows the same few accounts repeatedly. Diagnose and fix.
Solution

This is an engagement feedback loop, and it's the same failure as the recommendation filter bubble — the ranker is working exactly as designed and producing a bad product.

The loop:
   ranker predicts high engagement for account A
   → A's posts get top placement
   → placement drives engagement (position bias)
   → the model reads that as "A is engaging"
   → A gets even more placement
Each cycle tightens. Accounts that were never shown accumulate no
engagement data, so they are never shown.

Diagnosis — measure it rather than assuming: per-user author diversity (distinct accounts in the last N impressions) and platform-wide creator coverage (what fraction of active accounts received impressions this week). If either is trending down, the loop is running.

Fixes, in order:

1. Diversity constraints at ranking time — the most direct lever, and a rule rather than a model change:

cap posts per author in one feed page (e.g. max 2 of 50)
or apply MMR: score = λ × relevance − (1−λ) × similarity to
               already-selected items

2. Correct for position bias in training. The model currently learns "position 1 gets clicked" rather than "this content is good". Weight training examples by inverse propensity — the probability the item was shown — so a click on something ranked 40th counts for more than one ranked 1st. Without this, the loop is baked into the training data itself.

3. Exploration budget — reserve some slots for accounts the model is uncertain about, so new and rarely-shown creators can accumulate signal.

4. Optimise for session and next-day-return rather than per-post engagement. Variety demonstrably performs better on longer horizons — a user shown ten posts from one account engages less overall than one shown a varied feed.

5. Weight negative signals heavily — mutes, hides, unfollows. A user hiding three posts from an account is strong evidence that no engagement metric captures.

The honest tension: diversity typically reduces short-term engagement, so it will lose most A/B tests. It has to be a deliberate product decision informed by retention and creator ecosystem health, not something an experiment framework decides — the same judgement as accepting a small oversell rate or approximate leaderboard ranks. The measurable metric and the desired outcome are in conflict, and recognising that is the senior answer.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Push (fan-out on write) is the right default for feeds — reads vastly outnumber writes, so precompute once and serve many cheap reads.
  • Pure push breaks on celebrities: 100M followers means 100M writes for one post, over half an hour of a node's capacity.
  • The hybrid is the answer — push for normal accounts, pull for accounts above a tuned threshold, merged at read time.
  • Don't fan out to inactive followers — 80–90% of those writes land in feeds nobody opens. Often a bigger saving than the celebrity hybrid.
  • Cap and trim feed lists, or per-user-per-day growth becomes a petabyte problem.
  • The media pipeline dominates cost: feed metadata is 2 TB, media egress is 10 PB/day. Optimising the feed alone optimises the cheap half.
  • Modern formats and right-sized variants are the biggest cost levers — WebP/AVIF at 30% of JPEG, thumbnails instead of originals.
  • Upload direct to object storage with pre-signed URLs, and process variants asynchronously so posts appear instantly.
  • Record the fan-out mode per post, not per account, so threshold transitions can't duplicate or lose content.
  • Retrieval and ranking must be separable so the feed degrades to chronological rather than failing.
  • Ranking creates feedback loops — correct for position bias, add diversity constraints and exploration, or the feed collapses onto a few accounts.
  • Push vs pull is one trade with different inputs — chat reaches the opposite conclusion because its recipient and read ratios are inverted.
