Cost, Capacity & SLO Engineering Senior / Staff

What you'll master: why you cannot run a system near 100% utilisation (the queueing theory most engineers never learn), how to derive provisioning from an SLO rather than a guess, where cloud bills actually go — which is rarely where people look — unit economics as the metric that scales with the business, and total cost of ownership as the framing that decides build-versus-buy.

Prerequisites: Estimation, Observability (SLIs, SLOs, error budgets), Multi-Region & DR (resilience spend). Pairs with Trade-off Driven Design.

🎯 What this topic is really testing. Whether you treat cost as a design constraint alongside latency and availability, rather than as someone else's problem. A technically superior design that costs 10× more is frequently the wrong design — and the engineer who can say "this architecture costs roughly $40k/month at our scale, here's the cheaper option and what it gives up" is operating at a different level from one who only discusses throughput.

1. Why You Can't Run at 100% Utilisation

The instinct is that a server at 90% CPU is efficiently used. Queueing theory says otherwise, and this is the single most useful piece of maths in capacity planning:

For an M/M/1 queue, average wait time relative to service time:

   W = 1 / (1 − ρ)        where ρ = utilisation

   ρ = 0.50  →  W = 2×      (wait ≈ service time)
   ρ = 0.70  →  W = 3.3×
   ρ = 0.80  →  W = 5×
   ρ = 0.90  →  W = 10×
   ρ = 0.95  →  W = 20×
   ρ = 0.99  →  W = 100×

Latency does NOT rise linearly with load — it rises HYPERBOLICALLY,
and the knee is around 70-80%.

Going from 70% to 90% utilisation "saves" 22% of your servers and
TRIPLES your latency.

Two consequences that matter:

1. "We have 30% headroom" is not 30% more traffic. At 70% utilisation, adding 20% more traffic takes you to 84% and roughly doubles queueing delay. Headroom in utilisation terms is not headroom in latency terms.
2. Target ~70% for latency-sensitive services, and higher (85–90%) only for batch work where queueing delay doesn't matter. This is why the estimation lesson divides by 0.7 when converting QPS to servers.

2. Deriving Capacity From an SLO

SLO: 99% of requests under 200 ms.

WRONG: provision for average load and hope peaks are close enough.
   → the SLO is violated precisely during peaks, which is when
     traffic — and therefore user impact — is highest.

RIGHT, working backwards:
   1. p99 target 200 ms; measured service time ~40 ms
      → the queueing budget is 160 ms, i.e. W ≤ 5×
      → from the table above, ρ ≤ 0.80
   2. peak load = 6,000 req/s (3x average, measured)
   3. per-server capacity at acceptable latency = 500 req/s
   4. servers = 6,000 / 500 / 0.80 = 15
   5. survive one AZ of three failing → the surviving 2/3 must carry
      everything:  15 / (2/3) ≈ 23 servers
   6. add growth headroom (say 6 months at the observed rate)

Every number is derived from a stated requirement. That is the
difference between capacity planning and guessing.

Name the scaling trigger explicitly. "We'll add servers if it gets slow" is not a plan. "Scale out when p99 exceeds 150 ms sustained for 5 minutes, or when utilisation exceeds 70%" is — and it can be automated, alerted on, and reviewed.

4. Deep Dive: Unit Economics

Total cost is nearly useless as a management metric — it grows with the business, so it can't distinguish healthy growth from deteriorating efficiency. Cost per unit of value can:

   cost per active user     cost per order      cost per GB served
   cost per API call        cost per tenant     cost per transaction

Choose the unit the BUSINESS uses, so the metric is comparable
against revenue per unit.

WHY IT MATTERS:
   Month 1: $100k infra,  1M users  → $0.10/user
   Month 6: $250k infra, 4M users  → $0.0625/user

Total cost rose 150% and the business got MORE efficient. Without
per-unit tracking, that looks like a problem to be cut rather than
scale working as intended.

THE INVERSE IS THE REAL WARNING:
   cost/user RISING while users grow means something is scaling
   superlinearly — a query that got slower with data volume, an N+1
   that grows with the catalogue, unbounded log retention. That is
   the signal worth alerting on.

The most valuable thing unit economics enables is comparing infrastructure cost against revenue per unit. If you earn $2/user/month and spend $0.10 on infrastructure, cost optimisation is not your priority. If you spend $1.80, it's existential. That ratio determines how much engineering time cost work deserves — and answering "should we optimise this?" with a number rather than an opinion is the point.

7. Error Budgets as a Capacity Signal

The observability lesson introduced error budgets for alerting. They also drive capacity decisions:

   99.9% SLO over 30 days = ~43 minutes of allowed failure.

If capacity shortfalls are consuming that budget:
   → you cannot also spend it on risky deploys and experiments
   → so an under-provisioned system directly reduces SHIPPING VELOCITY

This reframes capacity from an ops concern into a product one:
"we're spending our error budget on being under-provisioned, which
means we can't ship features safely" is an argument a product leader
understands, and it converts an infrastructure request into a
velocity conversation.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — Why not run servers at 95% utilisation?

Because latency rises hyperbolically with utilisation, not linearly. Queueing theory gives the relationship between wait time and utilisation ρ:

W = 1 / (1 − ρ)

   ρ = 0.70 → wait ≈ 3.3× service time
   ρ = 0.90 → 10×
   ρ = 0.95 → 20×
   ρ = 0.99 → 100×

At 95% utilisation a request that takes 40 ms to service waits ~800 ms in queue. The server is "efficiently used" and the user experience is broken.

The saving is also smaller than it looks. Going from 70% to 90% utilisation lets you remove 22% of your servers — and triples latency. That's rarely a good trade for a user-facing service.

Two further reasons:

• No room to absorb spikes. At 95%, a 10% traffic increase pushes you past capacity and queues grow without bound.
• No room for failure. If one of three AZs fails, the survivors must absorb its load. At 95% they cannot, so the failure cascades — which is why capacity planning includes an N−1 term.

The practical target is ~70% for latency-sensitive services, and 85–90% is reasonable for batch work where queueing delay doesn't matter. It's also why the estimation lesson divides by 0.7 when converting QPS into a server count — that divisor is this maths, applied.

🟢 Beginner — Why track cost per user rather than total cost?

Because total cost grows with the business, so it can't distinguish healthy growth from deteriorating efficiency.

Month 1: $100k infra,  1M users → $0.10/user
Month 6: $250k infra,  4M users → $0.0625/user

Total cost rose 150%. Efficiency improved 38%.

Looking only at the total, this appears to be a cost problem
requiring cuts. Looking at the unit, it's scale working correctly.

The genuinely valuable signal is the inverse: cost per user rising while users grow means something scales superlinearly — a query that degrades with data volume, an N+1 that grows with the catalogue, log retention with no policy. That's a real problem, and it's completely invisible in the total, which is also rising for legitimate reasons.

Choose the unit the business already uses — per user, per order, per transaction, per tenant — so it can be compared directly against revenue per unit. That comparison is what tells you how much cost work is worth: if you earn $2/user and spend $0.10, optimisation isn't your priority; if you spend $1.80, it's existential.

It also enables per-feature and per-tenant attribution, which surfaces things like a single enterprise customer consuming a disproportionate share of infrastructure — a commercial conversation as much as a technical one.

🟡 Mid — Derive the server count for an SLO of "99% under 200 ms" at 6,000 req/s peak. 
1. LATENCY BUDGET
   p99 target 200 ms, measured service time ~40 ms
   → queueing budget 160 ms → W ≤ 5× service time
   → from W = 1/(1−ρ):  ρ ≤ 0.80

2. PER-SERVER CAPACITY
   measured: 500 req/s at acceptable latency

3. RAW COUNT
   6,000 / 500 = 12 servers at 100% utilisation
   at ρ = 0.80: 12 / 0.8 = 15 servers

4. FAILURE HEADROOM
   survive one of three AZs failing → the remaining 2/3 carry all
   traffic:  15 / (2/3) ≈ 23 servers

5. GROWTH
   add headroom for the forecast horizon (say 6 months at the
   observed growth rate) so you're not re-planning monthly.

ANSWER: ~23-25 servers, spread across 3 AZs.

Every number is derived from a stated requirement, which is what distinguishes capacity planning from guessing. The two steps people skip are the utilisation cap (step 1 — treating servers as usable to 100%) and failure headroom (step 4 — sizing for the happy path only).

What I'd verify: that 500 req/s figure must be measured at acceptable latency, not the maximum throughput before falling over — those are very different numbers. And the peak must be measured, not assumed: a 3× peak-to-average ratio is typical for consumer traffic but wrong for event-driven products that spike 10×+.

And name the scaling trigger: "scale out when p99 exceeds 150 ms sustained for 5 minutes, or utilisation exceeds 70%" — automatable, alertable and reviewable, unlike "add servers if it gets slow".

🟡 Mid — Self-host Kafka at $8k/month or use managed at $25k/month?

Managed, in almost all cases — because the sticker prices omit the largest cost.

SELF-HOSTED, fully loaded:
   infrastructure              $8,000/mo
   ~0.5 engineer (ongoing)    $12,000/mo   ← the dominant term
   on-call burden              $3,000/mo
   expected outage cost        $4,000/mo
   ────────────────────────────────────
   TCO                        $27,000/mo

MANAGED                       $25,000/mo
   and your engineers work on the product instead.

The "0.5 engineer" is not padding. Operating Kafka means version upgrades, partition rebalancing, broker replacement, capacity planning, monitoring, and debugging consumer-lag incidents at 3am. A fully-loaded senior engineer costs $20–25k/month, so half their attention exceeds the entire infrastructure difference being argued about.

The opportunity cost is larger still. That engineer isn't just expensive — they're not building product. Nobody wins customers by operating Kafka well.

When self-hosting genuinely wins:

• Very large scale, where the managed premium reaches millions and a dedicated platform team is justified regardless.
• You need control the managed service doesn't expose — specific configurations, plugins, or compliance requirements.
• You already have the expertise and it isn't a distraction from the product.

The general principle: buy anything that isn't your differentiator, and be honest about engineering time when comparing. The most common error in build-versus-buy is treating engineer hours as free because they don't appear on the cloud invoice.

🔴 Senior — Your cloud bill doubled in six months. How do you investigate?

Start by establishing whether it's a problem at all — that's the step people skip.

1. Check unit economics first. If traffic also doubled, cost per user is flat and this is scale working as intended. If traffic grew 20% while cost grew 100%, something scales superlinearly and that's the real finding.

2. Break down by line item and diff against six months ago. Which category grew, and does it correlate with a known change? The usual suspects, in order of frequency:

• Data egress — a new feature serving larger media, a chatty service mesh crossing AZs (cross-AZ traffic is charged), or a CDN misconfiguration causing origin fetches.
• Observability — frequently the fastest-growing line. One high-cardinality metric label added six months ago can multiply the bill; log volume grows superlinearly with traffic.
• Storage — it's cumulative. Without lifecycle policies, snapshots and old logs accrue forever.
• Orphaned resources — unattached volumes, forgotten dev environments, load balancers with no targets. Typically 5–20% of spend at most companies, and pure waste.
• Managed services quietly added without review.

3. Attribute by team and service using tags. Untagged spend is unmanageable spend, and if tagging is poor, fixing that is the highest-value first action.

4. Act in order of cost-to-benefit: delete waste (free), right-size instances (cheap — most fleets run at 15% CPU), apply lifecycle policies, then commitments for the confident baseline, and only then architectural changes requiring engineering time.

What I'd be careful about: a doubled bill often reflects genuine growth, and cutting reflexively can harm reliability. The right output is a per-unit efficiency target and a scaling trigger, not a blanket instruction to reduce spend — and if unit economics improved, the honest answer to leadership is "this is fine, here's why".

🔴 Senior — How do you decide how much reliability to buy?

By pricing the options and letting the business choose — reliability spend is a business decision with a technical mechanism, and each additional nine costs roughly 10× more than the last.

99%     → ~7.3 hours downtime/month
99.9%   → ~43 minutes
99.99%  → ~4.3 minutes
99.999% → ~26 seconds — no time for human response; everything
                        must be automated

1. Quantify the cost of downtime. Revenue per hour is the starting point, but usually the smallest component — SLA credits, churn, reputational damage and regulatory exposure often dominate. A B2B platform with contractual SLAs has a very different curve from an internal tool.

2. Price each level. Going from 99.9% to 99.99% typically means multi-AZ everything, automated failover, aggressive redundancy and much more rigorous testing — often a 2–3× infrastructure increase plus significant engineering time.

3. Find where the curve crosses. If an hour of downtime costs $6k and you experience four hours a year, that's $24k of expected loss. Spending $600k/year to avoid it is clearly wrong; spending $30k may be right.

4. Recognise the ceiling you don't control. Users' own networks are less reliable than 99.99%, so beyond a point you're buying reliability nobody can perceive. And your dependencies impose their own limits — you cannot be more available than the services you require.

5. Set it per service, not globally. Checkout and authentication may warrant 99.99%; recommendations and reporting may be fine at 99%. A uniform target overspends on the unimportant and underspends on the critical.

The framing that lands with non-engineers: "Which of these outcomes is acceptable when — not if — we have an incident?" That's a question a business leader can answer. "Should we invest in reliability?" is not, and asking it that way guarantees an unhelpful answer.

And I'd note the error budget connection: if capacity shortfalls are consuming the budget, you cannot also spend it on shipping. Under-provisioning therefore costs velocity, which is often a more persuasive argument internally than availability alone.

🏋️ Graded Exercises

Exercise 1 (Easy): Your service runs at 85% CPU. Marketing forecasts 20% traffic growth. What happens?
Solution 
Current:  ρ = 0.85 → W = 1/(1−0.85) = 6.7× service time
After 20% more traffic:
          ρ = 0.85 × 1.2 = 1.02 → OVER 100%

The system cannot serve the load at all. Queues grow without bound,
latency climbs until requests time out, clients retry, and effective
load rises further — a cascading failure.

Even reaching ρ = 0.95 (if capacity were slightly higher) means
W = 20×, so a 40 ms service time becomes 800 ms of queueing.

The point: at 85% you are already past the knee of the curve. There is no headroom for growth, for a traffic spike, or for losing a node — and the failure when it comes is non-linear rather than gradual.

What to do: scale out before the traffic arrives, targeting ~70% at the new volume. Required capacity increases by 1.2 (traffic) ÷ 0.7 (target utilisation) ÷ 0.85 (current) ≈ 1.45× the current fleet.

The broader lesson: 85% CPU looks like efficient use of resources on a dashboard, and is actually an incident waiting for a modest traffic increase. Capacity alerts should fire on utilisation trending toward 70%, not on saturation — by the time CPU alarms at 90%, latency has already degraded badly.

Exercise 2 (Easy): Name three cost levers you'd pull before rewriting a service, in order.
Solution

1. Delete waste (free, 5–20%). Unattached storage volumes, forgotten dev and staging environments, load balancers with no targets, old snapshots, idle instances from a scaled-down experiment. Most organisations carry a meaningful percentage of pure waste, and reclaiming it requires no engineering judgement — just an inventory.

2. Right-size instances (cheap, 20–40%). Fleets are usually provisioned by guess and run at 15% CPU. Compare requested resources against actual utilisation and resize. Low risk, quick, and it often dwarfs what a rewrite would save.

3. Storage lifecycle policies and observability hygiene (cheap, grows over time). Tier or expire old data automatically; sample verbose logs; remove high-cardinality metric labels. Observability alone is frequently 5–15% of the bill and often 30–50% reducible without losing anything anyone uses.

Then, if still needed: commitments/reserved capacity for the confident baseline (30–60% on committed spend, at the cost of flexibility), and spot instances for batch and stateless work (60–90%, at the cost of interruption handling).

Why rewriting comes last: it's the most expensive lever in engineering time, the slowest to deliver, and the riskiest. Teams frequently propose re-architecting while paying for hundreds of idle instances — spending months of engineering time to save less than a two-day cleanup would have.

The exception is when the estimate shows a single architectural fact dominates the bill — serving 2 MB images where 200 KB would do, for instance. Then the rewrite is the highest-value lever, which is why you estimate the bill's composition before choosing.

Exercise 3 (Medium): Derive capacity for a service with a 100 ms p99 SLO, 20 ms service time, and 10,000 req/s peak.
Solution 
1. LATENCY BUDGET
   p99 target 100 ms, service time 20 ms
   → queueing budget 80 ms → W ≤ 4× service time
   → 1/(1−ρ) ≤ 4  →  ρ ≤ 0.75

2. PER-SERVER CAPACITY
   at 20 ms service time, one server handles 1/0.020 = 50 req/s
   per core. Assume 8 usable cores → 400 req/s per server at
   full utilisation.

3. RAW COUNT AT TARGET UTILISATION
   10,000 / 400 = 25 servers at 100%
   at ρ = 0.75:  25 / 0.75 ≈ 34 servers

4. FAILURE HEADROOM (survive one of three AZs)
   34 / (2/3) ≈ 51 servers

5. GROWTH — add the forecast horizon.

ANSWER: ~51 servers across 3 AZs, ~17 per AZ.

The two steps that are usually omitted: the utilisation cap (treating servers as usable to 100%, which the queueing maths forbids) and the failure term (sizing so that losing an AZ doesn't push the survivors past their limit).

What I'd validate before trusting this:

• The 20 ms service time must be measured under realistic load, not in isolation — contention changes it.
• Cores aren't perfectly parallel. Shared resources, locks and GC mean 8 cores rarely give 8× a single core's throughput; measure actual per-server capacity rather than deriving it.
• The peak must be measured. A 3× peak-to-average ratio is typical for consumer traffic and badly wrong for event-driven products.

And load-test to confirm: the derivation gives you a starting point and the shape of the reasoning, but real per-server capacity at acceptable latency is an empirical number.

Exercise 4 (Medium): Your observability bill is 20% of infrastructure spend. Reduce it without losing the ability to debug.
Solution

20% is high but common — observability cost grows superlinearly with traffic, and it's rarely reviewed. The reductions are mostly free of debugging value.

1. Metric cardinality (usually the biggest and most dangerous). Audit series count per metric. One high-cardinality label — user ID, request ID, a raw URL path — creates a series per unique value and can multiply the bill overnight. Move those dimensions to logs and traces, where they belong, and use route templates rather than raw paths. This often halves the metrics line with no loss of capability.

2. Log volume and tiering.

ERROR/WARN → 100%, hot storage, 30-day retention
INFO       → 100% hot for 7 days, then cold object storage
DEBUG      → sampled at 0.1%, or OFF by default with the ability to
             enable dynamically per service when investigating

Dynamic debug logging is the key capability: being able to raise verbosity for one service at runtime costs nothing when off and preserves full debugging power when needed.

3. Trace sampling by value, not uniformly. Tail-based sampling keeps 100% of errors and slow traces while sampling successful fast requests at 0.1% — lower total volume than 1% head-based sampling, and it retains exactly the traces anyone opens during an incident.

4. Drop what nobody queries. Most observability platforms report which dashboards and metrics are actually used. Metrics nobody has queried in six months are pure cost.

What I would not cut: error logs, trace context propagation, and the SLI metrics backing your SLOs. Those are what you need most during an incident, and saving money by becoming blind is a false economy — one prolonged outage costs more than a year of the bill.

The framing: the goal isn't less observability, it's less redundant observability. Sampling by value and cutting cardinality preserve debugging capability while removing most of the volume.

Exercise 5 (Hard): Cost per user is rising while user count grows. Diagnose the possible causes.
Solution

This is the signal that matters — it means something scales superlinearly with users, so growth makes efficiency worse rather than better. Total cost alone would hide it.

Candidate causes, in order of likelihood:

1. Queries degrading with data volume. A query that was fine at 1M rows becomes expensive at 100M — a missing index, a full scan, or a join whose cost grows with the table. Each user costs more than the last because the dataset they query is larger. Check: query duration trends over time against table growth.

2. N+1 patterns that grow with the catalogue. A page fetching one row per item costs more as the catalogue grows, independent of user count.

3. Unbounded data retention. Storage is cumulative — without lifecycle policies, per-user data accumulates forever, so cost per user rises even at constant activity. Feed lists, event logs, and analytics tables are the usual culprits, which is why bounding them was a recurring theme in the case studies.

4. Cache hit rate falling. As the working set grows beyond cache capacity, more requests reach the database — and each miss is far more expensive than a hit. The hit rate degrades silently.

5. Fan-out growing with the network. In a social product, average connections per user grows over time, so each post costs more to deliver. Cost per user rises even with flat per-user activity.

6. Observability cardinality growing with users, if any metric is labelled per-user or per-tenant.

7. Non-technical: a changing user mix (enterprise customers using far more resources), or accumulated managed services and idle resources unrelated to growth.

How I'd investigate: break cost per user down by component — compute, storage, egress, database, observability — and identify which component's per-user cost is rising. That localises it immediately, and it's why per-unit tracking should be per line item rather than a single aggregate.

The general lesson: superlinear scaling is the thing that eventually kills a system's economics, and it's invisible in absolute numbers. Tracking cost per unit is what makes it detectable while it's still cheap to fix.

Exercise 6 (Hard): Present a $600k/year resilience investment to a sceptical CFO.
Solution

Frame it as a priced choice about acceptable loss, not as an engineering request. A CFO cannot evaluate "we should be multi-region"; they can evaluate "which of these outcomes is acceptable".

1. Quantify the exposure honestly, including what isn't revenue:

Revenue $50M/year ≈ $5,700/hour of downtime.
Historical: one multi-hour regional event every ~2 years.
   → direct revenue exposure ≈ $11-25k/year

But direct revenue is usually the SMALLEST component:
   - SLA credits owed to enterprise customers (contractual, immediate)
   - churn following a visible outage
   - sales cycles lost when procurement asks for our DR posture
   - regulatory exposure, if applicable
   - engineering time consumed by the incident and its aftermath

2. Present options with prices, not a recommendation:

tested backups + runbook   +$20k/yr   RTO hours
pilot light                +$300k/yr  RTO ~30 min
warm standby               +$600k/yr  RTO ~5 min
active-active              +$1.4M/yr  RTO seconds

3. Name the non-insurance benefits, which often carry the decision. A second region frequently doubles as latency reduction for users on another continent — moving it from pure cost avoidance to a product improvement. And documented DR is increasingly a sales requirement: if enterprise deals are being lost or delayed at the security-review stage, resilience spend is a revenue enabler, and that's a far stronger argument than downtime avoidance.

4. Offer a staged path. Tested backups and a rehearsed runbook cost very little and remove the worst outcomes. Propose starting there with a defined trigger for the next stage ("when enterprise revenue exceeds X, or when we sign a customer requiring a 15-minute RTO"). A staged request with a decision rule is far more persuasive than a single large ask.

5. Be willing to accept "no". If the business decides an occasional multi-hour outage is acceptable for this system, that is a legitimate decision — and your job is then to document the accepted risk clearly, so the choice is explicit rather than accidental. Engineers who treat every "no" on reliability as a failure to communicate are usually the ones not being listened to.

What I'd avoid: arguing for resilience on principle, using availability percentages without translating them into hours, or presenting a single option as though it were the only responsible choice. The credibility comes from showing you'd genuinely recommend the cheap option when the numbers support it.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Latency rises hyperbolically with utilisation — W = 1/(1−ρ). The knee is around 70–80%, and 90% means 10× queueing delay.
  • "30% headroom" is not 30% more traffic — queueing is non-linear.
  • Derive capacity from the SLO: latency budget → utilisation cap → server count → failure headroom → growth.
  • Name the scaling trigger explicitly so it can be automated and reviewed.
  • Estimate where the bill goes before optimising — egress and observability often dominate compute.
  • Track cost per unit, not total — total grows with the business and hides deteriorating efficiency.
  • Rising cost-per-user means something scales superlinearly — degrading queries, unbounded retention, falling cache hit rates.
  • Compare TCO, not sticker prices. Half an engineer's attention usually exceeds the infrastructure difference.
  • Buy anything that isn't your differentiator — nobody wins customers by operating Kafka well.
  • Pull cost levers in order: waste, right-sizing, lifecycle policies, commitments — then architecture.
  • Each additional nine costs ~10× more, and beyond a point you're buying reliability users can't perceive.
  • Under-provisioning spends the error budget, which directly reduces shipping velocity — often the most persuasive internal argument.
