Design Notification System Case Study

What you'll master: fan-out across channels with wildly different reliability and cost profiles, why per-channel worker isolation is the decision that makes the system survivable, deduplication with a deterministic key, digesting to prevent notification fatigue, quiet hours done correctly across timezones, and how to guarantee a two-second OTP while a viral event floods the same pipeline.

Prerequisites: Pub/Sub (queues, idempotency, DLQs), Realtime Apps (fan-out, push notifications), Microservices (bulkheads). Pairs with Design Chat.

🎯 What this problem is really testing. Whether you notice that "notification" is not one thing. An OTP and a marketing email share a pipeline but have opposite requirements — one must arrive in two seconds and bypass every throttle, the other is best-effort and should be suppressed at 11pm. Designs that treat all notifications uniformly either make OTPs slow or spam users into disabling notifications entirely. The second signal is third-party dependency isolation: every delivery channel is an external provider that will fail.

The classification that shapes everything — establish it before designing:

TRANSACTIONAL   OTP, password reset, payment receipt, security alert
   must arrive · low latency · BYPASSES quiet hours and rate limits
   · user cannot opt out of some of these (legal/security)

ENGAGEMENT      comment reply, like, mention, follow
   should arrive · batchable · respects preferences and quiet hours

MARKETING       promotions, re-engagement
   best effort · strictly rate-limited · respects everything
   · opt-out is legally required (CAN-SPAM, GDPR)

These share infrastructure and share almost nothing else.

2. Estimation

Assume 50M users, 20 notifications per user per day.

VOLUME
   50M × 20 = 1B notifications/day
   1 × 10⁹ / 10⁵ s = 10,000/s average
   → but the AVERAGE is the wrong number here.

BURST — the number that actually shapes the design
   A viral post by an account with 10M followers, delivered over
   ~5 minutes:  10M / 300 s ≈ 33,000/s from ONE event,
   on top of the 10,000/s baseline.
   → CONSEQUENCE: peak is ~4x average and arrives without warning.
     Provisioning for peak wastes money; provisioning for average
     drops notifications. This asymmetry is exactly what a QUEUE is
     for — absorb the burst, drain at sustained capacity.

FAN-OUT COST
   1B notifications × ~1 KB payload = 1 TB/day of provider traffic
   SMS at ~$0.0075 each: sending even 1% by SMS = 10M/day = $75,000/day
   → CONSEQUENCE: channel choice is a COST decision, not just a
     delivery decision. SMS is ~1000x the cost of push.

STORAGE
   delivery records: 1B/day × 200 B = 200 GB/day
   → retain raw for days, aggregate after — same pattern as click
     analytics in TinyURL.

The SMS cost figure is worth saying out loud: it immediately explains why systems default to push, fall back to email, and reserve SMS for genuinely critical messages.

        │             │        │             │
     APNs/FCM     SendGrid   Twilio      own store
        └─── each with its own retry, DLQ, rate limit ───┘

Producers emit EVENTS, never channel instructions. The order service
should not know that shipping notifications go by email — that's a
preference the notification service resolves.
💡 The per-channel queue split is the single most important structural decision. It's the bulkhead pattern applied to third-party dependencies. When SendGrid has an outage — and it will — only the email queue backs up. Push and SMS keep flowing, OTPs keep arriving, and email drains when the provider recovers. A monolithic "send notification" worker couples every channel's failure modes together, so one provider's bad day becomes a total notification outage.

4. Deep Dive: Deduplication

Duplicates are highly visible — a user receiving "you have 3 new messages" twice notices immediately, and at-least-once delivery means retries are guaranteed, not exceptional.

The key must be DETERMINISTIC — derived from stable inputs, so a
retry computes the same value:

   notification_id = hash(user_id, event_id, channel, template_version)

NOT a per-attempt UUID, which would differ on every retry and dedupe
nothing.

Then make the check atomic with the send:

   BEGIN;
     INSERT INTO sent_notifications(notification_id, user_id, channel)
       VALUES ($1, $2, $3);         -- UNIQUE → duplicate raises
     -- record intent BEFORE calling the provider
   COMMIT;
   → then call the provider with notification_id as ITS idempotency key

Two layers, because the provider call can't join your transaction. The local unique constraint stops your own duplicate processing; the provider's idempotency key stops duplicates arising from a lost response (you sent it, the ack was lost, you retry). Without the second layer, a network failure at exactly the wrong moment still double-sends.

Bound the dedupe table — keep records only as long as retries are plausible (24 hours), then expire. Retaining 1B rows/day forever is a storage problem for a check that becomes meaningless after a day.

5. Deep Dive: Digesting and Notification Fatigue

The failure mode here is a product failure, not a technical one: a user who receives 50 pushes in a minute disables notifications permanently, and you have lost that channel forever.

50 people like a post in 2 minutes.

❌ 50 separate pushes → user disables notifications. Permanent loss.
✅ ONE push: "50 people liked your post"

DIGEST WINDOW by priority:
   transactional : none — send immediately
   mentions      : ~30 s   (people expect these fast)
   likes/follows : ~5-15 min
   marketing     : daily or weekly digest

Implementation — a tumbling window per (user, notification_type):
   1. first event: create a pending digest, schedule delivery at now+W
   2. subsequent events in the window: increment the counter, update
      the summary (do NOT schedule another delivery)
   3. at W: render the aggregate and send once
CapPurpose
Per-type digest windowCollapses bursts of the same kind into one message
Frequency cap per user per dayHard ceiling regardless of how many events fire — the backstop against a bug spamming users
Priority-aware cappingTransactional never counts against the cap; marketing is capped first

The frequency cap deserves emphasis as a safety mechanism. Most notification-spam incidents come from a bug or a bad backfill, not from designed behaviour — a job re-processing old events can send every user thousands of notifications. A hard per-user daily cap turns that from a catastrophe into a contained incident, and it's cheap to implement.

6. Deep Dive: Preferences, Quiet Hours and Localisation

preferences(user_id, notification_type, channel, enabled)
   → per TYPE per CHANNEL, not one global switch. "Email me about
     security, push me about mentions, never SMS me about marketing."

quiet_hours(user_id, start_local, end_local, timezone)

Quiet hours must be evaluated in the user's timezone, and this is where implementations go wrong:

❌ if 22 <= utc_hour or utc_hour < 8: suppress
   → silences Tokyo users during their afternoon.

✅ local = utc_now.astimezone(user.timezone)
   if in_quiet_window(local.time(), user.quiet_start, user.quiet_end):
       if priority == TRANSACTIONAL: send anyway
       else: schedule for the end of the window

Edge cases that must be handled:
   - windows crossing midnight (22:00–08:00 is NOT start<end)
   - DST transitions — store the IANA zone ("Europe/London"), never a
     fixed UTC offset, or the window shifts twice a year
   - users who travel: decide whether the zone is their home setting
     or their current location (home setting is usually less surprising)

Transactional bypasses quiet hours — a security alert about a login from a new device is worthless delivered at 8am. But be deliberate about what counts: a payment receipt probably can wait, while a fraud alert cannot. Getting this classification wrong in either direction is a real product problem.

Localisation: render templates per user locale, and note that this happens after preference filtering (no point rendering a message you won't send) and before queueing to the channel worker (so the worker handles bytes, not business logic). Version templates so a template change doesn't invalidate in-flight deduplication keys unexpectedly.

7. Deep Dive: Provider Failures and Delivery Guarantees

ChannelGuarantee availableFailure handling
Push (APNs/FCM)Best-effort. The provider accepts it; the device may be off, uninstalled, or the OS may throttleRetry on 5xx; on "invalid token" delete the token — retrying a dead token forever is a common bug
EmailAccepted ≠ delivered. Bounces and spam filtering happen asynchronouslyProcess bounce webhooks; suppress hard-bounced addresses permanently or you damage sender reputation
SMSDelivery receipts available, with real latencyExpensive — cap aggressively; carrier filtering varies by country
In-appYou control it — the only channel where you can guarantee durabilityStore it; the user sees it whenever they open the app

The key insight: no external channel guarantees delivery, so critical notifications need redundancy across channels rather than a stronger guarantee on one. An OTP goes by SMS and push; a security alert goes by push, email and in-app. The in-app record is the durable backstop, since it's the only one you own end to end.

Per-channel resilience (the bulkhead paying off):
   - independent retry with jittered exponential backoff
   - circuit breaker per provider: stop hammering a dead API, fail fast
   - DLQ per channel, ALERTED — an unmonitored DLQ is a slow silent drop
   - rate limit to the provider's documented quota, or they throttle
     you and everything backs up
   - MULTI-PROVIDER fallback for critical paths: if Twilio is down,
     route OTPs via a secondary SMS provider. This is why the worker
     abstracts "send SMS" rather than "call Twilio".

8. Deep Dive: Guaranteeing the 2-Second OTP

This is the requirement that forces real separation, because a viral event flooding the same pipeline would otherwise delay it:

1. SEPARATE QUEUE, not just a priority flag.
   A priority field inside one queue still means OTPs sit behind a
   backlog of a million engagement notifications. A dedicated
   high-priority queue with its own workers is the only reliable
   isolation.

2. BYPASS everything optional on that path:
   no digesting, no quiet hours, no frequency caps, minimal
   preference lookup (cache it, or accept a stale read).

3. DEDICATED WORKER CAPACITY, always warm and over-provisioned.
   OTP volume is low, so this is cheap — and it means a burst
   elsewhere can never starve it.

4. MULTI-PROVIDER with fast failover: if the primary SMS provider
   exceeds a 1 s timeout, immediately try the secondary. Don't retry
   the primary three times first — the deadline is 2 seconds total.

5. MEASURE IT: p99 latency on this path specifically, with its own
   SLO and alert. The aggregate notification latency metric will look
   fine while OTPs fail, because OTPs are a tiny fraction of volume —
   the averaging trap from the observability lesson.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — Why separate queues per channel instead of one notification queue?

Failure isolation — it's the bulkhead pattern applied to third-party dependencies.

Every delivery channel is an external provider that will eventually fail: SendGrid has outages, APNs has degradations, Twilio has regional problems. With a single shared queue, an email provider outage causes email jobs to retry and back up, consuming the shared workers — so push and SMS notifications queue behind them and stop flowing too. One provider's bad day becomes a total notification outage.

With per-channel queues and workers, an email outage backs up only the email queue. Push notifications, SMS and OTPs continue unaffected, and email drains when the provider recovers.

Three further benefits:

• Independent scaling — channels have very different volumes and per-message costs, so they need different worker counts.
• Independent rate limiting — each provider has its own quota, and exceeding it gets you throttled or blocked.
• Independent retry policy — an email can retry for hours; an OTP that's 30 seconds late is useless and should be abandoned.

The same reasoning extends to priority: transactional notifications need a separate queue from bulk ones, because a priority field inside one queue still leaves an OTP waiting behind a million queued engagement notifications.

🟢 Beginner — How do you prevent sending the same notification twice?

With a deterministic idempotency key checked atomically before sending. Retries are guaranteed in an at-least-once system, so the design must make them harmless.

notification_id = hash(user_id, event_id, channel, template_version)

The key must be derived from stable inputs. A UUID generated per delivery attempt would differ on every retry and deduplicate nothing — that's the most common implementation error here.

Then check atomically:

INSERT INTO sent_notifications(notification_id, ...) VALUES (...);
-- UNIQUE constraint → a duplicate raises a violation
-- Catch it, treat as already-sent, ack the message.

A "SELECT then send then INSERT" sequence has both a crash window (send succeeds, process dies before recording) and a race (two concurrent retries both find nothing). The unique constraint provides the mutual exclusion.

Two layers are needed, because the provider call can't be inside your transaction: pass notification_id to the provider as its idempotency key. That covers the case where you called the provider successfully but lost the response — your local record may be absent while the message was already sent.

Expire the dedupe table after ~24 hours. Beyond the plausible retry window the check has no value, and at 1B notifications/day, retaining it indefinitely is a serious storage cost for nothing.

🟡 Mid — 50 people like a post in two minutes. Design the notification behaviour.

Send one notification: "50 people liked your post." Fifty separate pushes is a product failure — the user disables notifications, and you lose that channel permanently. That's the worst possible outcome, and it's irreversible.

Digest with a tumbling window per (user, notification_type):

1. First like arrives → create a pending digest for
   (user, "post_likes", post_id), schedule delivery at now + 10 min.
2. Subsequent likes in that window → increment the count and update
   the summary. Do NOT schedule another delivery.
3. At the deadline → render "N people liked your post" and send once.
4. If the user opens the app first → cancel the pending push (they've
   already seen it in-app).

Window length by type, since expectations differ:

mentions / direct replies : ~30 s   (people expect these promptly)
likes / follows           : 5-15 min
marketing                 : daily or weekly
transactional             : none — send immediately

Layer a hard frequency cap on top — say 10 engagement notifications per user per day regardless of activity. This is the safety net that matters most in practice, because notification-spam incidents usually come from a bug or a backfill re-processing old events rather than from designed behaviour. A cap turns that from a catastrophe into a contained incident.

Step 4 is worth calling out: cancelling pending notifications when the user has already seen the content in-app is a small detail that markedly improves quality — nobody wants a push about something they just read.

The trade is latency for quality: the user learns about likes up to 10 minutes late, which for engagement content nobody minds, and it's why the digest window must vary by type rather than being global.

🟡 Mid — Implement quiet hours correctly. What are the edge cases?

The rule is that quiet hours are evaluated in the user's local timezone, and transactional notifications bypass them.

local = utc_now.astimezone(ZoneInfo(user.timezone))   # IANA zone
if in_window(local.time(), user.quiet_start, user.quiet_end):
    if priority == TRANSACTIONAL:
        send()                       # security alerts can't wait
    else:
        schedule_for(end_of_window_in_local_time)
else:
    send()

The edge cases, each of which causes a real bug:

1. Evaluating in UTC. if 22 <= utc_hour or utc_hour < 8 silences Tokyo users during their afternoon and does nothing for users in the Americas at night. This is the most common error.

2. Windows crossing midnight. 22:00–08:00 cannot be tested with start <= t <= end, because start > end. Needs t >= start or t <= end when the window wraps.

3. DST. Store the IANA zone name ("Europe/London"), never a fixed offset ("+00:00"). With a stored offset, quiet hours silently shift by an hour twice a year. Also, some local times don't exist (the spring-forward gap) or occur twice (autumn), so scheduling "deliver at 08:00 local" needs care.

4. Travel. Decide whether the timezone is the user's home setting or their current location. Home is usually less surprising — a user on a short trip generally doesn't want their quiet hours to shift — but it should be an explicit decision.

5. Queued notifications releasing simultaneously. If everyone's quiet hours end at 08:00, you get a thundering herd at that moment across each timezone. Jitter the release, exactly as with cache TTLs.

6. Classification. "Transactional bypasses quiet hours" needs care: a fraud alert genuinely can't wait; a payment receipt can. Getting this wrong wakes people for receipts, or delays security alerts by eight hours.

🔴 Senior — Guarantee OTP delivery within 2 seconds while a viral event floods the system.

The requirement forces genuine physical isolation, not just prioritisation — and explaining why a priority flag is insufficient is the core of the answer.

1. A separate queue, not a priority field. With one queue, a viral event enqueues millions of messages ahead of the OTP; even with priority ordering, workers are busy and the OTP waits. A dedicated queue with dedicated workers is the only reliable isolation.

2. Dedicated, always-warm, over-provisioned workers. OTP volume is tiny relative to bulk traffic, so keeping generous headroom is cheap — and it guarantees a burst elsewhere can never starve this path. Don't autoscale it reactively; scaling takes longer than the deadline.

3. Strip everything optional from the path. No digesting, no quiet hours, no frequency caps, no template A/B logic. Cache the minimal preference lookup, or accept a stale read — a two-second budget doesn't allow a synchronous preference-service call.

4. Multi-provider with aggressive failover. A 1-second timeout on the primary SMS provider, then immediately try the secondary. Critically, don't retry the primary three times first — the deadline is two seconds total, so the retry budget must be spent on a different provider rather than the same failing one.

5. Send by multiple channels simultaneously for the most critical cases — SMS and push together. No external channel guarantees delivery, so redundancy across channels is the only real guarantee available.

6. Measure this path separately. This is where the design usually fails silently: OTPs are a tiny fraction of volume, so aggregate notification latency stays green while OTP p99 is 30 seconds. It needs its own SLO, its own dashboard and its own alert — the averaging trap from observability.

The trade to state: this costs money — idle warm capacity, a second SMS provider, and duplicate sends across channels. That's justified because a failed OTP means a user cannot log in, which is a conversion and support cost far exceeding the infrastructure. Tying the spend to the business impact is what makes it a decision rather than gold-plating.

🔴 Senior — Your email provider is down for 6 hours. Walk through what happens and what you'd have built.

What should happen with the design above: the email queue backs up while push, SMS and in-app are entirely unaffected. Email workers see 5xx responses, the circuit breaker opens, and they stop hammering the dead API — retrying with jittered backoff instead. When the provider recovers, the queue drains at a controlled rate. Users receive their emails late; nothing is lost.

What breaks if you haven't designed for it:

• A shared queue means email retries consume workers and block push and SMS.
• Without a circuit breaker, workers spend their time waiting on timeouts — the slow-dependency failure from microservices, where slow is worse than down.
• Without backoff, you hammer a recovering provider and prolong the outage.
• An unbounded queue grows until it exhausts memory or disk.
• On recovery, draining six hours of backlog at full speed re-triggers the provider's rate limits.

What I'd have built, beyond the isolation:

1. A secondary provider for critical email (password resets, security alerts). The worker abstracts "send email" rather than "call SendGrid", so failover is a routing decision. Bulk marketing email can simply wait.

2. Bounded queue with an age-based policy. A marketing email six hours stale is worthless — drop it rather than delivering a confusing message. A password reset is also stale, but there the right action is to notify the user to request a new one. Encode the TTL per notification type.

3. Controlled drain on recovery — ramp throughput gradually rather than flooding the provider, which would trip their rate limits and restart the outage.

4. Channel fallback for the critical path: if email is down and the notification is security-critical, send by push or SMS instead. This requires the notification service to understand intent ("alert this user") rather than mechanism ("email this user") — which is exactly why producers publish events rather than channel instructions.

5. Alerting on queue age, not just depth — depth can look stable while the oldest message is hours old.

The framing: every notification channel is a third-party dependency with an availability you don't control. The design question isn't "how do we prevent this" but "what degrades, and what still works" — and the per-channel isolation is what makes the answer "only email is late".

Exercise 1 (Easy): An account with 10M followers posts. Compute the notification burst and say what it forces.
Solution 
10M followers, but not all get a push:
   ~40% have notifications enabled for this account → 4M notifications
   delivered over ~5 minutes (300 s)

   4,000,000 / 300 ≈ 13,300 notifications/s from ONE event
   on top of a ~10,000/s baseline → peak ~2.3x average, instantly.

What it forces:

1. A queue between event and delivery. The burst arrives without warning, so you either provision permanently for peak (wasteful) or absorb it in a queue and drain at sustained capacity. The queue converts a capacity problem into a latency problem — which is exactly the right trade here, since nobody minds learning about a post 30 seconds late.

2. Batching the fan-out. Don't emit 4M individual messages. The fan-out service should emit batches ({post_id, follower_ids[1000]}), turning 4M messages into 4,000 — a 1000× reduction in queue operations, and the same batching move as chat and feed fan-out.

3. Priority separation. This burst must not delay OTPs, which is only reliable with a physically separate high-priority queue.

4. Autoscaling on queue depth, not CPU — workers waiting on provider APIs have low CPU while the backlog grows.

5. Accepting delay. Delivering over 10 minutes instead of 5 is fine. Recognising which requirements are soft is what makes the burst affordable.

Exercise 2 (Easy): Why should producing services publish events rather than calling "send email"?
Solution

Because the producer shouldn't know how a user wants to be contacted — that's a preference, and it changes independently of the business logic.

❌ order_service.send_email(user, "Your order shipped")
   - the order service now knows about email templates, user
     preferences, quiet hours, localisation, and provider APIs
   - a user preferring SMS requires changing the ORDER service
   - adding an in-app channel means changing EVERY producer

✅ publish OrderShipped { order_id, user_id, tracking }
   - the notification service resolves channels from preferences
   - adding a channel is one change, in one place
   - the order service knows only that an order shipped

The three benefits:

1. Decoupling — producers describe facts, the notification service decides delivery. Adding SMS support requires no producer changes.

2. Consistency — preferences, quiet hours, dedupe and rate limits are enforced in one place. If every service sent its own emails, each would implement (and forget) these rules differently.

3. Multiple consumers — the same OrderShipped event also feeds analytics and the fulfilment service. A direct "send email" call serves exactly one purpose.

This is the command-versus-event distinction from pub/sub: SendEmail is a command implying one specific handler, while OrderShipped is a past-tense fact that any number of consumers can react to. Publishing commands dressed as events recouples exactly what the architecture was meant to separate.

Exercise 3 (Medium): Implement the dedupe check. Show why the naive version fails.
Solution 
❌ NAIVE — two independent failure modes:

def send(user, event, channel):
    key = uuid4()                          # BUG 1: not deterministic
    if db.exists(key): return              # BUG 2: check is not atomic
    provider.send(user, render(event))     #        with the send
    db.insert(key)

BUG 1: a retry generates a NEW uuid, so nothing is ever deduplicated.
BUG 2: even with a stable key, a crash between send() and insert()
       causes a resend; and two concurrent retries both pass the
       existence check and both send.
✅ CORRECT — deterministic key, atomic claim, provider-side backstop:

import hashlib

def notification_id(user_id, event_id, channel, template_version):
    raw = f"{user_id}:{event_id}:{channel}:{template_version}"
    return hashlib.sha256(raw.encode()).hexdigest()[:32]

def send(user, event, channel):
    nid = notification_id(user.id, event.id, channel, TEMPLATE_VERSION)
    try:
        # Atomic claim. UNIQUE(notification_id) does the mutual exclusion.
        db.execute(
            "INSERT INTO sent_notifications(notification_id, user_id, "
            "channel, state, created_at) VALUES (%s,%s,%s,'sending',now())",
            (nid, user.id, channel))
    except UniqueViolation:
        return                              # already handled; safe no-op

    # Provider idempotency key covers the lost-response case, which our
    # own transaction cannot: we may have sent and never learned it.
    provider.send(user, render(event, user.locale), idempotency_key=nid)
    db.execute("UPDATE sent_notifications SET state='sent' "
               "WHERE notification_id=%s", (nid,))


def demo():
    # The invariant: N concurrent attempts for the same event produce
    # exactly ONE provider call.
    calls = []
    class FakeProvider:
        def send(self, *a, **k): calls.append(k['idempotency_key'])
    # (illustrative; in a real test run this across threads)
    nid1 = notification_id(1, "evt-9", "push", 3)
    nid2 = notification_id(1, "evt-9", "push", 3)
    assert nid1 == nid2, "key must be deterministic across retries"
    nid3 = notification_id(1, "evt-9", "email", 3)
    assert nid1 != nid3, "different channels must not collide"
    print("ok — deterministic, channel-scoped dedupe key")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    demo()

Why the key includes the channel: the same event legitimately produces a push and an email. Without the channel component, sending the push would suppress the email.

Why it includes the template version: if a template is corrected and the notification is deliberately resent, a new version yields a new key. It also means a template change doesn't silently collide with in-flight keys.

Why record intent before calling the provider: a crash mid-call leaves a 'sending' row you can reconcile, rather than no evidence at all — the same intent-then-act pattern as the exactly-once pipeline in pub/sub.

)

Lifecycle rules:

1. Register on every app launch, not just first install. Tokens rotate — on reinstall, OS updates, app restores from backup, and periodically by the platform. Upsert by device_id so a rotated token replaces the old one rather than adding a row.

2. Delete immediately on an invalid-token response. APNs returns Unregistered; FCM returns NotRegistered/InvalidRegistration. These are permanent — the app was uninstalled or the token is dead. Retrying them forever wastes quota, pollutes delivery metrics, and (with some providers) counts against your reputation. This is the single most commonly missed rule.

3. Prune by inactivity. Delete tokens not seen for ~6 months. Users replace phones without formally uninstalling, so dead tokens accumulate indefinitely otherwise.

4. Send to all active tokens for a user, then suppress on other devices once one acknowledges or the user reads the content — otherwise a notification stays badged on the tablet after being read on the phone.

5. Handle the shared-device case: when a user logs out, remove their token binding for that device. Otherwise the next person to use it receives the previous user's notifications — a genuine privacy incident, and one that's easy to overlook.

6. Deduplicate tokens across users. If the same token appears for two user IDs, the newer registration wins and the older is removed; the device has changed hands.

Monitoring worth having: the ratio of invalid-token responses. A sudden spike usually means a broken registration path in a new app release — a signal you'd otherwise discover from declining engagement weeks later.

Exercise 5 (Hard): A bug causes a backfill job to re-emit 6 months of events. Millions of users are about to be notified about old activity. What saves you?
Solution

This is the realistic disaster scenario, and several independent layers should each be sufficient to contain it — defence in depth, because any single one can be missing or misconfigured.

1. Deduplication (should stop it entirely). If the dedupe key is hash(user_id, event_id, channel) and the backfill re-emits the same event IDs, every notification is recognised as already-sent and becomes a no-op. This is the primary defence and the reason the key must be derived from the event rather than the delivery attempt.

Where it fails: if the backfill generates new event IDs for old activity, dedupe sees them as novel. So it can't be the only layer.

2. Event freshness check. Reject notifications for events older than a threshold — nobody needs a push about a comment from six months ago. A simple if event.created_at < now() - max_age: drop stops the entire class of incident regardless of event IDs, and it's cheap.

3. Per-user frequency caps. A hard ceiling of, say, 10 engagement notifications per user per day means even a total failure of layers 1 and 2 limits the damage to 10 messages per user instead of thousands. This is the backstop that turns a catastrophe into an annoyance.

4. Global rate limiting and anomaly detection. Alert when notification volume exceeds a multiple of the baseline. At 1B/day normally, a sudden 50× spike should page someone and automatically throttle the pipeline. Detection matters because the first three layers might all be bypassed by an unusual code path.

5. A kill switch. The ability to pause notification processing entirely, per type, within seconds. Queued messages remain queued; you triage and purge before resuming. Without this, the only response to an in-progress incident is a deploy, which is far too slow.

What I'd do during the incident: hit the kill switch first, then purge the offending messages from the queue by event-age or source, then re-enable. Afterwards, add the freshness check if missing, and — the real lesson — treat backfills as a first-class hazard: any job re-processing historical events should run with notifications explicitly disabled by default, requiring an opt-in flag to send. Most notification-spam incidents in the industry come from exactly this pattern rather than from designed behaviour.

Exercise 6 (Hard): Design delivery tracking: which notifications were sent, delivered, opened. What can you actually know?
Solution

Start with what's knowable per channel, because the honest answer is that certainty decreases sharply as you move toward "opened":

ChannelSentDeliveredOpened
Push✅ provider accepted⚠️ some platforms report; device may be off✅ if the app reports the tap
Email✅ provider accepted⚠️ via bounce/delivery webhooks, asynchronous⚠️ tracking pixel — blocked by privacy features, so systematically undercounts
SMS✅✅ carrier delivery receipts (delayed)❌ unknowable
In-app✅✅ you control it✅ you control it

The key point: "sent" means the provider accepted it, not that a human saw it. Conflating the two produces dashboards that overstate reach — and email open rates in particular became largely meaningless once mail clients began pre-fetching images, which fires the tracking pixel whether or not anyone read the message.

Design:

notification_events(notification_id, state, channel, timestamp, meta)
   states: created → queued → sent → delivered → opened → clicked
                                  ↘ failed(reason) → retrying → dead

- Append-only event log, not a mutable status column: states arrive
  out of order (a delivery webhook can land after an open event) and
  you want the full history for debugging.
- Webhooks from providers update it asynchronously.
- Aggregate into per-campaign/per-type counters; retain raw events
  for days, aggregates for years — the same tiering as click
  analytics in TinyURL.

Volume check: 1B notifications/day × several state transitions ≈ several billion events daily. Don't store these in your primary database — use a time-series or columnar store, and aggregate aggressively.

What the tracking is actually for, which should drive how much you build:

• Debugging ("did the user receive their OTP?") — needs per-notification lookup by user and time, and this is the highest-value use.
• Health monitoring — delivery-rate drops per channel signal provider problems, often before the provider's own status page.
• Token hygiene — invalid-token responses drive deletions.
• Product analytics — engagement by type, which informs digest tuning.

Privacy constraint worth raising: notification content and open tracking are personal data. Retain minimally, avoid storing message bodies in the event log, and honour deletion requests — the analytics value doesn't justify indefinite retention of what you told each user and when they read it.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • "Notification" isn't one thing — transactional, engagement and marketing share infrastructure and almost nothing else.
  • Per-channel queues and workers are the key structural decision — bulkheads around third-party providers that will fail.
  • A priority flag in a shared queue is not isolation. Critical paths need physically separate queues and warm dedicated workers.
  • Producers publish events, never channel instructions — otherwise every service learns about preferences, templates and providers.
  • Dedupe keys must be deterministic and derived from the event, with an atomic claim plus a provider-side idempotency key.
  • Digest to prevent fatigue — 50 pushes means the user disables notifications permanently, which is irreversible.
  • A hard per-user frequency cap is the backstop against buggy backfills, which cause most real spam incidents.
  • Quiet hours in the user's IANA timezone, handling midnight-crossing windows and DST — and jitter the release so windows don't end in a herd.
  • No external channel guarantees delivery. Use redundant channels for critical notifications, with in-app as the durable backstop you control.
  • Delete invalid push tokens immediately and process email bounces, or you waste quota and damage sender reputation.
  • SMS costs ~1000× push — channel selection is a cost decision as much as a delivery one.
  • Measure critical paths separately — OTP failures are invisible in an aggregate dominated by bulk traffic.
