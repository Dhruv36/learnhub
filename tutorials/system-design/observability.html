Observability Engineering Deep Dive

What you'll master: which pillar answers which question (and the cost of reaching for the wrong one), why cardinality is the constraint that decides your entire metrics design and budget, why averages are actively misleading and percentiles can't be averaged either, how sampling keeps tracing affordable without losing the traces you need, how to derive alerts from SLOs and error-budget burn rate rather than intuition, and how to build systems that are debuggable by people who didn't write them.

Prerequisites: Microservices (tracing becomes mandatory), Estimation (tail latency). Pairs with Cost, Capacity & SLO Engineering.

🎯 The distinction worth opening with: monitoring tells you whether known failure modes are occurring — you predicted them and built a dashboard. Observability is whether you can answer questions you didn't anticipate, without shipping new code. In a distributed system most outages are novel, so "we have dashboards" is not the same as "we can debug this". That gap is what this lesson is about.

PillarAnswersCost modelWeakness
Metrics"Is the system healthy right now? What's the trend?"Cheap and near-constant per series — but cost scales with cardinality, not trafficPre-aggregated, so you cannot ask a question you didn't define in advance

                                    find which service, then LOGS for why.
  "Which service is slow?"        → TRACES. Grepping logs across ten

                                    that one trace view answers instantly.
  "Why did order 12345 fail?"     → LOGS, correlated by request ID.
  "Are we breaching our SLO?"     → METRICS.

The efficient path is almost always metrics → traces → logs:
broad and cheap, then narrow and expensive.

2. Deep Dive: Cardinality — The Constraint That Decides Everything

This is the single most important practical concept in metrics, and it's what turns a modest monitoring bill into an enormous one overnight.

A time series is created for EVERY UNIQUE COMBINATION of label values.

  http_requests_total{method, status, endpoint}
     5 methods × 8 statuses × 20 endpoints = 800 series.   Fine.

  Add user_id (1,000,000 users):
     800 × 1,000,000 = 800,000,000 series.

Each series costs memory in the ingester and storage per data point.
At roughly 1-8 KB of memory per active series, 800M series is
terabytes of RAM. Your monitoring system dies — and it typically
takes the ingestion path down for EVERY other service too.

This is a "cardinality explosion", and it is the most common way
teams take down their own observability platform.
Never a metric labelWhyWhere it belongs
user ID, email, session IDUnbounded — one series per userLogs or trace attributes
request ID, trace IDUnique per request — one series eachLogs and traces (that's their purpose)
Raw URL path (/orders/12345)Unbounded — one series per resourceUse the route template: /orders/{id}
Error message textUnbounded — stack traces and interpolated valuesLog the message; label with a bounded error class
Timestamp, IP addressUnbounded by definitionLogs

The rule: metric labels must have bounded, low cardinality — ideally under a few hundred distinct values, and their product must stay manageable. High-cardinality dimensions are exactly what logs and traces exist for. This division of labour is why there are three pillars rather than one: they have fundamentally different cost models.

Modern column-oriented tools (Honeycomb, ClickHouse-backed systems) handle high cardinality far better by storing wide events rather than pre-aggregated series — which is the technical argument behind "observability" as distinct from traditional metrics. Worth knowing, but the constraint still exists; it's raised, not removed.

3. Deep Dive: Why Averages Lie (And Percentiles Don't Average)

1,000 requests: 990 take 50 ms, 10 take 5,000 ms.

   mean = (990×50 + 10×5000) / 1000 = 99.5 ms   → "looks fine"
   p99  = 5,000 ms                              → 1% of users wait 5s

The mean is a number no individual request experienced. It hides
exactly the population you care about — the unhappy one.

The subtler error, which even experienced engineers make: you cannot average percentiles.

Server A: p99 = 100 ms  (over 1,000,000 requests)
Server B: p99 = 900 ms  (over 10 requests)

  average of the p99s = 500 ms   ← MEANINGLESS

The true combined p99 is ≈ 100 ms, because B contributes almost
nothing to the population. Averaging percentiles weights a server
handling 10 requests equally with one handling a million.

Correct approach: aggregate the underlying HISTOGRAMS (bucket counts
add correctly), then compute the percentile from the merged
distribution. This is why Prometheus histograms store buckets and
compute quantiles at query time — and why dashboards that average
per-instance p99s across a fleet are silently wrong.
PercentileTells youUse for
p50 (median)The typical experienceBaseline performance tracking
p95 / p99The unhappy minoritySLOs and alerting — this is what users complain about
p99.9Extreme tailLarge systems, and fan-out services where the tail dominates
maxThe single worst caseDebugging; too noisy for alerting

Why the tail matters more than it appears: a page assembling 100 service calls hits someone's p99 with probability 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. So a "1% slow" backend makes most page loads slow. In fan-out architectures, tail latency is the user experience — which is why p99 belongs in your SLO and the mean belongs nowhere near it.

4. SLIs, SLOs, and Error Budgets

SLI — the measurement:  "99.95% of requests succeeded in the last 30 days"
SLO — the internal target: "99.9% of requests succeed"
SLA — the contractual promise (looser than the SLO, with penalties)

ERROR BUDGET = 1 − SLO, and it is a SPENDABLE RESOURCE:

  99.9% over 30 days → 0.1% × 43,200 min ≈ 43 minutes of failure allowed
  99.99%             → ~4.3 minutes
  99.999%            → ~26 seconds  (essentially no human response time —
                                     everything must be automated)

Each additional nine costs roughly 10x more to achieve, so the right
question is never "how reliable can we be?" but "what does the business
actually need, and what does it cost?"

The error budget's real function is organisational: it converts "be more careful" into a measurable resource that trades against shipping velocity. Budget healthy → ship faster, take risks, run experiments. Budget nearly exhausted → freeze risky changes and spend the effort on reliability. It ends the perpetual argument between product and operations by making the trade explicit and numeric.

Choosing the SLI matters more than the target. It must reflect what users actually experience:

Weak SLIBetter SLIWhy
Server uptimeSuccessful request rateA server can be "up" and returning 500s
Average latencyProportion of requests under 300 msAverages hide the unhappy tail
CPU utilisationQueue wait timeCPU is a cause, not a user experience
"The API is up"Per-critical-journey success (checkout, login)A healthy API with a broken checkout is a broken product

5. Deep Dive: Alerting That Doesn't Burn Out The Team

Alert on symptoms users feel, not on causes. Causes are numerous, transient and system-specific; symptoms are few and stable.

❌ Cause-based alerts 
CPU > 90% on server-7
Memory > 80%
Disk queue depth > 10
Thread pool 90% utilised

Noisy: transient spikes are
usually harmless, so people
learn to ignore them.

Incomplete: misses every
failure that DOESN'T show up
as high CPU.

Result: alert fatigue, and the
real page gets missed.
✅ Symptom-based alerts 
Error rate > 1% for 5 min
p99 latency > 2s for 10 min
Checkout success < 99%
Queue age > 15 min

Few, meaningful, and each maps
to real user impact.

Root cause (CPU? slow query?
bad deploy?) is investigated
AFTER the page, using metrics,
traces and logs.

Burn-rate alerting is the refinement that makes SLO-based paging practical. Alerting the moment the budget is touched is far too noisy; alerting only on exhaustion is far too late:

Burn rate = how fast you are consuming the error budget
            relative to spending it evenly over the window.

  burn rate 1  → the budget lasts exactly the full 30 days
  burn rate 14.4 → the entire 30-day budget is gone in ~2 days

Multi-window alerting (the Google SRE pattern):
   PAGE:   burn rate > 14.4 sustained over 1 hour   (fast burn — urgent)
   TICKET: burn rate > 3    sustained over 6 hours  (slow burn — not 3am)

Two windows (a long one for significance, a short one for freshness)
prevent both false pages from brief blips and slow leaks going
unnoticed.

Every page needs a runbook naming what it means, its user impact, first diagnostic steps and known remedies. And the discipline that keeps alerting healthy: if an alert fires and nobody acts, delete it or downgrade it. Alerts that are routinely ignored actively train people to ignore the one that matters.

6. Deep Dive: Tracing and Sampling Economics

Distributed tracing propagates a trace context through every hop:

  traceparent: 00-4bf92f3577b34da6a3ce929d0e0e4736-00f067aa0ba902b7-01
                  └─ trace ID (whole request) ──┘ └─ span ID ─┘

Each service creates a SPAN (start, end, attributes) and passes the
context onward. The backend assembles spans into a waterfall showing
exactly where time went.

The hard requirement: context must propagate EVERYWHERE — through
HTTP headers, message queues, thread pools, and async boundaries. One
service that drops it breaks every trace passing through it, which is
why retrofitting tracing is painful and instrumenting early is worth
far more than it seems.

Sampling is mandatory at scale — storing every trace at 100K req/s is unaffordable:

StrategyHowTrade-off
Head-basedDecide at the first service (e.g. keep 1%); propagate the decisionSimple and cheap, and the decision is consistent across the trace. But you discard 99% before knowing which were interesting — the failed request you need is probably gone.
Tail-basedBuffer all spans, decide after the trace completes: keep all errors, all slow traces, and a sample of the restKeeps exactly the traces you want. Costs a buffering layer that must hold every in-flight trace, and requires all spans of a trace to reach the same collector.
Adaptive / prioritySample rates per route; higher for low-traffic or high-value endpointsEnsures rare endpoints still appear. More configuration to maintain.

Tail-based sampling is what you want if you can afford it: 100% of errors and slow requests, 0.1% of successful fast ones. The traces you actually open during an incident are, almost by definition, the anomalous ones — and head-based sampling throws those away with the same probability as everything else.

7. Structured Logs and Correlation

❌ Unstructured 
log.info("User " + id +
  " failed login from " + ip)

"User 42 failed login from 1.2.3.4"

To count failures by IP you must
write a regex. Every format change
breaks every query. Aggregation
across services is near-impossible.
✅ Structured 
log.info("login_failed", {
  user_id: 42,
  source_ip: "1.2.3.4",
  reason: "bad_password",
  trace_id: ctx.trace_id })

Queryable as data:
  event=login_failed
  | count by source_ip

And trace_id joins it to the trace
and to every other service's logs.

The correlation ID is the keystone. Generate a trace ID at the edge (or adopt an incoming one), propagate it through every service call and queue message, and include it in every log line. Without it, a request touching six services produces six unrelated log streams and debugging becomes archaeology. With it, one ID retrieves the complete story.

Return the trace ID to the client in an error response. When a user reports "my order failed at 3pm", a support ticket carrying trace_id: 4bf92f35… turns an hour of searching into one query — a small implementation detail with outsized operational value.

What must never be logged: passwords, tokens, authorization headers, full card numbers, or unnecessary PII. Logs are widely readable, shipped to third-party vendors and retained for years — they're a common breach path, and redaction belongs in the logging layer rather than in each call site.

Interview Questions

🟢 Beginner — What are the three pillars, and which answers "why was this one request slow"?

Metrics are aggregated numbers over time — error rate, p99 latency, QPS. They answer "is the system healthy right now?" and are cheap to store, but pre-aggregated, so you can't ask a question you didn't define in advance.

Logs are discrete records of events. They answer "what exactly happened in this specific case?" and carry the most detail, at the highest storage cost.

Traces follow one request across all the services it touches, recording a span per hop. They answer "where did the time go?"

For "why was this one request slow" you want a trace — it shows a waterfall of every hop with its duration, so the slow segment is immediately visible. Then you'd use the logs from that service, correlated by the same trace ID, to find out why.

The efficient debugging path is metrics → traces → logs: broad and cheap first, then narrow and expensive. Grepping logs across ten services to find a bottleneck is hours of work that a single trace view answers instantly — reaching for the wrong pillar is a real cost, not just a stylistic preference.

🟢 Beginner — Why alert on error rate rather than CPU?

Because error rate is a symptom users feel and CPU is a cause that may or may not matter.

CPU alerts are noisy: transient spikes are usually harmless, so a batch job or a garbage collection cycle pages someone at 3am for a system that's working perfectly. People learn to dismiss these alerts — and then dismiss the one that mattered.

CPU alerts are also incomplete: plenty of severe failures never involve high CPU at all — a deadlock (CPU goes idle), a downstream dependency failing, an expired certificate, a full disk, a bad deploy returning 500s. Alerting on causes means enumerating every possible cause in advance, which is impossible.

Symptom alerts are few and complete: "error rate > 1%" fires regardless of which underlying cause is responsible this time. It maps directly to user impact, so if it fires, something genuinely matters; if it doesn't, users are fine.

The root-cause investigation happens after the page, using metrics, traces and logs. Resource metrics like CPU are valuable for dashboards and capacity planning — they're just poor paging criteria.

A practical refinement worth mentioning: require the condition to persist (e.g. 5 minutes) so brief blips don't page, and use burn-rate alerting so severity scales with how fast you're consuming the error budget.

🟡 Mid — Your metrics bill has tripled and the backend is falling over. Diagnose.

Almost certainly a cardinality explosion — someone added a high-cardinality label to a metric.

Metrics systems create a separate time series for every unique combination of label values. Adding one unbounded dimension multiplies series count by that dimension's size:

http_requests_total{method, status, endpoint}
   5 × 8 × 20 = 800 series ✓

...then someone adds user_id (1M users):
   800 × 1,000,000 = 800,000,000 series ✗

At ~1-8 KB of ingester memory per active series, that's terabytes of
RAM. Cost scales with CARDINALITY, not with traffic — which is why the
bill can triple without any traffic increase.

Diagnosis: most systems expose series-count-per-metric (Prometheus: topk(10, count by(__name__)({__name__=~".+"}))). Look for the metric that grew, then find the offending label. Correlate with recent deploys — this is nearly always a code change, and often a well-intentioned one ("let's break down latency by customer").

Common culprits: user or session ID; raw URL paths (/orders/12345 instead of the template /orders/{id}); error message text containing interpolated values or stack traces; IP addresses; and unbounded enum-like fields that grew over time.

Fix: drop or relabel the offending dimension at the collector (an immediate mitigation that doesn't require a deploy), then fix the instrumentation. Move the high-cardinality dimension to logs or trace attributes, which are designed for it. Then add guardrails: a series-count limit per metric, alerting on cardinality growth, and code review attention on new labels.

The principle to state: metric labels must be bounded and low-cardinality — that constraint is precisely why logs and traces exist as separate pillars with different cost models.

🟡 Mid — Explain SLIs, SLOs and error budgets, and how they change team behaviour.

SLI is the measurement — "99.95% of requests succeeded over the last 30 days". SLO is the internal target — "99.9% succeed". SLA is the contractual promise to customers, usually looser than the SLO so you have margin before penalties apply.

The error budget is 1 − SLO, expressed as allowed failure:

99.9%   over 30 days → ~43 minutes of failure allowed
99.99%               → ~4.3 minutes
99.999%              → ~26 seconds (no time for human response —
                       everything must be automated)

How it changes behaviour is the real point. Reliability stops being a vague argument ("be more careful" vs "ship faster") and becomes a quantified, spendable resource:

• Budget healthy → ship aggressively, run risky experiments, do that migration. You have room, and unspent budget is wasted opportunity.
• Budget nearly exhausted → freeze risky changes and spend engineering effort on reliability until it recovers.

This ends the perpetual product-vs-operations conflict by making the trade explicit and numeric. It also makes clear that 100% reliability is the wrong target — each additional nine costs roughly 10× more, and users typically can't distinguish 99.9% from 99.99% because their own network is less reliable than either.

Choosing the SLI matters more than the number. "Server uptime" is a weak SLI — a server can be up and returning 500s. Better SLIs measure user journeys: successful request rate, checkout completion rate, proportion of requests under 300 ms. And measure from as close to the user as possible; a backend that's healthy while the CDN is broken still means a broken product.

🔴 Senior — Design observability for a 30-service platform at 100K req/s. Address cost.

At this scale, cost is the design constraint — naive instrumentation can easily exceed the compute bill, and I'd design each pillar around its cost model.

Metrics — cheap, so keep them universal but bounded:

RED metrics per service (Rate, Errors, Duration) with STRICTLY
bounded labels: service, route TEMPLATE, method, status class.
   30 services × ~20 routes × 5 methods × 5 status classes
   ≈ 15,000 series. Trivial.

Latency as HISTOGRAMS (not summaries) so percentiles can be computed
correctly across instances by merging buckets.
Enforce a per-metric series limit and alert on cardinality growth —
one bad label is how monitoring platforms die.

Traces — sample, but sample intelligently:

100K req/s × ~10 spans × ~1 KB = 1 GB/s if unsampled. Unaffordable.

TAIL-BASED sampling:
   100% of errors
   100% of traces over the latency threshold
   0.1% of successful fast requests
→ ~1-5 MB/s stored, and it keeps exactly the traces anyone opens
  during an incident.

Requires a buffering collector layer that holds in-flight traces and
routes all spans of one trace to the same collector — real
infrastructure, and worth it. OpenTelemetry for instrumentation so
the vendor stays swappable.

Logs — the largest bill, so tier them:

100K req/s × 2 KB = 200 MB/s = 17 TB/day if everything is logged.

Tiering:
   ERROR/WARN         → 100%, hot storage, 30-day retention
   INFO (state changes)→ 100%, hot 7 days → cold object storage 90 days
   DEBUG              → sampled 0.1%, or enabled dynamically per-service
                        via a runtime flag when investigating
All structured, all carrying trace_id.

The dynamic-debug-logging capability is worth calling out: being able to raise log verbosity for one service (or one customer) at runtime, without a deploy, is enormously valuable during an incident and costs nothing when off.

Cross-cutting: trace context propagated through HTTP, queues and async boundaries — one service dropping it breaks every trace through it, so this needs shared middleware rather than per-team implementation. Trace IDs returned in error responses so support tickets are directly actionable.

Alerting: SLO-based multi-window burn-rate alerts per critical user journey, not per service. Thirty services alerting individually produces an unusable page volume; alert on the journeys users care about, then use dashboards and traces to localise.

The framing: observability spend should be budgeted as a percentage of infrastructure cost (commonly 5–15%) and reviewed like any other line item. Teams that don't set that budget discover it accidentally when the bill arrives — usually via a cardinality explosion.

🔴 Senior — p99 latency doubled but no single service's metrics changed. How do you investigate?

This pattern — aggregate degradation with no obvious per-service culprit — has a few characteristic causes, and it's a good test of whether someone has actually debugged distributed systems.

1. Tail amplification from fan-out (most likely). If a request touches many services, the composite p99 is driven by each service's tail, not its median. A modest tail increase across several services compounds:

10 services, each p99 = 100 ms:
   P(at least one hits its p99) = 1 − 0.99¹⁰ ≈ 10%

Each service's OWN p99 dashboard can look nearly unchanged while the
end-to-end p99 doubles, because you're sampling the tail ten times.

Investigate with traces, not metrics — compare slow traces against fast ones and look at where the extra time sits. It's often spread thinly rather than concentrated.

2. A shared dependency that isn't in the service list. A database, cache, DNS resolver, auth service or service mesh sidecar degrading affects everything slightly. Check infrastructure metrics and the p99 of shared components specifically.

3. A changed traffic mix. No service got slower; the distribution of requests changed — more expensive queries, larger payloads, a new client hitting a costlier endpoint, or a big customer onboarding. Break latency down by route, client and tenant. This is very common and looks mysterious until you segment.

4. Percentile aggregation error. Verify the dashboard isn't averaging per-instance p99s, which is mathematically invalid and can shift when instance counts or traffic balance change. Recompute from merged histograms.

5. Resource saturation below the alert threshold. Queueing latency is non-linear — utilisation rising from 60% to 75% can double latency while every dashboard still shows "healthy". Check utilisation trends, connection pool waits and queue depths, not just their alert states.

6. Client-side or network changes — a new region, mobile traffic share rising, TLS renegotiation, or a CDN misconfiguration. Compare server-measured latency against client-measured (RUM) latency; a divergence localises it outside your services.

Method: start from traces rather than metrics, because this is exactly the question metrics can't answer — pre-aggregated series lost the per-request structure. Pull a sample of slow traces and a sample of fast ones from the same period, and diff them. That comparison usually identifies the cause within minutes, and it's the reason tail-based sampling (keeping slow traces) is worth its cost.

🏋️ Graded Exercises

Exercise 1 (Easy): 990 requests take 50 ms; 10 take 5,000 ms. Compute the mean and p99, and say which you'd put in an SLO.
Solution 
mean = (990 × 50 + 10 × 5000) / 1000
     = (49,500 + 50,000) / 1000 = 99.5 ms

p99  = the 990th value when sorted = 50 ms
p99.9 = 5,000 ms      (the slowest 0.1%)

Careful with the boundary: with exactly 1% slow requests, the p99 sits right at the transition. The instructive figures are that the mean of 99.5 ms is a value no single request experienced — 99% took 50 ms and 1% took 5 seconds — and that the extreme tail is 100× the typical case.

For the SLO, use a percentile — and preferably express it as a threshold rather than a percentile value: "99% of requests complete under 300 ms". That form is unambiguous, aggregates correctly across instances (it's a ratio of counts), and states the user promise directly.

Never put the mean in an SLO. It can stay comfortably green while a growing minority of users experiences a broken product, which is precisely the failure mode SLOs exist to catch.

Exercise 2 (Easy): Which of these are safe as metric labels: HTTP method, user ID, status code, URL path, region, error message?
Solution
LabelSafe?Reason
HTTP method✅ Safe~7 values, fixed
User ID❌ NeverUnbounded — one series per user. Belongs in logs/traces
Status code✅ SafeBounded; better still, group into classes (2xx/4xx/5xx)

The test to apply: "how many distinct values can this take, at our largest customer, in a year?" If the answer is unbounded or grows with users/requests/resources, it is not a metric label. Total series is the product of all label cardinalities, so one bad label multiplies everything.

Exercise 3 (Medium): Design the alerting for a checkout service: SLO 99.9% success, p99 under 500 ms. Specify what pages and what doesn't.
Solution

SLIs: checkout success rate (completed / attempted) and checkout latency p99, measured as close to the user as possible — ideally including the CDN and gateway, since a healthy backend behind a broken edge is still a broken checkout.

Error budget: 99.9% over 30 days → ~43 minutes of failure allowed.

PAGE (wake someone up) — fast burn:
  • burn rate > 14.4 over 1h AND > 14.4 over 5m
      (budget exhausted in ~2 days; the short window confirms it's
       still happening, the long window confirms it's significant)
  • checkout success < 95% for 5 minutes  (catastrophic, page regardless)
  • checkout requests = 0 for 5 minutes during business hours
      (the "is it even receiving traffic?" check people forget —
       a total outage can look like perfect success rate)

TICKET (next business day) — slow burn:
  • burn rate > 3 over 6h AND > 3 over 30m
      (a leak that will exhaust the budget in ~10 days)
  • p99 latency > 500 ms for 30 minutes
  • payment provider error rate elevated but below SLO impact

DASHBOARD ONLY (never alerts):
  • CPU, memory, disk, thread pools, GC — causes, not symptoms
  • individual pod restarts
  • per-service latency (used for diagnosis after a page)

Why two windows per alert: the long window establishes that the burn is significant; the short window confirms it's still occurring, so the alert resolves promptly once fixed rather than holding someone on a resolved incident.

Why the zero-traffic check matters: if checkout stops receiving requests entirely, success rate is either 100% or undefined — every ratio-based alert stays green through a total outage. This is a classic gap.

Each page needs a runbook with user impact, first diagnostics, known causes and escalation. And review alert history monthly: any alert that fired without action gets deleted or downgraded, because ignored alerts train people to ignore the important one.

Exercise 4 (Medium): Your tracing bill is unaffordable at 1% head-based sampling, yet you rarely have a trace for the errors you investigate. Redesign.
Solution

The diagnosis: head-based sampling decides at the first service, before anything is known about the request. So errors are kept at the same 1% rate as everything else — meaning 99% of the traces you most want are discarded. You're paying for volume and getting poor coverage of the interesting cases, which is the worst combination.

Redesign — tail-based sampling:

Buffer spans at a collector until the trace completes, then decide:

   keep 100%  of traces containing an error
   keep 100%  of traces exceeding the latency threshold (e.g. p99)
   keep 100%  of traces for low-traffic / high-value routes
   keep 0.1%  of successful fast traces (baseline for comparison)

Result: LOWER total volume than 1% head-based (because the vast
majority of traffic is fast and successful, sampled at 0.1%), while
retaining essentially every trace anyone would actually open.

What it costs: a collector tier that buffers in-flight traces (memory proportional to request rate × trace duration), and a routing requirement that all spans of one trace reach the same collector — typically consistent hashing on trace ID at the load balancer. That's real infrastructure, and it's the reason tail-based sampling isn't the default everywhere.

Intermediate options if that's too much:

• Head-based with priority propagation — the edge service marks known-interesting requests (a debug header, a specific customer, a canary deployment) for 100% sampling, propagating the decision. Cheap, and covers the "reproduce this customer's problem" case.
• Per-route rates — 100% on checkout, 0.01% on health checks. Rare endpoints stay visible while high-volume noise is cut.
• Keep the baseline low but retain error logs at 100% with trace IDs, so even without the trace you can reconstruct the path.

The principle: sample by value, not uniformly. Uniform sampling optimises for statistical representativeness, but debugging needs the anomalies — and those are exactly what uniform sampling discards.

Exercise 5 (Hard): A user reports "the site was broken at 2:15pm" but all dashboards were green. Design the investigation and the observability gaps it reveals.
Solution

Green dashboards plus a real user problem means your measurements don't reflect user experience. That's the finding, and it's more valuable than the individual incident.

Investigation:

1. Get identifying detail — user ID, approximate time, page, and ideally a trace ID from an error response. Query logs for that user around 14:15.
2. Segment aggressively. Global metrics average away localised failure. Break error rate and latency down by region, client version, browser, device type, CDN PoP, and customer tier. A failure affecting 2% of users in one region is invisible globally and total for them.
3. Check what you don't measure — the gaps below.

Likely gaps this reveals:

• No client-side (RUM) monitoring. The most common cause. Server metrics are green because the failure was in the browser — a JavaScript error, a failed third-party script, a broken asset deploy, a CORS or CSP misconfiguration. The server returned 200 for everything, and the page didn't work. Fix: real user monitoring capturing JS errors, Core Web Vitals and failed resource loads.

• Averaging over segments. One region, one CDN PoP or one client version failing entirely moves the global average imperceptibly. Fix: SLOs computed per significant segment, and alerting on segment-level breaches.

• No synthetic monitoring. If traffic to a broken path is low, nothing shows up. Fix: scripted checks running the critical journeys continuously from multiple locations — these also catch failures during low-traffic periods when ratio-based alerts are statistically meaningless.

• Measuring at the wrong boundary. If SLIs are measured at the application server, then CDN, load balancer, DNS and TLS failures are all invisible. Fix: measure as close to the user as possible.

• Short retention or coarse resolution. A two-minute outage disappears in five-minute aggregation buckets. Fix: retain high-resolution recent data.

The process improvement that matters most: treat "user reported an issue we didn't detect" as its own incident class with a required follow-up — what would have caught this, and are we adding it? Detection gaps compound silently, and user reports are the only signal you have that your observability is incomplete. A team that closes each gap converges on genuinely trustworthy dashboards; a team that doesn't keeps being surprised.

Exercise 6 (Hard): Design observability for an async pipeline: API → queue → workers → database, where failures are invisible to the user until much later.
Solution

Async pipelines break the assumptions behind normal observability: there's no request/ response to measure, the user has already received a 202, and a failure surfaces hours later as "my report never arrived". Success has to be measured differently.

1. Propagate trace context through the queue. This is the piece most implementations miss — trace headers must be serialised into the message, then restored by the worker, so the producer's span and the consumer's span belong to the same trace. Without it, tracing stops at the queue boundary and the two halves of the pipeline can never be correlated.

2. Measure the metrics that only exist in async systems:

queue_depth                 — backlog size
oldest_message_age  ← THE most important one. Depth can look fine
                      while one partition is stalled; message age
                      cannot hide it.
consumer_lag per partition  — aggregate lag hides a single stuck consumer
processing_duration         — per message type
retry_count / DLQ_depth     — any DLQ message should alert
end_to_end_latency          — enqueue timestamp → completion.
                              This is the ONE the user actually feels.

3. Alert on age and DLQ, not depth. A deep queue that's draining quickly is fine; a shallow queue where the oldest message is 30 minutes old means something is stuck. And any DLQ arrival should raise a ticket — an unmonitored DLQ is just a slower silent drop.

4. Track completion, not just submission. The critical async failure is work that's accepted and never completes — no error is ever raised, so error-rate alerting shows nothing:

Record every job with a state machine: submitted → processing →
completed | failed, with timestamps.

Then alert on the reconciliation question:
   "jobs submitted > 1 hour ago still not in a terminal state"

This is the async equivalent of an error rate, and it catches the
silent-loss failures that no per-message metric will.

5. Make the SLO end-to-end and user-facing: "99% of reports are delivered within 10 minutes of request" — not "the worker processed 99% of messages successfully", which can be true while messages are lost before ever reaching the worker.

6. Close the loop to the user. Since failure is invisible to them, the system must actively surface status: a job-status endpoint, a notification on failure, and a trace/job ID in the original 202 response so support can investigate a specific submission.

The framing: in synchronous systems, failure announces itself with an error response. In async systems, the most dangerous failure is silence — work accepted and quietly dropped. So observability must be built around reconciliation (what was promised vs what completed) rather than around error rates, which is a genuinely different design posture and the reason async pipelines need deliberate instrumentation rather than the defaults.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Monitoring answers known questions; observability answers unanticipated ones. Most outages are novel, so dashboards alone aren't enough.
  • Metrics → traces → logs is the efficient debugging path: broad and cheap first, narrow and expensive last.
  • Cardinality is the constraint that decides your metrics design. One unbounded label multiplies series count and can take down the whole platform.
  • Never label metrics with user IDs, request IDs, raw paths or error text — those belong in logs and traces, which is precisely why three pillars exist.
  • Averages hide the unhappy tail, and percentiles cannot be averaged — aggregate histograms and compute the quantile from the merged distribution.
  • In fan-out architectures the tail is the experience: 100 calls at p99=1% means ~63% of pages hit someone's tail.
  • Alert on symptoms users feel, not causes. Cause-based alerts are noisy and incomplete.
  • Use multi-window burn-rate alerts: page on fast burn, ticket on slow burn.
  • Delete alerts nobody acts on — they train the team to ignore the one that matters.
  • Sample traces by value, not uniformly. Tail-based sampling keeps every error and slow trace while costing less than 1% head-based.
  • Propagate a trace ID everywhere — including through queues and async boundaries — and return it in error responses so support tickets are actionable.
  • Async pipelines need reconciliation, not error rates: the dangerous failure is silence — work accepted and never completed.
  • Instrument from day one. Context propagation is very hard to retrofit across many services.
