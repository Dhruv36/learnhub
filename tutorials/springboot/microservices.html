Resilience & Microservices Production

What you'll master: the mechanism by which a healthy service is killed by a sick one — a thread blocked on a remote call is a thread that cannot serve anyone, and the arithmetic that turned a dependency slowing from 40 ms to 8 s into a complete outage in 90 seconds while CPU sat at 6%; why the default timeout in most Spring HTTP clients is infinite, and the three timeouts you must set rather than one; why a read timeout does not mean the work did not happen — the most expensive misunderstanding in retry logic, which double-charged 1,143 customers; how retries multiply through a call chain, where three layers of "just retry 3 times" became 27× load on a service that was already failing; circuit breakers as they actually behave, including the two defaults that mean your breaker will never open and the slow-call threshold that is the only one that would have helped; bulkheads, and why one shared connection pool makes every dependency a single point of failure; idempotency keys that must store the response, not the fact of having seen a request; the dual-write problem and the outbox that fixes it; sagas, and why a compensation is not a rollback; and the availability arithmetic that explains why twelve 99.9% dependencies in a row is a 98.8% service. Prerequisites: REST Controllers, Transactions and Actuator & Observability.

Part 1 — The Timeout Is the Whole Lesson

  AN IN-PROCESS CALL AND A REMOTE CALL ARE NOT THE SAME KIND OF THING

    orderService.price(cart)      returns, throws, or the JVM dies.
                                  There is no fourth outcome.

    paymentClient.charge(order)   returns, throws, hangs FOREVER,
                                  or SUCCEEDS AND YOU NEVER FIND OUT.

  The last two have no local equivalent, and every pattern in this
  lesson exists because of them.

  THE ONE THAT KILLS SERVICES IS "HANGS":
    a blocked thread is not free. It holds a Tomcat worker, its
    stack, its transaction, and often a database connection --
    while doing nothing at all.

MEASURED: a dependency got slow and a healthy service died in 90 s

  Order service: Tomcat max-threads 200, ~250 req/s steady.
  Every order calls the pricing service. No timeout configured.

  BEFORE      pricing p99 = 40 ms
              threads in use: 250 req/s x 0.04 s = 10
              CPU 18%, everything fine.

  T+0         pricing deploys a bad query; p99 goes to 8 s.
  T+10s       threads needed: 250 x 8 = 2000, threads available 200
  T+12s       all 200 Tomcat threads blocked in socketRead0
  T+15s       accept queue (acceptCount 100) full
  T+18s       new connections REFUSED -- including /actuator/health
  T+25s       readiness fails; pods pulled from the load balancer
  T+40s       the pods still in rotation take all traffic and die
  T+90s       0 of 24 pods serving. Total outage.

  DURING THE OUTAGE:
    CPU               6%
    heap              41% used
    GC                nothing unusual
    error rate        0% -- until connections got refused
    thread dump       200 threads, all in SocketInputStream.read

  The service was never broken. It was FULL.

  Little's Law is the whole explanation:
      threads needed = arrival rate x service time
      250/s x 8 s = 2000
  Latency multiplied by 200, so concurrency demand multiplied by
  200. Nothing else had to go wrong.
🔑 Your capacity is not measured in requests; it is measured in request-seconds. A dependency getting 200× slower consumes 200× your thread pool at the same request rate. That is why "we have plenty of CPU" is irrelevant during this failure, and why the fix is not a bigger machine — a bigger thread pool only moves the wall. The fix is a bound on how long one request may hold a thread, which is what a timeout is.
  THE DEFAULTS ARE INFINITE. ALL OF THEM.

    RestTemplate (no config)          connect: infinite  read: infinite
    RestClient   (no config)          connect: infinite  read: infinite
    WebClient / Netty                 connect: 30 s      read: infinite
    JDK HttpClient                    connect: infinite  read: infinite
    Feign (no config)                 connect: 10 s      read: 60 s

  "It has always worked" is not evidence of a timeout. It is
  evidence that the dependency has not hung yet.

  THREE TIMEOUTS, NOT ONE -- and the third is the one people miss

  1. CONNECT TIMEOUT   how long to wait for the TCP handshake.
                       Short: 1-2 s. A healthy host answers in ms.

  2. READ TIMEOUT      how long to wait for response bytes after
                       the request is sent. Your real latency
                       budget for the dependency.

  3. POOL ACQUISITION  how long to wait for a CONNECTION FROM THE
     TIMEOUT           POOL before the call even starts. Default
                       in Apache HttpClient: INFINITE.

  Miss #3 and you have rebuilt the original bug one level down:
  100 connections busy for 2 s each, 250 req/s arriving, and every
  thread now blocks waiting for a connection instead of waiting for
  a response. The read timeout never fires, because the read never
  starts.

  SETTING THEM -- RestClient on Apache HttpClient 5

    @Bean
    RestClient pricingClient(RestClient.Builder builder) {
        var cm = PoolingHttpClientConnectionManagerBuilder.create()
            .setMaxConnTotal(100)
            .setMaxConnPerRoute(100)          // default is 5. FIVE.
            .setDefaultConnectionConfig(ConnectionConfig.custom()
                .setConnectTimeout(Timeout.ofSeconds(2))
                .setSocketTimeout(Timeout.ofSeconds(2))
                .build())
            .build();

        var rc = RequestConfig.custom()
            .setConnectionRequestTimeout(Timeout.ofMillis(200))  // #3
            .setResponseTimeout(Timeout.ofSeconds(2))
            .build();

        var http = HttpClients.custom()
            .setConnectionManager(cm)
            .setDefaultRequestConfig(rc)
            .build();

        return builder
            .requestFactory(new HttpComponentsClientHttpRequestFactory(http))
            .baseUrl("http://pricing")
            .build();
    }

  NOTE maxConnPerRoute. The Apache default is 5 connections PER
  ROUTE. A service calling one downstream host is capped at 5
  concurrent calls no matter how many threads it has -- a bulkhead
  you did not choose, sized by a library default.

  THE TIMEOUT BUDGET -- a number you derive, not guess

  Your own SLA is the ceiling. Everything downstream fits inside it.

    checkout p99 target                     1500 ms
      - local work, serialisation            100 ms
      = budget for remote calls             1400 ms

    pricing    (required, sequential)        400 ms
    inventory  (required, sequential)        400 ms
    promotions (optional, parallel)          300 ms
    -------------------------------------------------
    worst case on the critical path          800 ms + slack

  SET THE TIMEOUT AT ~p99.9 OF THE DEPENDENCY, NOT AT YOUR PATIENCE.

    pricing p50  12 ms
    pricing p99  38 ms
    pricing p999 210 ms   -> 400 ms is generous
                             2 s is 10x too slow to notice trouble
                             50 ms fails 1% of GOOD requests

  A TIMEOUT LONGER THAN YOUR OWN SLA IS NOT A TIMEOUT. If checkout
  must answer in 1.5 s, a 30 s timeout on pricing means the client
  gave up 28.5 seconds ago and you are still holding a thread for a
  response nobody will read.
⚠️ Deadline propagation is the version of this that scales. Fixed per-hop timeouts restart the clock at every hop, so a 4-hop chain of "2 s each" has an 8-second worst case even though the caller gave up at 1.5 s. Pass the remaining budget down (a header such as X-Deadline-Ms, or gRPC deadlines natively), have each hop bound its own calls by what is left, and reject immediately when the budget is already spent. Spring does not do this for you; a ClientHttpRequestInterceptor reading a request-scoped deadline is about forty lines, and it is the difference between a chain that fails fast and one that fails eventually.

Part 2 — Retries Multiply, and a Timeout Is Not a Rollback

  THE ASSUMPTION THAT COSTS THE MOST MONEY

    POST /charge  --------------------------->  gateway
                                                charge SUCCEEDS
                                                committed, money moved
    <---------  (response lost / read timeout)

    caller: "timed out, must have failed, retry"
    POST /charge  --------------------------->  charged AGAIN

  A read timeout tells you ONE thing: you did not get a response.
  It tells you NOTHING about whether the work happened.

MEASURED: 1,143 customers charged twice

  Payment gateway had a 40-second latency spike. Read timeout 5 s,
  max-attempts 3, no idempotency key.

    calls that timed out but had SUCCEEDED server-side   1,143
    of those, retried and succeeded a second time        1,143
    duplicate charges                                    1,143
    refunds issued over the following 6 days             1,143
    chargebacks that beat the refunds                       37
    engineer-days spent reconciling                          9

  The retry logic worked exactly as written. The bug was believing
  a timeout meant "did not happen".
FailureSafe to retry?Why
Connection refused / DNS failureYesThe request never reached the server, so nothing happened
Connect timeoutYesNo TCP connection was established, so no request was sent
503 / 429 with Retry-AfterYesThe server is explicitly saying it did not process this
Read timeoutOnly if idempotentThe request was sent and may have been fully processed. This is the dangerous one
500 Internal Server ErrorOnly if idempotentA 500 can be thrown after a commit — it does not mean "no effect"
400 / 422 validation errorNoDeterministic. The same request will fail identically forever
401 / 403NoRetrying an authorisation failure is how an account gets locked
404NoUnless you are polling for something being created — and that is a poll with its own budget, not a retry
  RETRY AMPLIFICATION -- the number nobody computes

  Three layers, each "sensibly" retrying 3 times:

    gateway         -> 3 attempts
      order service -> 3 per gateway attempt          = 9
        pricing     -> 3 per order attempt            = 27

  27 requests hit pricing for ONE user action.

MEASURED: a retry storm

  pricing degrades: 6% of requests failing, p99 1.4 s.
  Normal load 250 req/s.

    T+0    250 req/s, 6% failing
    T+30s  clients retry -> 250 + retries of the failures
           failure rate rises because pricing is now busier
    T+2m   effective load 690 req/s (2.8x), failure rate 34%
    T+4m   effective load 810 req/s, failure rate 71%
           pricing is spending most of its capacity on requests
           whose callers HAVE ALREADY GIVEN UP

  Retries turned a partial degradation into a total one. The
  dependency did not recover until traffic was shed at the gateway.

  THE PROPERTY THAT MAKES THIS SO BAD: retry load is highest
  exactly when the dependency has least capacity. The feedback
  loop is positive.

  WHAT ACTUALLY MAKES RETRIES SAFE

  1. EXPONENTIAL BACKOFF WITH JITTER
     Without jitter every client that failed at T retries at
     T+200ms TOGETHER -- a synchronised thundering herd.

       resilience4j.retry.instances.pricing:
         max-attempts: 3
         wait-duration: 200ms
         enable-exponential-backoff: true
         exponential-backoff-multiplier: 2
         enable-randomized-wait: true         # the important one
         randomized-wait-factor: 0.5

     Full jitter -- sleep = random(0, backoff) -- spreads the herd
     completely and is the version worth defaulting to.

  2. A RETRY BUDGET -- cap retries as a FRACTION of traffic
     "Retry up to 3 times" is per-request and has no global bound.
     "Retries may not exceed 10% of requests" is a system property.
     Resilience4j has no built-in budget; a RateLimiter on the
     retry path, or a shared counter, gets you there.

  3. RETRY AT ONE LAYER ONLY -- usually the one closest to the
     dependency. Everyone else fails fast. 3 attempts at one layer
     is 3x load; 3 at three layers is 27x.

  4. AN IDEMPOTENCY KEY on anything that mutates state (Part 5).
🔑 Retry is the only resilience pattern that adds load to a system that is already failing. Timeouts, breakers and bulkheads all subtract. That asymmetry is why a retry deserves a budget, a backoff and a justification, while a timeout deserves only a number. If you are adding retries to a call you have not made idempotent, you are choosing duplicate side effects over transient failures — sometimes correct, never accidental.

Part 3 — Circuit Breakers, and the Defaults That Stop Yours Working

  THE STATE MACHINE

    CLOSED ---- failure rate over threshold ----> OPEN
      ^                                            |
      |                                    wait-duration elapses
      |                                            v
      +--- trial calls succeed ---- HALF_OPEN <----+
                                        |
                            trial calls fail -> OPEN again

  CLOSED     calls pass through; outcomes recorded in a sliding
             window.
  OPEN       calls are REJECTED IMMEDIATELY. No thread held, no
             connection used, no timeout waited out --
             CallNotPermittedException in microseconds.
  HALF_OPEN  a fixed small number of trial calls is admitted.

  WHAT IT BUYS, precisely:
    1. Your threads stop being consumed by a dependency that is
       going to fail anyway (the Part 1 failure, prevented).
    2. The dependency stops being hammered while it recovers.
    3. Failure becomes FAST, so callers get an answer inside their
       own SLA instead of after a full timeout.

  THE TWO DEFAULTS THAT MEAN YOUR BREAKER NEVER OPENS

  resilience4j defaults:
      slidingWindowSize        100
      minimumNumberOfCalls     100      <-- (1)
      failureRateThreshold     50%      <-- (2)

  (1) The breaker decides nothing until it has seen 100 calls. An
      endpoint doing 3 requests a minute takes 33 MINUTES to
      accumulate a decision. During an incident it simply never
      opens.

  (2) 50% means half of all traffic must be failing. A dependency
      failing 30% of requests -- catastrophic for users -- keeps
      the breaker closed forever.

  AND THE THIRD, WHICH IS WORSE:
      A SLOW DEPENDENCY THAT RETURNS 200 IS NOT A FAILURE.

      pricing responds in 9 s, HTTP 200, correct body.
      failure rate: 0%. Breaker: CLOSED, and it stays closed while
      your threads are consumed exactly as in Part 1.

      The breaker you needed was:
        slow-call-duration-threshold: 1s
        slow-call-rate-threshold: 50     # 50% of calls over 1s
                                         # trips the breaker

  SLOW-CALL RATE IS THE MOST UNDER-USED SETTING IN RESILIENCE4J,
  and it addresses the failure that actually happens most often:
  not down, just slow.

  A CONFIGURATION THAT WORKS

    resilience4j:
      circuitbreaker:
        configs:
          default:
            sliding-window-type: TIME_BASED
            sliding-window-size: 30            # 30 seconds
            minimum-number-of-calls: 10        # not 100
            failure-rate-threshold: 40
            slow-call-duration-threshold: 1s
            slow-call-rate-threshold: 40
            wait-duration-in-open-state: 10s
            permitted-number-of-calls-in-half-open-state: 5
            automatic-transition-from-open-to-half-open-enabled: true
            record-exceptions:
              - java.io.IOException
              - java.util.concurrent.TimeoutException
              - org.springframework.web.client.HttpServerErrorException
            ignore-exceptions:
              - com.acme.order.PricingValidationException
        instances:
          pricing:
            base-config: default
          payments:
            base-config: default
            failure-rate-threshold: 25         # stricter: money

  TIME_BASED vs COUNT_BASED matters more than it looks. A
  COUNT_BASED window of 100 on a low-traffic endpoint can still
  hold outcomes from an hour ago; a TIME_BASED window always
  describes the recent past, which is the question you are asking.

  record-exceptions / ignore-exceptions is the other half of
  correctness: a 400 from validation is not the dependency
  failing, and counting it opens the breaker for EVERYONE because
  one caller sent bad input.
⚠️ Breaker state is per JVM. Twenty-four pods have twenty-four independent breakers, each learning from its own traffic. That is usually what you want — a pod in a bad AZ should open without dragging the fleet — but it has two consequences: the dependency receives up to 24 probe calls per half-open cycle, and a low-traffic endpoint spread over 24 pods may never give any single pod enough calls to reach minimum-number-of-calls. Both push you towards time-based windows and low minimums.
  FALLBACKS: WHAT A GOOD ONE LOOKS LIKE

    @CircuitBreaker(name = "pricing", fallbackMethod = "cachedPrice")
    public Price price(Cart cart) { return pricingClient.price(cart); }

    private Price cachedPrice(Cart cart, Throwable t) {
        meterRegistry.counter("pricing.fallback",
                              "reason", t.getClass().getSimpleName())
                     .increment();
        return priceCache.lastKnown(cart)          // stale, honest
                 .orElseThrow(() -> new PricingUnavailableException());
    }

  THE FALLBACK METHOD MUST have the same signature plus a
  Throwable, the same return type, and live in the same class --
  and the call must arrive THROUGH THE PROXY. Calling price() from
  another method of the same bean bypasses the proxy entirely and
  no breaker runs at all (see AOP & Proxies).

  GOOD FALLBACKS                      BAD FALLBACKS
  ---------------------------------   ------------------------------
  stale cached data, marked stale     return null
  a degraded response (no             swallow the exception and
    recommendations shown)              return an empty list
  queue the work, return 202          return a DEFAULT PRICE
  503 + Retry-After, fast             log.error and carry on as if
  a documented partial response         nothing happened

  THE TEST: would you be comfortable if this ran for four hours
  without anyone noticing? "Returned an empty cart" fails that
  test. "Served a price 40 seconds stale, and said so" passes.

Part 4 — Bulkheads: One Pool Makes Every Dependency Fatal

  THE SHARED-RESOURCE PROBLEM

    Tomcat threads: 200, shared by EVERY endpoint.

    /orders     -> pricing, inventory, payments
    /catalog    -> nothing remote, pure DB
    /health     -> nothing at all

    pricing hangs -> /orders consumes all 200 threads
                  -> /catalog, which never touches pricing,
                     cannot get a thread either
                  -> /health cannot answer, so the pod is killed

  A dependency used by ONE endpoint took down ALL endpoints,
  because the thread pool is the shared resource and nothing
  partitioned it.

  A BULKHEAD PARTITIONS THE RESOURCE so a failure is contained to
  the compartment that owns it. The name is from ships: a breach
  floods one compartment, not the hull.

  TWO KINDS IN RESILIENCE4J, AND THEY ARE NOT INTERCHANGEABLE

  SEMAPHORE BULKHEAD  (default, cheap)
    Caps CONCURRENT calls, on the CALLER's thread.

      resilience4j.bulkhead.instances.pricing:
        max-concurrent-calls: 20
        max-wait-duration: 50ms

    - no extra threads, no context switch, no context loss
    - the caller still blocks for the call duration while holding
      its Tomcat thread
    - protects the DEPENDENCY and caps the damage, but does not
      free the calling thread

  THREAD-POOL BULKHEAD  (expensive, stronger)
    Runs the call on a SEPARATE pool; caller gets a Future.

      resilience4j.thread-pool-bulkhead.instances.pricing:
        core-thread-pool-size: 20
        max-thread-pool-size: 20
        queue-capacity: 50

    - frees the Tomcat thread only if you actually go async
    - ThreadLocal context (SecurityContext, MDC traceId, the
      transaction, RequestAttributes) DOES NOT PROPAGATE unless
      you configure it. This is the number one surprise.
    - only applies to methods returning CompletableFuture

  WHICH TO USE: semaphore for nearly everything. Thread-pool only
  when you genuinely must release the calling thread -- and then
  budget for the context-propagation work.

  SIZING A BULKHEAD -- Little's Law again, not a guess

    pricing p99 = 40 ms, peak rate to pricing = 250 req/s
    concurrency = 250 x 0.040 = 10 in flight

    max-concurrent-calls: 20      # 2x headroom
    max-wait-duration:    50ms    # fail fast when full

  WHAT IT BUYS AT 8 s LATENCY (the Part 1 scenario):
    without: 200 Tomcat threads consumed, whole service down
    with:    20 threads consumed on pricing, 180 still serving
             /catalog, /health and every endpoint that does not
             need pricing. /orders fails fast with 503.

  THE CONNECTION POOL IS A BULKHEAD TOO -- often the one that
  matters most:

    ONE RestClient/pool shared by pricing + inventory + payments
      -> pricing hangs, holds all 100 connections
      -> payments calls block waiting for a connection
      -> "payments is down" (it is not)

    ONE POOL PER DOWNSTREAM. Always. It costs a few beans, and it
    is the difference between one broken dependency and three.
🔑 Decorator order changes the meaning, and Resilience4j has a fixed one when you stack annotations: Retry(CircuitBreaker(RateLimiter(TimeLimiter(Bulkhead(call))))). Retry is outermost, which is what you want — each attempt is separately bounded by the time limiter and separately counted by the breaker, and once the breaker is open the retry sees CallNotPermittedException immediately instead of hammering a dead dependency. Invert it, so the breaker wraps the retry, and one logical call records a single outcome after three attempts: the breaker sees a third of the failures it should, and opens far too late.

Part 5 — Idempotency, the Dual Write, and Sagas

  THE MOMENT A WORKFLOW CROSSES A SERVICE BOUNDARY, THE DATABASE
  TRANSACTION IS GONE.

    @Transactional
    void placeOrder(Order o) {
        orderRepo.save(o);              // your database  -- rolls back
        paymentClient.charge(o);        // ANOTHER SERVICE -- does not
        shippingClient.book(o);         // ANOTHER SERVICE -- does not
    }

  If shipping fails, the rollback undoes the order row and leaves
  the CHARGE IN PLACE. You have taken money for an order that no
  longer exists, and no log line says so.

  Worse: holding a transaction open across two remote calls pins a
  database connection for the whole 800 ms (see Transactions), so
  this code fails the connection pool as well.

  TWO PATTERNS REPLACE THE TRANSACTION:
    IDEMPOTENCY   makes "did it happen?" answerable and retries safe
    SAGA          makes a multi-step workflow undoable, semantically

  IDEMPOTENCY DONE PROPERLY -- store the RESPONSE, not a flag

  The naive version:

    if (seenKeys.contains(key)) return;        // WRONG
    charge(order);
    seenKeys.add(key);

  Three defects: the caller gets no result on the duplicate, the
  window between charge() and add() loses the record on a crash,
  and two concurrent requests both pass the contains() check.

  THE VERSION THAT WORKS -- one table, one unique constraint

    create table idempotency (
      key           varchar(64)  primary key,
      request_hash  varchar(64)  not null,
      status        varchar(16)  not null,   -- IN_PROGRESS|DONE
      response_body text,
      status_code   int,
      created_at    timestamptz  not null default now()
    );

    @Transactional
    public Receipt charge(String key, ChargeRequest req) {
        var hash = sha256(req);
        try {
            idem.insertInProgress(key, hash);       // unique key
        } catch (DuplicateKeyException e) {
            var prior = idem.get(key);
            if (!prior.requestHash().equals(hash))
                throw new IdempotencyKeyReusedException();   // 422
            if (prior.status() == IN_PROGRESS)
                throw new RequestInFlightException();        // 409
            return deserialize(prior.responseBody());        // replay
        }
        Receipt r = gateway.charge(req);
        idem.complete(key, 200, serialize(r));
        return r;
    }

  WHAT EACH PIECE IS FOR:
    unique constraint   the DATABASE resolves the concurrent race,
                        not application logic
    request_hash        the same key with a DIFFERENT body is a
                        client bug -- reject it, do not replay a
                        response for a request nobody made
    IN_PROGRESS         a concurrent duplicate gets 409 rather than
                        a second charge
    response_body       a retry gets THE SAME ANSWER, which is what
                        idempotency actually means
    created_at          for the TTL job; keys are not kept forever
                        (24h-7d is typical, and it must exceed the
                        longest client retry window)
OperationIdempotent as written?How to make it so
GET /orders/42YesNothing to do — reads always are
PUT /orders/42 {status:PAID}YesSetting a value to the same value twice is one effect. Add a version for lost-update safety
DELETE /orders/42YesThe second delete is a no-op; return 204 either way
POST /ordersNoClient-supplied Idempotency-Key header + the table above
POST /accounts/1/credit {amount:10}No — the dangerous shapeA relative change can never be made idempotent by inspection. Needs a key, or restate it as an absolute value plus a version
Kafka consumer applying an eventNoAt-least-once delivery guarantees duplicates. Dedupe on event id, or make the write naturally idempotent (see Messaging & Kafka)
Sending an emailNo, and unfixable downstreamDedupe on your side before calling the provider — you cannot un-send
  THE DUAL-WRITE PROBLEM -- the bug in nearly every "publish an
  event after saving" implementation

    @Transactional
    void placeOrder(Order o) {
        orderRepo.save(o);                       // (1) database
        kafka.send("orders", new OrderPlaced(o)); // (2) broker
    }

  These are two separate systems and there is no transaction over
  both. Four things can happen:

    (1) ok, (2) ok        fine
    (1) fails             fine -- nothing published
    (1) ok, (2) FAILS     order exists, NOBODY KNOWS. No shipment,
                          no email, no analytics. Silent.
    (2) ok, (1) ROLLS BACK  an event for an order that does not
                          exist. Downstream services act on a ghost.

  Note that send() is asynchronous by default: the transaction can
  commit and the send fail afterwards, with the exception on a
  producer callback thread that nothing is watching.

MEASURED: what "rare" means

    orders in one month                        2,140,000
    publish failures after a successful commit       311  (0.015%)
    of those, orders never shipped                   311
    customer complaints                               44
    the rest found by a reconciliation job written
      after the fact                                 267

  0.015% is a rounding error until it is 311 people who paid for
  something that was never sent.

  THE OUTBOX PATTERN -- one transaction, one system

    @Transactional
    void placeOrder(Order o) {
        orderRepo.save(o);
        outboxRepo.save(new OutboxEvent(         // SAME database,
            "orders", o.getId(), toJson(event))); // SAME transaction
    }

    create table outbox (
      id            uuid primary key,
      aggregate_id  varchar(64) not null,
      topic         varchar(64) not null,
      payload       jsonb       not null,
      created_at    timestamptz not null default now(),
      published_at  timestamptz
    );

  A RELAY publishes rows and marks them, or Debezium tails the
  write-ahead log:

    @Scheduled(fixedDelay = 500)
    void relay() {
        for (var e : outboxRepo.findUnpublished(100)) {
            kafka.send(e.topic(), e.aggregateId(), e.payload())
                 .get();                        // wait for the ack
            outboxRepo.markPublished(e.id());
        }
    }

  NOW THE ONLY FAILURE IS A DUPLICATE PUBLISH: if the relay dies
  between send and mark, the event goes out twice. That is
  at-least-once, which consumers must already handle -- and it is
  a fundamentally better failure than "lost forever", because a
  duplicate is detectable and a silence is not.

  COST, HONESTLY: a table, a relay (or Debezium), ordering per
  aggregate only, publish latency of up to the poll interval, and
  a cleanup job. Worth it wherever the event has real-world
  consequences; overkill for analytics telemetry.

  SAGAS -- a workflow as local transactions plus compensations

    order    -> payment -> inventory -> shipping
    each step COMMITS LOCALLY and publishes what it did

    if shipping fails:
      compensate inventory  (release the reservation)
      compensate payment    (REFUND -- not "undo")
      compensate order      (mark CANCELLED, notify)

  CHOREOGRAPHY            each service reacts to events
    + no central component, services stay decoupled
    - the workflow exists in nobody's code; understanding it means
      reading six services. Debugging is archaeology.

  ORCHESTRATION           one component drives the steps
    + the workflow is READABLE IN ONE PLACE, with explicit state
    + timeouts, retries and compensation are centralised
    - the orchestrator is a component to own and keep available

  Rule of thumb: 2-3 steps, choreography. More than that, or any
  money involved, orchestration -- because the first question in
  every incident is "where did this order get stuck?", and only
  orchestration answers it without a distributed log search.

  A COMPENSATION IS NOT A ROLLBACK. It is a new business fact.
    - it is VISIBLE: the customer saw a charge, then a refund
    - it can FAIL: the refund API can be down, so compensations
      need their own retries and their own dead-letter path
    - it must be IDEMPOTENT: it will be retried
    - some steps CANNOT be compensated: an email is sent, a
      physical item has shipped. Order the saga so irreversible
      steps come LAST.
    - there is no isolation: another transaction can observe the
      intermediate state -- an order that is paid but not yet
      shipped, or briefly reserved stock that is about to be
      released.
🔑 Sagas trade atomicity for availability, and the honest framing is that you cannot buy back the isolation. A distributed workflow will be observed mid-flight; a customer will see a charge before a shipment, a report will count an order that is later cancelled. The engineering work is not hiding that — it is choosing a step order where the visible intermediate states are acceptable, and putting the irreversible steps at the end. If your domain genuinely cannot tolerate any intermediate state, that is strong evidence the two pieces of data belong in one service and one database.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is a circuit breaker and what are its states?

It is a wrapper around a call to a dependency that stops calling it when it is clearly failing. Three states: CLOSED, where calls pass through and outcomes are recorded in a sliding window; OPEN, entered when the failure rate crosses a threshold, where calls are rejected immediately without being attempted; and HALF_OPEN, entered after a wait duration, where a few trial calls are admitted — if they succeed the breaker closes, if they fail it opens again.

The point is not really "protect the dependency", although it does that. The point is that failing fast means your threads stop being consumed waiting on something that is going to fail anyway. A timeout still costs you two seconds of a held thread per call; an open breaker costs microseconds.

In Spring you get it from Resilience4j, either with @CircuitBreaker(name = "pricing") or by decorating a call programmatically.

Beginner: Why does every remote call need a timeout?

Because a remote call has a failure mode local calls do not have: it can hang indefinitely. A thread blocked on a socket read is not free — it holds a Tomcat worker, its stack, and often a database connection — so a slow dependency converts directly into lost capacity.

The arithmetic is Little's Law: threads needed equals arrival rate times service time. At 250 requests a second and 40 ms responses you need 10 threads. If that dependency slows to 8 seconds you need 2,000, and you have 200. Every thread ends up blocked, new connections are refused, health checks stop answering and the pod is killed — while CPU sits at 6%, because the service is not broken, it is full.

The trap is that the defaults do not save you: RestTemplate, RestClient and the JDK client all default to no read timeout. "It has always worked" only means the dependency has not hung yet.

Mid: When is it safe to retry a failed call, and when is it not?

The question to answer is not "was there an error?" but "could the work have already happened?"

Safe without further thought: connection refused, DNS failure, connect timeout — the request never reached the server. Also a 503 or 429, where the server is explicitly telling you it did not process the request.

Not safe by default: a read timeout or a 500. The request was sent and may have been fully processed; the response was simply lost. A read timeout tells you only that you did not get an answer, not that nothing happened. I have seen exactly this charge 1,143 customers twice during a gateway latency spike — the retry logic worked as written, and the assumption underneath it was wrong.

Never: 400, 422, 401, 403. They are deterministic; retrying gets the same answer, and retrying an auth failure is how an account gets locked.

So the rule is: retry freely when the operation is idempotent, and make it idempotent with an idempotency key when it is not. And whatever you retry needs exponential backoff with jitter, plus a limit on how many layers retry — three layers each retrying three times is 27× load on a dependency that is already struggling.

Mid: Your circuit breaker never opened during an incident. Why?

There are three usual reasons, and they are all defaults.

minimumNumberOfCalls is 100. The breaker records nothing until it has seen a hundred calls in the window. On an endpoint doing a few requests a minute that is half an hour, so during a ten-minute incident it never had enough data to decide. A time-based window with a minimum around ten fixes it.

failureRateThreshold is 50%. A dependency failing 30% of requests is a serious incident for users and leaves the breaker closed forever.

And the one that catches most people: the dependency was not failing, it was slow. Nine-second responses with HTTP 200 are a 0% failure rate. The breaker stays closed while every thread you have is consumed waiting. That is what slowCallDurationThreshold and slowCallRateThreshold are for, and they are the settings I check first.

Two more possibilities worth checking: the annotated method was called from inside the same bean, so the proxy was bypassed and no breaker ran at all; or 4xx responses were being recorded as failures and someone raised the threshold to stop nuisance trips.

Senior: How do you maintain consistency across services without distributed transactions?

You accept that you cannot have atomicity, and you buy back the properties that actually matter one at a time.

Idempotency first, because the network forces retries. That means a key supplied by the caller, a table with a unique constraint on it, and — the part people miss — storing the response, so a retry gets the same answer rather than a second effect. The unique constraint is what resolves the concurrent-duplicate race; application-level "have I seen this?" checks do not.

Then the dual write. Saving to the database and publishing an event are two systems with no transaction over them. In one system I looked at, that lost 311 events out of 2.1 million orders — 0.015%, which sounds like nothing until it is 311 people who paid for something never shipped. The fix is an outbox: write the event into a table in the same transaction as the state change, and have a relay publish it. That converts "silently lost" into "occasionally duplicated", and duplicates are something consumers must handle anyway.

Then sagas for multi-step workflows: local transactions plus compensating actions. The thing I would emphasise is that a compensation is not a rollback. It is a new business fact — a refund rather than an un-charge. It is visible to the customer, it can itself fail so it needs retries and a dead-letter path, it must be idempotent, and some steps cannot be compensated at all, which means the irreversible ones go last.

For anything with money in it, or more than about three steps, I would orchestrate rather than choreograph — because the first question in every incident is "where did this order get stuck?", and only an orchestrator with explicit state answers that without a distributed log search.

And I would say plainly what is being given up: isolation. There is no way to prevent someone observing the intermediate state. The design work is choosing an order where the visible intermediate states are acceptable — and if none is, that is strong evidence the data belongs in one service.

Senior: A team wants to split their monolith into microservices. How do you advise them?

I would start by asking what problem they are solving, because the answer determines whether this helps at all. Microservices are an organisational solution: they stop many teams from blocking on one release train. If the pain is "our deploys are scary" or "the code is a mess", distribution does not fix either, and it adds the whole of this lesson to their workload.

The costs are concrete. Every in-process call becomes a network call with timeouts, retries and partial failure. Transactions stop working across boundaries, so they inherit idempotency keys, outboxes, sagas and reconciliation jobs. Debugging needs distributed tracing before it needs anything else. Nobody can run the system on a laptop. And availability is multiplicative — twelve dependencies at a genuinely good 99.9% is 98.8%, which is 43 minutes of downtime a month becoming eight and a half hours.

So my advice is usually: modularise first, extract second. Hard module boundaries inside the monolith — separate packages, a public API per module, no cross-module entity references, events between modules rather than direct calls. That gets most of the organisational benefit with real transactions and in-process calls, and it is the only reliable way to discover where the boundaries actually are. Boundaries drawn wrongly are cheap to move inside a monolith and extremely expensive to move between services.

Then extract for a specific reason: a component that must scale on a different axis, a team that needs its own release cadence, a genuinely different availability requirement, or data with different compliance rules. "We might need to scale later" is not a reason — a clean module is easy to extract when that day comes.

The failure I would warn them about explicitly is the distributed monolith: services that must deploy together, share a database, or form a chatty synchronous chain. That has every cost of distribution and every coupling of a monolith, and it is worse than either. The tell is simple — if you cannot deploy one service on its own on a Tuesday afternoon, you have not built microservices.

If they do proceed, I would insist on three things before the second service ships: distributed tracing with a propagated trace id, a timeout and a bulkhead on every remote call, and one connection pool per downstream. Those are cheap on day one and nearly impossible to retrofit calmly during an incident.

2 (easy): A payment gateway spike caused 1,143 duplicate charges. The code below is unchanged since. Fix it. 
  @Retry(name = "payments", maxAttempts = 3)
  public Receipt charge(ChargeRequest req) {
      return gateway.charge(req);        // read timeout 5s
  }
Solution

The defect is the assumption that a read timeout means "did not happen". The gateway received the request, charged the card and committed; only the response was lost. Retrying sends a second, entirely valid charge.

  // 1. the caller supplies a key that is STABLE ACROSS RETRIES
  //    (generated once per business intent, not per attempt)

  @Retry(name = "payments", maxAttempts = 3)
  public Receipt charge(String idempotencyKey, ChargeRequest req) {
      return gateway.charge(idempotencyKey, req);
  }

  // 2. the receiving side, where the guarantee actually lives
  @Transactional
  public Receipt charge(String key, ChargeRequest req) {
      var hash = sha256(req);
      try {
          idem.insertInProgress(key, hash);         // unique PK
      } catch (DuplicateKeyException e) {
          var prior = idem.get(key);
          if (!prior.requestHash().equals(hash))
              throw new IdempotencyKeyReusedException();  // 422
          if (prior.status() == IN_PROGRESS)
              throw new RequestInFlightException();       // 409
          return deserialize(prior.responseBody());       // replay
      }
      Receipt r = doCharge(req);
      idem.complete(key, 200, serialize(r));
      return r;
  }
DetailWhy it matters
The key is generated before the first attemptA key generated per attempt is not an idempotency key, it is a request id — and it dedupes nothing
The unique constraint does the workTwo concurrent duplicates both pass an application-level "have I seen this?" check. The database does not have that race
The response is storedIdempotency means the retry gets the same answer, not that it is silently ignored
The request hash is comparedThe same key with a different body is a client bug — reject it rather than replaying a response for a request nobody made
Keys expireUnbounded growth otherwise; the TTL must exceed the longest client retry window

If you do not control the receiver — a third-party gateway — check whether it supports an idempotency key header, because most payment providers do. If it genuinely does not, the only correct answer is to stop retrying and instead reconcile: record the attempt before calling, and have a job query the gateway for the outcome of attempts with no recorded response.

3 (medium): Pricing responds in 9 seconds with HTTP 200 for six minutes. The circuit breaker never opened and the service went down. Explain and fix the configuration. 
  resilience4j.circuitbreaker.instances.pricing:
    failure-rate-threshold: 50
    sliding-window-size: 100
    wait-duration-in-open-state: 60s
Solution

Why it never opened: nothing failed. Every call returned 200 with a correct body, so the failure rate was 0% and a failure-rate breaker has nothing to trip on. Meanwhile each call held a Tomcat thread for nine seconds, which is the actual outage.

Two further defects would have prevented it opening even if calls had failed: slidingWindowSize: 100 is count-based, so on a low-traffic endpoint the window can hold outcomes from an hour ago; and minimumNumberOfCalls defaults to 100, so the breaker records no decision until it has seen a hundred calls.

  resilience4j.circuitbreaker.instances.pricing:
    sliding-window-type: TIME_BASED
    sliding-window-size: 30                 # last 30 seconds
    minimum-number-of-calls: 10
    failure-rate-threshold: 40
    slow-call-duration-threshold: 1s        # THE FIX
    slow-call-rate-threshold: 40            # 40% over 1s -> OPEN
    wait-duration-in-open-state: 10s
    permitted-number-of-calls-in-half-open-state: 5
    automatic-transition-from-open-to-half-open-enabled: true

  # and the two things the breaker cannot do for you
  resilience4j.timelimiter.instances.pricing:
    timeout-duration: 1s
  resilience4j.bulkhead.instances.pricing:
    max-concurrent-calls: 20
    max-wait-duration: 50ms

  public OrderResult placeOrder(OrderRequest req) {
      Order o = createPending(req);                     // committed
      try {
          Receipt r = payments.charge(o.idempotencyKey(), o);
          orders.markPaid(o.getId(), r);                // own tx
          shipping.book(o.idempotencyKey(), o);         // own tx
          orders.markConfirmed(o.getId());

          orders.markCancelled(o.getId(), e);

Why the order matters: the order row is committed as PENDING before any remote call, so there is always a durable record of the intent. Every subsequent step moves it through explicit states. If the process dies at any point, a recovery job can see an order stuck in PENDING or PAID and finish or compensate it — which is impossible if the only record was in a transaction that rolled back.

The compensation is a refund, not a rollback. The customer sees a charge and then a refund; that is a real, visible business fact. It can also fail, so payments.refund needs its own retries and a dead-letter path — a compensation that silently fails is worse than the original defect, because now nobody is looking.

What I would build next, in order: the recovery job for stuck orders (it is what makes this safe rather than merely correct on the happy path), then the outbox relay, then metrics on orders.stuck by state. If the workflow grows past these three steps I would move it to an explicit orchestrator, because the catch blocks stop being readable at about five steps.

5 (hard): A checkout endpoint calls six services. It must answer in 1.5 s p99 and be available 99.9%. Design it.
Solution

Start with the availability arithmetic, because it decides the architecture. Six required dependencies at 99.9% each gives 0.9996 = 99.4% — about 4 hours of downtime a month, four times the budget. No amount of retry tuning fixes that; the only lever is making dependencies optional.

      local work + serialisation           100 ms

That last move is the important one. The instinct is to shrink the payment timeout to fit the budget, which just converts slow payments into failed ones. Making the slowest required step asynchronous is what actually reconciles a 1.5 s SLA with a 3 s dependency, and it is a product decision as much as a technical one — someone must design the "your order is confirming" state.

  THE REST OF THE DESIGN

  BULKHEADS      one connection pool and one semaphore bulkhead per
                 downstream, sized from Little's Law. The optional
                 four together may never consume more than ~30% of
                 the thread pool.

  DEADLINES      X-Deadline-Ms propagated; each hop bounds its own
                 calls by what remains and rejects immediately when
                 the budget is spent.

  RETRIES        only pricing/inventory/fraud/promos (idempotent
                 reads), one layer, 2 attempts, full jitter.
                 NEVER an automatic retry on payments.

  IDEMPOTENCY    a key minted when the user presses Buy, so a
                 double-click and a client retry collapse to one
                 order.

  OBSERVABILITY  a trace id on every hop; per-dependency fallback
                 counters; an alert on orders stuck in PENDING_STOCK
                 or PENDING_PAYMENT for over 10 minutes.

What I would measure to prove the design: not the happy-path p99, but the p99 with each dependency failed in turn — a game day. The design above claims that killing recommendations changes nothing and killing pricing serves stale prices; both claims are testable, and both are usually wrong the first time you test them.

The honest weak point is PENDING_STOCK. Accepting orders you may not be able to fulfil trades a hard failure for a soft one, and the soft one lands on customer support. That is a business decision about oversell risk, not an engineering one, and it should be made deliberately rather than discovered in an incident review.

Not merge services back together — it is the right long-term answer for some of them, and it is a six-month conversation about team ownership that will consume the whole quarter and ship nothing. Not build a service mesh: it does move timeouts and retries into infrastructure, which is genuinely attractive, but it is a platform project with its own operational learning curve, and it does not make dependencies optional, which is where the availability actually is. Not chase dashboards — traces answer incident questions; dashboards answer questions you already knew to ask.

The number I would track is not availability, which moves too slowly to steer by. It is median time-to-diagnosis per incident, and the count of remote calls with no timeout. Both move weekly, both are unambiguous, and both predict the availability number a quarter later.

The conversation I would start but not finish: which of the twelve services exist for a real reason. Some almost certainly must deploy together, share a database, or sit in a synchronous chain — a distributed monolith paying every cost of distribution for none of the benefit. Merging two of those back is often the largest single availability win available, and it is worth having the evidence gathered by the end of the quarter even if the work lands in the next one.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • A remote call has two outcomes local calls do not have: it can hang forever, and it can succeed without you finding out. Every pattern here exists for those two.
  • Capacity is request-seconds, not requests. A dependency 200× slower consumes 200× your thread pool: 40 ms → 8 s took a service down in 90 seconds at 6% CPU.
  • The default read timeout is infinite in RestTemplate, RestClient and the JDK client. "It has always worked" is not a timeout.
  • Set three timeouts — connect, read, and pool acquisition. Missing the third rebuilds the bug one level down, where the read timeout never fires.
  • Derive timeouts from your own SLA and the dependency's p99.9, not from patience. A timeout longer than your SLA is not a timeout.
  • A read timeout does not mean the work did not happen. That assumption charged 1,143 customers twice.
  • Retry is the only pattern that adds load to a failing system. Three layers × three attempts is 27×, and retry pressure peaks exactly when capacity is lowest.
  • Retry at one layer, with jitter and a budget, and only for operations that are idempotent — by nature or by key.
  • The breaker defaults stop it working: minimumNumberOfCalls: 100 and a 50% failure rate mean a low-traffic endpoint never opens during an incident.
  • Set slow-call thresholds. A dependency answering 200 in 9 seconds has a 0% failure rate and will consume every thread you have.
  • Breaker state is per JVM — 24 pods learn independently, which argues for time-based windows and low minimums.
  • A good fallback is stale-but-labelled or a fast 503; a default price or an empty list is a silent wrong answer nobody notices for hours.
  • One connection pool per downstream. A shared pool makes every dependency a single point of failure for all the others.
  • Decorator order is Retry(CircuitBreaker(TimeLimiter(Bulkhead))) — invert it and the breaker sees a third of the failures it should.
  • Idempotency means storing the response, keyed by a caller-supplied key with a unique constraint — the database resolves the race that application checks cannot.
  • Saving to the database and publishing an event is a dual write. 0.015% loss was 311 orders nobody downstream ever heard about; the outbox converts silent loss into duplicates.
  • A compensation is not a rollback. It is visible, it can fail, it must be idempotent, and irreversible steps go last.
  • Availability is multiplicative: twelve 99.9% dependencies is 98.8% — 43 minutes a month becomes 8 hours 38. The fix is making dependencies optional, not making them better.
  • The distributed monolith is worse than either option. If you cannot deploy one service alone on a Tuesday, modularise the monolith instead.
