Performance: Throughput vs Latency Operations

What you'll master: where end-to-end time actually goes, from send() to a consumer's handler, and which setting moves each part; the producer's latency-throughput curve, and why raising linger.ms can lower latency under load; the broker's request-time breakdown — queue, local, remote, response — which turns "Kafka is slow" into a specific component in one query; request handler and network idle ratios; consumer fetch tuning; the JVM and OS settings that matter (and the heap size that quietly destroys throughput); what TLS really costs; and a repeatable method for investigating a latency regression. Prerequisites: Partition Count & Capacity Planning.

🔑 Most "Kafka is slow" reports are not Kafka. In a healthy cluster, stages 2–5 total single-digit milliseconds. The large terms are the ones your configuration chose — linger.ms on the producer, fetch.min.bytes with a 500 ms maximum wait on the consumer — and the processing time in the handler. Measure the end-to-end split before tuning brokers, or you will spend a week on a component contributing 3% of the latency.

Part 2 — The Producer's Latency-Throughput Curve

Purgatory is not a problem. Requests waiting deliberately — acks=all produce requests awaiting replication, fetch requests waiting for fetch.min.bytes — sit in a delayed operation purgatory, and its size grows with normal load. A large purgatory alongside healthy latency is expected; it is RemoteTimeMs that says whether the waiting is excessive.

Part 4 — Consumer-Side Latency

The consumer's default fetch settings are tuned for efficiency, and they can add hundreds of milliseconds to a lightly loaded topic.

fetch.min.bytes = 1        (default)  → the broker replies as soon as ANY data exists: low latency
fetch.max.wait.ms = 500    (default)  → if no data, the broker holds the fetch up to 500 ms

raising fetch.min.bytes to 64 KB on a low-volume topic:
  the broker waits until 64 KB accumulates OR 500 ms passes
  at 2 KB/s that is the full 500 ms, every time → +500 ms of latency for fewer requests
SettingRaise it forCost
fetch.min.bytesFewer, larger fetches on high-volume topicsUp to fetch.max.wait.ms of latency when data is sparse
max.partition.fetch.bytesFaster catch-up on large backlogsConsumer memory (the catch-up arithmetic from the consumers lesson)
max.poll.recordsFewer loop iterationsLonger batches against the poll deadline
client.rack (follower fetching)Cross-zone costSlightly higher latency: the follower learns the high watermark a moment later

Consumer parallelism is the other half. If the handler takes 20 ms per record, one consumer on one partition can do 50 per second whatever the broker does. The fixes are the ones from the consumers lesson — batch the work, parallelise by key, add partitions — and no broker tuning substitutes for them.

TLS has a real cost, and it is not only CPU. Encryption happens in user space, so the broker can no longer use sendfile to move data from the page cache straight to the socket — the zero-copy path from the first lesson. Expect a measurable throughput reduction and higher CPU; measure it on your hardware rather than trusting a published percentage, and budget CPU for it rather than treating security as free.

4. FOLLOW THAT LEAD.

5. CROSS-CHECK the usual suspects: a GC pause, a backfill consumer, a reassignment
                       in progress, a quota throttling, a zone failover.
6. CONFIRM by changing ONE thing and measuring the same percentile again.
ObservationLikely cause
p99 spikes every few minutes, p50 flatGC pauses, or segment rolls plus writeback bursts

❌ Tuning broker configuration first. A team reports a latency regression, and the response is to raise num.io.threads, add brokers and adjust flush settings. The numbers do not move, because the actual change was a consumer group that started a backfill, pulling old segments from disk and evicting the page cache that tailing consumers relied on.

✅ Measure the split before changing anything. The request breakdown names the component in minutes, and the client-side percentiles say whether the broker is involved at all. Tuning without that is guessing, and every change makes the next measurement harder to interpret.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What contributes to end-to-end latency in Kafka?

Working forwards: the producer's batching wait (up to linger.ms), the produce request itself including any queueing on the broker, the leader's append, replication to the in-sync replicas when acks=all, the high watermark advancing so consumers may read the record, the consumer's fetch wait, and finally the application's own processing.

In a healthy cluster the broker-side parts total single-digit milliseconds. The large, adjustable terms are usually the ones configuration chose: linger.ms on the producer, and on the consumer fetch.min.bytes together with fetch.max.wait.ms, which defaults to 500 milliseconds — so a consumer on a quiet topic can wait half a second for a record that already exists.

And in most real pipelines the largest term is the handler: a database write or an API call per record dwarfs everything Kafka does.

That is why the first step in any latency investigation is splitting the measurement rather than tuning: the client-side percentiles and the broker's request-time breakdown say within minutes which part to work on.

Beginner: Does raising linger.ms always increase latency?

No — that is the counter-intuitive part. At low load it does: the producer waits up to the linger value before sending, and the record's latency rises by roughly that amount.

Under heavy load the opposite can happen. With linger.ms=0 the producer sends many small requests; each has fixed overhead on the broker, the request handler threads queue, and the queueing delay grows. Raising the linger produces fewer, fuller batches, so the same volume moves in far fewer requests, queueing falls, and the p99 can improve substantially — while throughput also goes up.

Compression interacts with it too: bigger batches compress much better, so a larger linger can reduce the bytes on the wire, which helps when the bottleneck is network or replication.

The way to know which side you are on is the producer's own metrics: a very small batch-size-avg with high request rates means batching is not happening, and a record-queue-time-avg near the configured linger with low throughput means you are simply paying the wait. I would test a few values against a fixed offered load rather than reasoning about it.

Mid: Produce latency has risen to 400 ms at p99 while consumers are fine. How do you find the cause?

Produce-only points at the write path, and specifically at replication, since consumption is healthy.

I would start with the broker's request-time breakdown for Produce requests — queue, local, remote, response — at the 99th percentile. If RemoteTimeMs dominates, the leader is waiting for in-sync followers, which is the most common cause of exactly this pattern with acks=all. Then I would find which follower: ISR shrink and expand events in the broker logs name the broker, and that broker's disk latency, CPU steal and GC pauses usually explain it. This is the stalled-replica case from the replication lesson, where a broker that is alive but slow holds up every acknowledgement.

If RequestQueueTimeMs dominates instead, the request handler pool is saturated: RequestHandlerAvgIdlePercent near zero, often because of a CPU increase such as newly enabled TLS or broker-side recompression. If LocalTimeMs dominates, the append itself is slow — disk saturation or page cache pressure, frequently caused by a catch-up consumer pulling old segments.

I would also check the obvious external causes: a partition reassignment running without a throttle, a zone failover concentrating leadership, or quotas producing ThrottleTimeMs.

Then fix the specific cause and re-measure the same percentile. What I would avoid is weakening acks to make the number look better: that trades a latency problem for a durability one.

Mid: Why should a Kafka broker have a small heap?

Because Kafka does not cache data in the JVM. Records are written to the page cache and read from it, so the operating system's cache is what keeps consumers off the disk. Memory given to the heap is memory taken from that cache.

A broker on a 64 GB machine with a 48 GB heap leaves the kernel very little to cache with, so fetches that would have been served from memory become disk reads, and tail latency rises for everyone. The same machine with a 6 GB heap has nearly 58 GB of page cache and behaves completely differently.

The second reason is garbage collection. Kafka's heap usage is mostly short-lived request buffers, which a modest heap handles with short pauses. A very large heap produces longer pauses, and a pause that exceeds replica.lag.time.max.ms or the broker session timeout has consequences beyond latency: the broker drops out of ISRs or is fenced by the controller, moving partitions and causing churn.

So the guidance is a few gigabytes of heap — commonly around six — with the rest of RAM left to the OS, and monitoring of GC pause duration rather than collection counts.

Senior: Design an end-to-end latency budget of 200 ms at p99 for an order pipeline.

I would allocate the budget across the stages, leave margin, and then choose settings to fit — rather than tuning first and hoping.

StageBudgetSetting or design choice
Producer batching10 mslinger.ms=10, sized batches; measured under peak load
Produce + replication (acks=all)25 msRF 3 in one region; healthy ISR; alert on RemoteTimeMs
Consumer fetch10 msfetch.min.bytes=1; no follower fetching on this path if it adds latency
Processing100 msThe application's own budget: a database write plus business logic
Margin55 msGC pauses, failovers, retries — the events that create the p99

Design consequences. Cross-region replication in the synchronous path would consume the whole budget, so this pipeline stays in one region with three zones. Exactly-once transactions add the commit interval to consumer-visible latency, so if it is needed the commit interval must be small and accounted for. Any batching in the handler — grouping database writes — must fit the 100 ms allocation.

Then measure end to end, not per component: stamp the event's creation time in the payload, and have the consumer report the difference at processing time as a histogram. That single metric is the SLO, and it catches everything, including causes nobody predicted.

Recommendation: publish the budget table with the service's documentation, alert on the end-to-end p99 rather than on component metrics, and treat any change that adds a synchronous hop — a new join, a cross-region replica, a transactional boundary — as a change to the budget that has to be re-approved.

Senior: A cluster's p99 doubled after enabling TLS. What is happening, and what are the options?

Two effects, one of which surprises people.

The expected one: encryption and decryption cost CPU on brokers and clients, and the TLS handshake adds work on every new connection — significant for clients that reconnect often.

The surprising one: TLS removes the zero-copy path. Without it, a broker can send data straight from the page cache to the socket with sendfile, never copying it into user space. With TLS, every byte must be read into the JVM, encrypted and written out, which costs both CPU and memory bandwidth. Fetch-heavy clusters feel this most, because that path carried the bulk of their traffic.

I would confirm it with the request breakdown — RequestQueueTimeMs and ResponseSendTimeMs rising, RequestHandlerAvgIdlePercent and NetworkProcessorAvgIdlePercent falling — and with broker CPU.

Options, in the order I would consider them: add CPU or brokers, since the work is real and security is not optional; make sure clients reuse connections rather than reconnecting; check the cipher suite, because hardware-accelerated AES-GCM is far cheaper than some alternatives; reduce the bytes flowing through TLS with better producer compression; and use follower fetching so cross-zone consumer traffic disappears entirely.

Recommendation: budget the capacity rather than removing TLS. Where a specific internal path genuinely cannot afford it — replication between brokers on a private network, for example — that is a risk decision to make explicitly with security, not a tuning knob to turn quietly.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Split the latency before tuning: batching, request, replication, fetch wait and processing are separate terms with separate fixes.
  • In a healthy cluster the broker-side terms are single-digit milliseconds; the big ones are usually configuration and handler time.
  • Raising linger.ms can lower p99 under load by replacing many small requests with few full ones.
  • The broker request breakdown names the component: queue, local, remote, throttle, response.
  • RemoteTimeMs dominating means a slow in-sync follower — the most common cause of produce-only latency.
  • Request handler and network idle ratios below ~0.3 mean that pool is the constraint; a saturated CPU means finding the work, not adding threads.
  • Purgatory size grows with healthy load and is not itself a problem.
  • fetch.min.bytes on a quiet topic costs up to fetch.max.wait.ms — 500 ms by default.
  • Consumer parallelism, not broker tuning, fixes slow handlers.
  • Give brokers a small heap and the rest of RAM to the page cache; large heaps starve the cache and lengthen pauses.
  • Watch GC pause duration, not count: a long pause causes ISR churn and fencing, not just latency.
  • Set vm.swappiness very low — swapping a broker produces multi-second stalls.
  • Size socket buffers from the bandwidth-delay product for cross-region links.
  • TLS costs CPU and removes zero-copy; budget capacity for it rather than disabling it.
  • Change one thing and re-measure the same percentile; simultaneous changes make attribution impossible.
