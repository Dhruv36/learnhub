Kafka vs the Alternatives Architecture

What you'll master: a decision framework that starts from the problem shape rather than the technology; Kafka against queues, against the other log-shaped systems, and against simply using a database; the honest cases where you do not need Kafka at all; managed versus self-managed, priced in engineering time rather than instance hours; how to write a decision record that survives the people who wrote it; and a capstone recap of the traps from the whole track — the ones that turn a good choice into a bad system. Prerequisites: the rest of this track, particularly What Kafka Actually Is and Multi-Region & Disaster Recovery.

Part 1 — Start From the Problem Shape

Most bad messaging decisions come from picking a technology and then describing the problem in its terms. The useful order is the reverse: describe what the data is and what consumers need, and the choice usually follows.

QuestionIf yesPoints to
Do several independent consumers need the same data?Fan-out with no coordination between themKafka, Pulsar, Kinesis, Pub/Sub
Does anyone need to re-read history?Replay, reprocessing, new consumers starting from the pastA log — Kafka and friends
Must records for one entity stay in order?Ordering per keyA partitioned log
Is it work to be done exactly once by one worker?Per-message ack, retry, delay, priorityA queue: SQS, RabbitMQ, a DB-backed job table
Does the caller need an answer?Request/response, correlation, timeoutsHTTP or gRPC
Do you need to look up the current value by key?Random access, queries, indexesA database (possibly fed from Kafka)
Is it a few hundred events a day with one consumer?Very low volumeA database table and a poller
🔑 "Events" is not the same as "a log". Plenty of event-driven systems are better served by a queue or a database outbox than by Kafka. The features that justify a log — retention, replay, many independent readers, partitioned ordering at high throughput — are specific, and if a design does not use them, Kafka is being paid for in operational complexity without return.

The clean test: if a failed message should be retried later without holding up everything behind it, you want a queue. If a consumer that starts tomorrow should see what happened today, you want a log. Systems that need both usually get both: a log of facts, and a queue for the work those facts trigger.

Part 3 — Kafka Versus Other Logs

SystemDistinctive propertyChoose it when

RedpandaKafka-protocol compatible, no JVM, thread-per-core designYou want Kafka's API with fewer moving parts and lower tail latency, and accept a different vendor

Protocol compatibility is worth more than feature comparisons. Systems that speak the Kafka protocol let you keep clients, Connect, Streams and tooling, which makes a later change a migration of infrastructure rather than of every application. Systems with their own protocol lock the choice into every service that connects, and that is the cost to weigh against whatever they do better.

Be sceptical of benchmark claims in both directions. As the capacity lesson showed, the numbers depend entirely on record size, batching, compression, acks, replication and whether catch-up reads are included. A vendor benchmark chooses those; your workload will not match it. The operational questions — who runs it, how upgrades work, what happens at 3 a.m. — separate these systems far more than throughput does.

Part 4 — When the Answer Is Not a Message System

an honest sequence many teams should follow

1. one producer, one consumer, low volume
   → an outbox table and a poller. Ten lines of SQL, no cluster.
2. a second consumer appears; volume grows
   → the outbox becomes the source for CDC, or the poller publishes to a broker
3. replay, fan-out, throughput or stream processing become real requirements
   → NOW Kafka earns its operational cost, and the event contracts already exist

adopting Kafka at step 1 is the common mistake: the cluster arrives years before the requirement.

Part 6 — Writing the Decision Down, and the Traps to Avoid

a decision record that survives its authors

  CONTEXT     the data, the volume, the consumers today and expected
  REQUIREMENTS retention, replay, ordering, delivery guarantee, latency, throughput, residency
  OPTIONS     what was considered, including "not a message system"
  DECISION    what was chosen
  BECAUSE     the two or three requirements that actually decided it
  ACCEPTED    the costs taken on: operational load, RPO, duplicates, partition-count commitments
  REVISIT     the condition that should reopen this — "if a second region is required",
              "if volume exceeds X", "if we need per-message retries"

The "revisit" line is what makes such a record useful three years later. Most architecture arguments are really about whether the original conditions still hold, and a record that names them turns a debate into a check.

The traps this track has covered, collected — because choosing Kafka correctly and then falling into these produces the same unhappiness as choosing wrongly:

The thread running through all of them is that Kafka's guarantees are narrow and precise, and most incidents come from assuming a broader one: that acknowledged means durable, that delivery means once, that ordering is global, that a consumer group is keeping up, that a topic's schema is a contract someone is enforcing. The engineers who operate Kafka well are the ones who can state each guarantee's exact boundary — which is also what an interviewer is listening for.

Interview Questions

Beginner: When would you choose a queue over Kafka?

When the messages represent work rather than facts, and each item needs its own lifecycle: retried on its own, delayed, prioritised, or dead-lettered without affecting anything else. Image processing jobs, email sending, payment capture attempts — one worker should handle each item, and a failure should not hold up the queue behind it.

Kafka cannot do that natively. It tracks one offset per partition, so a record that fails blocks the records behind it unless you build retry topics, which give up ordering. It has no delayed delivery and no priorities, and parallelism is capped by the partition count.

Conversely I would choose Kafka when the messages are facts that several systems care about, when history matters — replay, a new consumer reading the past — or when throughput or per-key ordering at scale is the requirement.

Many systems want both, and that is a perfectly good design: publish facts to Kafka, and let a service that must do work enqueue it in a queue built for work.

Beginner: What does Kafka give you that a database table cannot?

Several things, though fewer than people assume for small systems.

Independent consumers at no coordination cost: any number of consumer groups read the same data at their own pace, each with its own position, without the producer knowing or a table being queried repeatedly. Retention with replay: a new consumer can start from the beginning of history. Ordered, partitioned throughput: per-key ordering while scaling across brokers, well beyond what a single table and poller sustain. And a processing ecosystem — Connect, Streams, sinks — built around that model.

What a table does better: querying by key, transactions across entities, arbitrary indexes, and ad-hoc analysis. And for one producer, one consumer and modest volume, an outbox table with a poller is genuinely simpler and has no cluster to operate.

So the honest answer is that Kafka replaces the transport and history, not the database. The common architecture uses both: the database as the source of truth, an outbox for reliable publication, and Kafka to distribute the facts to everyone who needs them.

Mid: A team proposes replacing their RabbitMQ setup with Kafka. How do you evaluate it?

I would ask what is wrong with the current setup, because the answer determines whether Kafka helps at all.

Good reasons to move: several consumers now need the same messages and copies are being maintained; someone needs to replay history and cannot; throughput is at the broker's limit; ordering per entity is required and cannot be guaranteed; or a stream-processing capability is wanted that the queue has no answer for.

Reasons that predict trouble: "Kafka is the standard", "it is faster", or a wish to consolidate technologies. If the workload is genuinely job processing — per-message retries, delays, priorities — Kafka makes it worse, and the team will rebuild those features as retry topics, losing ordering and gaining moving parts.

The costs to make explicit: an operational surface the team may not have (or a managed service's bill), redesigning consumers for at-least-once with idempotency, partition counts that cap parallelism, and a migration in which both systems run at once.

My usual recommendation is a split rather than a replacement: move the messages that are genuinely facts — the ones other teams keep asking for copies of — to Kafka, and leave the job-processing queues where they are. That captures the benefit without taking on the mismatch, and it makes the decision reversible.

Mid: Managed Kafka or self-managed? How do you decide?

Mostly by comparing engineering time with the price difference, and by being honest about what is actually removed.

What managed removes: upgrades and their rehearsal, capacity planning and broker provisioning, disk and quorum health, rebalancing brokers, the 3 a.m. page. On a modest platform that is a significant fraction of an engineer continuously, and the risk that the knowledge sits with one person. Against that, unit prices are higher — often several times the raw infrastructure cost.

What it does not remove: everything in this track that is not cluster operations. Topic and key design, partition counts, schema compatibility, consumer correctness and idempotency, DR strategy, client tuning, ACLs and quotas. Teams that expect the service to make Kafka simple rather than to make running Kafka someone else's job are disappointed.

Reasons to stay self-managed: scale where the price difference is very large, specific version or plugin requirements, data residency or isolation the vendor cannot meet, or an existing platform team for whom this is routine.

My default for a small or medium team is managed, on a Kafka-protocol-compatible service so the choice stays portable, and to spend the saved time on the design work that no vendor can do for you. I would revisit it when volume makes the price difference comparable to a dedicated engineer.

Senior: Design the messaging strategy for a company moving from a monolith to services.

I would resist choosing one technology for everything, and instead define the shapes and match each.

Facts between domains → Kafka. Domain events published by the owning service, one topic per aggregate, keyed by entity id, produced through a transactional outbox so the event exists exactly when the change does. Schemas in a registry with FULL_TRANSITIVE compatibility because teams deploy independently. This is what actually decouples the services.

Work → queues. Jobs with retries, delays and priorities stay in SQS or RabbitMQ, where those features exist. A service that must do work in response to an event consumes the event and enqueues the job.

Synchronous needs → HTTP/gRPC. If a caller needs an answer now, that is an API call, not a request topic with a reply topic.

Read models → databases fed from Kafka, so queries hit an indexed store rather than a log, and analytics goes to a warehouse fed by the same events.

Sequencing matters as much as the choices. Early in a migration there are few consumers and the volume is small, so I would start with outbox tables and a simple relay, and introduce Kafka when the second and third consumers appear. The event contracts — names, keys, envelopes, versioning — are worth designing from day one, because they outlive the transport.

Recommendation: Kafka for domain facts with outbox publication and registry-managed schemas; queues for work; HTTP for answers; databases for state. Plus a platform investment that is easy to forget: shared client libraries with sane defaults, a topic inventory with owners, and a paved road for schema changes — because 40 teams making these decisions independently is what produces the god topic and the twelve incompatible envelopes.

Senior: Your platform has run Kafka for three years. How do you know whether it is still the right choice?

By checking the conditions that justified it, which is why they should have been written down. If the decision record names them — replay, fan-out, throughput, ordering — the review is a measurement rather than an argument.

Signals that it is still right: multiple independent consumer groups per topic; replay actually used, for backfills and incident recovery; throughput or partition counts that no single-node system would serve; stream processing in production; and incidents that are about design mistakes rather than about Kafka being the wrong shape.

Signals that it is not: most topics have exactly one consumer; nobody has ever replayed anything; volume is low enough that a database table would serve; the retry topics and dead-letter machinery suggest the workload is really job processing; or the platform team spends most of its time on Kafka operations rather than on enabling teams.

And the middle case, which is the most common: Kafka is right for a subset and over-applied to the rest. The response is not to remove it but to stop the default — a paved road that routes low-volume, single-consumer integrations to simpler options, so the cluster carries what benefits from it.

Recommendation: an annual review against the recorded conditions, with three numbers — consumer groups per topic, replay events per quarter, and platform engineering time spent on operations versus enablement. Those distinguish "still the right tool" from "the tool we happen to have" better than any architectural discussion.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Decide from the problem shape — log, queue, RPC or database — not from the technology you know.
  • A log gives retention, replay, fan-out and partitioned ordering; if a design uses none of them, Kafka is overhead.
  • Work with per-message retries, delays or priorities belongs in a queue, not in a partitioned log.
  • Request/response belongs in HTTP or gRPC; reply topics are hand-built RPC.
  • Kafka is not a database: no index, no queries, no cross-entity transactions.
  • For one low-volume consumer, an outbox table and a poller beat a cluster — and become a CDC source later.
  • Protocol compatibility is portability; it makes the choice reversible in a way feature lists do not.
  • Distrust vendor benchmarks: record size, acks, compression and catch-up reads decide the numbers.
  • Managed services remove running Kafka, not designing with Kafka.
  • Price engineering time, not just infrastructure — it is usually the larger term.
  • Split clusters only for concrete isolation requirements; use quotas and ACLs for tenancy.
  • Write a decision record with accepted costs and revisit conditions, so the argument is checkable later.
  • Review the choice against those conditions annually: consumer groups per topic, replays used, operations versus enablement time.
  • Over-application is more common than a wrong choice — fix the default, not the technology.
  • Kafka's guarantees are narrow and precise; nearly every incident in this track comes from assuming a broader one.
