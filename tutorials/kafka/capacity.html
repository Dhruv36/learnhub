Partition Count & Capacity Planning Operations

What you'll master: the four dimensions a Kafka cluster runs out of — network, disk throughput, disk space and partition count — and which one you will hit first; the egress arithmetic that surprises everyone, where replication and three consumer groups turn 100 MB/s in into 500 MB/s out; storage sizing with replication, retention and real headroom; what each partition costs a broker, and why recovery time grows with partition count; benchmarking that measures the right thing — p99 under catch-up reads, not average throughput on a warm cache; sizing for a lost zone rather than for a good day; and a cost model in which cross-zone traffic can exceed the brokers. Prerequisites: Cluster Architecture & KRaft and the partition-sizing material in Topics, Partitions & the Log.

Part 1 — The Four Things You Run Out Of

DimensionLimitSymptom when reached
NetworkNIC bandwidth per broker, both directionsProduce latency rises; follower lag; ISR shrinks
Disk throughputSequential write + catch-up read rateRequest queue time grows; page cache thrashes
Disk spaceRetention × rate × replicationA full log directory takes the broker offline
Partition countFiles, memory, fetcher work, recovery time per brokerSlow restarts, long leader elections, high idle CPU

Egress is the dimension people forget. Every byte produced is written once to the leader, copied to each follower, and then read by every consumer group. The broker's outbound traffic is therefore a multiple of the inbound:

ingress          100 MB/s produced to the cluster
replication out  100 × (RF − 1) = 200 MB/s          leaders sending to followers
consumer out     100 × 3 groups  = 300 MB/s          three groups each reading everything
                 ─────────────────────────────
total egress     500 MB/s across the cluster, against 100 MB/s in

per broker, 6 brokers: ~17 MB/s in, ~83 MB/s out — plus catch-up reads, which are bursty
a 10 Gb/s NIC is ~1.2 GB/s: fine here, but a fourth consumer group adds another 100 MB/s
🔑 Adding a consumer group is a capacity decision. It costs no storage and no producer work, which makes it feel free — and it adds a full copy of the topic's traffic to broker egress and, if the group is in another zone, to the cross-zone bill. A platform team that lets teams self-serve new consumer groups should track egress per group, not just topic count.

A full log directory is an outage, not a warning. When a broker cannot write, the affected log directory fails and its partitions go offline; with enough of them, the broker stops being useful entirely. Disk alerts need to fire with days of margin, and the growth rate matters more than the percentage — a steady climb means retention or compaction has stopped, as the compaction lesson describes.

Part 3 — What a Partition Costs

Partitions are the unit of parallelism, so more looks better. Each one is also a set of files, a replication stream, a producer batch and an entry in every metadata view.

CostWhere it lands
Open file handlesSeveral per segment (log, index, timeindex) per replica — a broker with tens of thousands of partitions needs a raised nofile limit
Page cache pressureEvery active partition writes to its own segment; more partitions means less sequential locality
Replication workFetcher threads multiplex partitions; more partitions means more per-fetch bookkeeping
Producer memory and batchingbuffer.memory divided across partitions: more partitions, smaller batches, worse compression
Recovery timeAfter an unclean shutdown, each log is checked and recovered — the dominant restart cost on partition-heavy brokers
Failover timeA broker's loss means electing leaders for every partition it led
restart arithmetic after an unclean shutdown
  4,000 partition replicas on the broker, log recovery ~25 ms per log with default threads
  → ~100 s of recovery before it serves anything
  num.recovery.threads.per.data.dir = 1 by default; raising it to the number of cores cuts this
  (a clean shutdown avoids the check entirely — another reason never to SIGKILL a broker)

Keep a per-broker partition budget. KRaft raised how many partitions a cluster can manage, but the per-broker costs above did not go away. Pick a number the team is comfortable operating — thousands per broker rather than tens of thousands, then verified by testing restart and failover times — and treat partition creation as spending from it. A topic with 200 partitions "because it might grow" spends 600 replicas of that budget at RF 3.

Part 4 — Benchmarking That Means Something

The built-in tools are enough, if they are pointed at the right question. The common mistake is measuring average throughput on an empty cluster with data that fits in the page cache — a number that has no relationship to production behaviour.

$ kafka-producer-perf-test.sh --topic bench --num-records 50000000 --record-size 900 \
    --throughput -1 --producer-props bootstrap.servers=b1:9092 acks=all compression.type=lz4 \
    linger.ms=20 batch.size=131072
50000000 records sent, 214,204 records/sec (183.8 MB/sec), 41.2 ms avg latency,
  892.0 ms max latency, 33 ms 50th, 96 ms 95th, 184 ms 99th, 402 ms 99.9th.

$ kafka-consumer-perf-test.sh --bootstrap-server b1:9092 --topic bench --messages 50000000 --threads 4
DoWhy
Use your real record size, compression and acksEach changes the answer by multiples
Run long enough to exceed page cache and to hit GCA 30-second run measures RAM, not the system
Read p99 and p99.9, not the averageUsers experience the tail; averages hide stalls
Include a catch-up readerCold reads evict the page cache and change tail latency for everyone
Test with the real partition count and consumer groupsEgress and batching both depend on them
Kill a broker mid-runFailover behaviour under load is the number that matters in an incident

❌ Capacity planning from a benchmark of tail reads only. The test produces and consumes at the head of the log, everything is served from the page cache, and the cluster looks capable of three times its eventual real load. In production a new consumer group starts from the beginning, or a consumer recovers from an outage, and those cold reads pull old segments from disk — evicting the cache that tailing consumers were using and pushing p99 up for everyone.

✅ Benchmark the bad day. Run the steady-state load, then start a consumer reading from the earliest offset, and watch the tail latency of the healthy consumers. That number sets your real headroom, and it is the reason clusters are sized well below their theoretical ceiling.

Part 5 — Headroom: Size for the Failure, Not the Good Day

three zones, 9 brokers, steady state 60% utilisation

zone lost:
  6 brokers carry what 9 carried  →  60% × 9/6 = 90% utilisation
  plus: leaders that were in the lost zone move here   (more egress per surviving broker)
  plus: consumers that were in the lost zone reconnect elsewhere
  plus: when the zone returns, re-replication traffic competes with client traffic
→ 90% is not survivable with those additions; the cluster needed ~45-55% steady-state utilisation
EventExtra load it createsPlan for it by
Losing a zoneTraffic concentrated on the remaining brokersSteady state at 50–60% of capacity
A broker replacedFull re-replication of its partitionsThrottled reassignment; spare disk and network
A consumer group catching upCold reads from disk at maximum rateQuotas on that group; headroom for the burst
A traffic spikeIngress and egress rise togetherHistorical peak-to-average ratio, not the average
A rolling upgradeOne broker down at a time, leadership movingCapacity for N−1 during maintenance

Peak, not average, is the input. A cluster sized on daily average will be at three times that during the evening peak, and a cluster sized on the peak of a normal day has nothing left for the day a batch job replays a month of history. Plan from the observed peak with a stated growth rate, and revisit the numbers on a schedule.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What resources does a Kafka broker run out of first?

Usually network, then disk throughput, then disk space, with partition count as a fourth limit that shows up as slow restarts and failovers rather than as saturation.

Network is first because Kafka multiplies traffic. Every produced byte is written to the leader, copied to each follower, and read by every consumer group. With replication factor 3 and three consumer groups, 100 MB/s of ingress becomes about 500 MB/s of egress across the cluster.

Disk throughput matters mainly for reads that miss the page cache. Writes are sequential and cheap; consumers that are caught up read from memory. It is the catch-up readers — a new consumer group, or one recovering from an outage — that pull old segments from disk and compete with everything else.

Disk space is the most predictable: rate × retention × replication factor, plus headroom. It is the least likely to surprise you, provided compaction and retention are actually working — and a full log directory takes the broker offline rather than merely warning.

CPU is rarely the limit, unless TLS or broker-side recompression is in play, both of which add real work.

Beginner: How do you estimate storage for a topic?

Bytes per day, times retention in days, times the replication factor, plus headroom.

The detail that matters most is measuring the compressed size, because the broker stores what the producer sent, and JSON-like payloads often compress three to five times. Estimating from uncompressed record sizes overshoots by a large factor; estimating from a single small record undershoots, because compression works much better across a full batch.

Then the multipliers: replication factor 3 means three copies of every byte on the cluster's disks. I add roughly 40% headroom, which covers bursts, the space needed when a failed broker's partitions are re-replicated elsewhere, and partition reassignments.

Two exceptions are worth remembering. Compacted topics are sized by the number of keys and their value size, not by the write rate, because superseded records are removed. And tiered storage, where available, moves closed segments to object storage, so brokers only need space for recent data — though not for compacted topics.

Mid: A cluster runs comfortably at 60% utilisation across three zones. Is that enough?

No, if the goal is to survive a zone loss. With nine brokers across three zones, losing one zone leaves six brokers carrying what nine carried: 60% becomes 90% before anything else is counted.

And other things are counted. Leadership for the lost zone's partitions moves to the survivors, so their egress rises more than their share of ingress. Consumers that ran in the lost zone reconnect to brokers in the others. When the zone returns, re-replication of everything written meanwhile competes with live traffic. At 90% baseline, each of those pushes the cluster past its limits, and the failure becomes a latency incident on top of the capacity event.

So for a three-zone cluster I size steady state at roughly 50–60% of capacity measured at peak, not on the daily average — which usually means the honest question is whether that 60% was measured at peak or on average, because those differ by a factor of two or three in most businesses.

I would verify it rather than calculate it: run a game day that blocks one zone, watch produce latency, consumer lag and under-replicated partitions, and see whether the cluster is still within its SLOs. That answer is worth more than any utilisation target.

Mid: How do you benchmark a Kafka cluster properly?

Decide the question first, then make the test resemble production in the ways that change the answer.

Match the workload: real record sizes, the compression the producers use, the same acks and idempotence settings, the partition count the topic will have, and the number of consumer groups — each of these moves throughput by multiples.

Run long enough that the data exceeds the page cache and that garbage collection has happened. A short run on an empty cluster measures RAM. Read the tail: p99 and p99.9 from kafka-producer-perf-test.sh, not the average, because the average hides exactly the stalls users notice.

Include the bad day. Start a consumer from the earliest offset while the steady load runs: those cold reads evict the page cache and show what happens to everyone else's tail latency. Kill a broker mid-run and watch produce latency during the failover and the re-replication afterwards.

What I would not do is take a published throughput number and plan against it. Those benchmarks are usually tail-read, large-batch, no-TLS, no-catch-up scenarios chosen to produce a big number; the useful figure is what your workload sustains on your hardware with a failure in progress.

Senior: Plan capacity for a new platform: 500,000 records/s peak at 800 bytes, RF 3, 7-day retention, four consumer groups.

Traffic: 500,000 × 800 B = 400 MB/s ingress uncompressed; with lz4 on JSON-like data, assume roughly 120 MB/s on the wire — and I would measure it rather than assume. Egress: replication 120 × 2 = 240 MB/s, consumers 120 × 4 = 480 MB/s, so about 720 MB/s across the cluster, plus catch-up reads.

Storage: 120 MB/s × 604,800 s = ~72 TB for 7 days, × 3 for replication = ~218 TB, × 1.4 headroom ≈ 300 TB.

Brokers: with a 10 Gb/s NIC (~1.2 GB/s) I would target no more than ~300 MB/s of egress per broker at steady state to leave failure headroom, so ~9 brokers on network grounds; 300 TB over 9 brokers is ~33 TB each, which argues for dense storage nodes or tiered storage for the older days. Rounding to 12 brokers across three zones gives the 50–60% utilisation that survives a zone loss and keeps per-broker storage reasonable.

Partitions: driven by the slowest consumer group's per-partition throughput, as the partitions lesson sets out — measured with the real handler, then given growth headroom, and kept within a per-broker partition budget verified by restart tests.

Controllers: three dedicated, one per zone.

Cost levers to apply from day one: follower fetching so consumer traffic stays in-zone, producer-side compression, and tiered storage for the tail of retention if the platform supports it.

Recommendation: 12 brokers plus 3 controllers across three zones, sized on measured compressed throughput, validated by a benchmark that includes a catch-up reader and a broker failure, and reviewed quarterly against actual peak growth.

Senior: The Kafka bill doubled while traffic grew 20%. Where do you look?

Traffic and cost have diverged, so something other than volume changed. I would decompose the bill before touching anything: compute, storage, network, and which of them grew.

Network is the usual answer, and specifically cross-zone transfer. New consumer groups are the common cause: each one adds a full copy of the topic's egress, and two-thirds of it crosses zones by default. Four groups instead of two doubles that line while ingress barely moves. The fix is follower fetching with client.rack, which keeps consumer reads in-zone, plus a check that producers actually compress.

Storage next: a retention change, compaction that stopped (the cleaner health checks from the compaction lesson), replication factor raised on a large topic, or a topic created with defaults that nobody reviewed. Days-to-full and per-topic disk usage answer this quickly.

Compute: brokers added to chase a latency problem whose real cause was something else — for example catch-up reads evicting the page cache, which more brokers only partly help.

And the non-technical causes: a cluster split in two for isolation, which duplicates headroom and controllers; or a move to a managed service, where the unit price is higher and the saving is in effort rather than on the bill.

Recommendation: report cost per TB ingested alongside the absolute figure, attribute egress per consumer group, and enable follower fetching. In my experience the combination of self-service consumer groups and default leader-only fetching explains most cost surprises of this shape.

6 (hard): Build a cost model and decide whether tiered storage pays.
Solution 
current: 12 brokers, 30 MB/s ingress on the wire, RF 3, 30-day retention
  storage on brokers   30 MB/s × 2.6 M s × 3 = 234 TB provisioned (with headroom ~330 TB)
  the last 27 days of that data are read by: an occasional backfill, and nothing else
OptionLocal diskObject storageNotes
Keep 30 days local~330 TB—Today's shape; storage dominates the bill
Tiered: 3 days local, 27 remote~33 TB~78 TB (RF 1 in object storage)Object storage is far cheaper per TB and not replicated 3×
Cut retention to 7 days~77 TB—Cheapest, but loses 23 days of replay
tiering also changes behaviour, not just cost:
  + brokers hold less data → faster re-replication, faster restarts, smaller failure blast radius
  + catch-up reads of old data no longer evict the page cache (they go to object storage)
  − reads of old data are slower and incur request costs
  − NOT available for compacted topics: those stay entirely local
  − another dependency in the read path, with its own failure modes to test

Recommendation: tier the large event topics, keep compacted state topics local, and treat the retention cut as a separate conversation with the teams that replay — it is an availability decision dressed as a cost one. Then re-run the model as cost per TB ingested, so the saving is visible even as volume grows.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • A cluster runs out of network, disk throughput, disk space or partition count — usually in that order.
  • Egress is ingress × (RF − 1) + ingress × consumer groups; 100 MB/s in can be 500 MB/s out.
  • Every new consumer group is a capacity and cross-zone cost decision, not a free subscription.
  • Size storage from compressed bytes, times retention, times replication factor, plus real headroom.
  • A full log directory takes a broker offline; alert on growth rate and days-to-full, not a percentage.
  • Compacted topics are sized by keys × value size, not by write rate.
  • Each partition costs file handles, batching efficiency, fetcher work and recovery time.
  • Unclean restart time scales with partition count — a reason to shut down cleanly and to raise recovery threads.
  • Keep a per-broker partition budget and treat topic creation as spending from it.
  • Benchmark with real record sizes, compression, acks and partition counts, for long enough to leave the page cache.
  • Read p99 and p99.9, and include a catch-up reader and a broker failure — that is the number capacity plans need.
  • Cold reads evict the page cache and raise everyone's tail latency; quota backfills.
  • Three-zone clusters should sit at 50–60% of capacity at peak, because a zone loss concentrates the rest.
  • Recovery after a failure can hurt more than the failure; throttle re-replication by default.
  • Cross-zone traffic can rival the brokers in cost; follower fetching and compression are the main levers.
  • Track cost per TB ingested, not the absolute bill, to see whether the platform is improving.
