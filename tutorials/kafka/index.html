What Kafka Actually Is Foundations

What you'll master: the distributed commit log, and why a log is not a queue — the single idea that explains almost every Kafka behaviour that surprises people; brokers, topics, partitions, replicas and the KRaft controller quorum that fully replaced ZooKeeper in Kafka 4.0; why Kafka is fast despite writing everything to disk, and the settings that quietly switch each speed-up off; retention decoupled from consumption, and the silent data skip when a slow consumer falls off the end of the log; exactly what Kafka guarantees and what it leaves to your code; and an honest test for when Kafka is the wrong tool. Prerequisites: none, though Pub/Sub & Queues gives useful context.

Part 1 — A Log, Not a Queue

Kafka stores records in an append-only log. Producers add to the end; every record gets a sequential number called its offset; nothing is modified in place and nothing is removed because someone read it. A consumer is just a program remembering a position in that log.

Partition 0 of topic "orders"

offset:    0      1      2      3      4      5      6      7
        ┌──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┬──────┐
        │ o-17 │ o-18 │ o-19 │ o-20 │ o-21 │ o-22 │ o-23 │ o-24 │ ◄── producers APPEND here
        └──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┴──────┘
                  ▲                           ▲
          billing group                analytics group
          next offset = 2              next offset = 6

READING MOVES A CONSUMER'S POSITION. IT NEVER REMOVES A RECORD.
Records disappear only when RETENTION says so — whether anyone read them or not.

Compare that with a traditional queue such as RabbitMQ or SQS, where the broker hands a message to one consumer, waits for an acknowledgement, and deletes it. The two models look similar on a whiteboard and behave completely differently in production.

🔑 Almost every Kafka surprise is this table. "Why can't I retry just this one message?" — because nothing tracks individual messages. "Why can't I add a twentieth consumer to my 12-partition topic?" — because a partition is the unit of parallelism. "Why did my new consumer read three days of history?" — because the history was still there. Kafka trades per-message bookkeeping for throughput, cheap fan-out and replay. The Spring Boot track's Messaging lesson shows the consumer-side consequences in application code; this track covers the machinery underneath.

The consequence people forget: consumers do not slow producers down. A queue with no consumers fills up and eventually pushes back. A Kafka topic with a stalled consumer keeps accepting writes at full speed, and the stalled group simply falls further behind — its lag grows. That isolation is valuable (a broken analytics job cannot take down checkout), but it also means nothing forces anyone to notice a dead consumer. Lag is the number you must alert on, because the system will not complain on your behalf.

Part 2 — The Moving Parts

A Kafka deployment is a cluster of broker processes. A topic is split into partitions; each partition is replicated to several brokers; one replica is the leader and serves all reads and writes for that partition, the others follow it.

KRaft replaced ZooKeeper. For most of Kafka's life, cluster metadata lived in a separate ZooKeeper ensemble. Since Kafka 3.3 the KRaft mode has been production-ready, and Kafka 4.0 (March 2025) removed ZooKeeper entirely: metadata is now itself a replicated log, __cluster_metadata, kept by a small quorum of controller nodes. A ZooKeeper-based cluster must be migrated to KRaft on a 3.x release — 3.9 is the bridge — before it can be upgraded to 4.x. Much of the material online still describes ZooKeeper; treat it as history.

How a client finds anything. You give a client a bootstrap.servers list, but that is only a starting point. The client asks any bootstrap broker for metadata, receives the full list of brokers and partition leaders — each described by the address that broker advertises — and then opens direct connections to those advertised addresses.

1. client ──► bootstrap broker "kafka:9092"        "give me metadata for topic orders"
2. broker ──► client     "orders-0 leader = node 1 at localhost:9092"   ◄── advertised.listeners
3. client ──► localhost:9092                        connects to ITSELF, not to the broker
   WARN Connection to node 1 (localhost/127.0.0.1:9092) could not be established.
        Node may not be available.

❌ "It connects, then every request times out." A broker in Docker or Kubernetes advertises an address that is only valid on its own host — commonly localhost. Bootstrap succeeds, because the bootstrap address you typed is reachable; then the metadata sends the client to an address it cannot reach, and produce or fetch calls hang until they time out.

✅ Advertise, per listener, the address each kind of client can actually reach. Define one listener for clients inside the network (kafka:19092) and another for clients outside it (localhost:9092 or a load-balancer name), and map them in advertised.listeners. Part 3 — Why It Is Fast

Kafka writes every record to disk and still moves hundreds of megabytes per second per broker. It does so by not fighting the operating system, and by never handling records one at a time.

1. Sequential appends. A partition is a set of segment files, and all writes go to the end of the newest one. Disks, including SSDs, are far faster at sequential I/O than at random I/O, and a tailing consumer reads sequentially too.

2. The operating system's page cache, not a JVM cache. Kafka does not keep records in its heap. Writes land in the page cache, and the kernel flushes them to disk when it chooses. Consumers that are keeping up read data written moments ago, which is still in the page cache, so a healthy cluster serves most fetches from memory without touching the disk.

producer ──► broker JVM ──► page cache ──(kernel flushes later)──► disk
                               │
tailing consumer ◄─────────────┘   served from RAM: written seconds ago, still cached

lagging consumer ◄──── disk ◄── old segments: cold reads, and they EVICT hot pages
                                 that the tailing consumers were about to read

Durability, then, does not come from fsync — by default Kafka leaves flushing to the OS. It comes from replication: an acknowledged record exists in the memory of several brokers on separate machines. That is a deliberate trade, and it is why the durability settings in the replication lesson matter so much.

3. Zero-copy transfer. For a plaintext connection, a broker can hand data from the page cache to the network socket with sendfile, so the bytes never enter user space. Enabling TLS disables this, because data has to be encrypted by the JVM before it is sent. That is not a reason to skip TLS — it is a reason to budget CPU for it and to measure the difference.

4. Batching and compression, end to end. Producers group records per partition into batches and compress each batch. The broker stores the batch as received (with the default topic setting compression.type=producer) and consumers decompress it. One request carries hundreds of records, and compression works far better on a batch than on single records.

Speed-upWhat it buysWhat quietly turns it off

🔑 Give a broker a modest heap and a lot of RAM. Because the page cache does the caching, a broker needs only a few gigabytes of heap; the remaining memory is what keeps tailing reads off the disk. A 64 GB machine running a 48 GB heap has left the kernel almost nothing to cache with, and every fetch becomes a disk read. It is one of the most common self-inflicted performance problems.

Part 4 — Retention: Storage Decoupled From Consumption

A record's lifetime is set by the topic's retention policy, never by whether anyone has read it. The defaults keep data for seven days (retention.ms = 7 days) with no size limit (retention.bytes=-1, applied per partition when set).

segment files for orders-0 (retention.ms = 7 days)

 00000000000000000000.log   closed, newest record 9 days old   ► DELETED at the next retention check
 00000000000001843002.log   closed, newest record 6 days old   ► kept
 00000000000003702415.log   ACTIVE, being written              ► never deleted while active

Retention deletes WHOLE, CLOSED segments. A quiet topic whose active segment never
fills can hold records well past retention.ms, until segment.ms (7 days) rolls it.

The dangerous interaction: a consumer that falls off the end of the log. If a group is stopped for longer than retention, the offset it wants to resume from no longer exists. The client then applies auto.offset.reset, and the default is latest.

group "billing" committed offset 1,200,000, then stopped for 9 days
retention deleted everything below offset 1,843,002

consumer restarts ─► asks for 1,200,000 ─► OFFSET_OUT_OF_RANGE
                 ─► auto.offset.reset = latest (DEFAULT)
                 ─► jumps to the END of the log
                 ─► every record from 1,843,002 up to the end is SKIPPED, with a single INFO log line

auto.offset.reset = earliest  ─► resumes at 1,843,002: nothing still retained is skipped (reprocesses; loses only the deleted range)
auto.offset.reset = none      ─► throws an exception: a human decides
auto.offset.resetWhen no valid offset exists, start at…Choose it for
latest (default)The end — only new recordsMetrics and live dashboards, where old data is worthless
earliestThe oldest retained recordAnything where missing a record is a bug; handlers must be idempotent
noneNowhere — throwFinancial or audit consumers, where a person should decide

Committed offsets also expire. The broker deletes a group's committed offsets once the group has had no members for offsets.retention.minutes, which defaults to 7 days. A batch job that runs monthly therefore comes back every month with no committed offsets at all — and auto.offset.reset decides what it reads. Exercise 6 walks through recovering from exactly this.

Sizing retention is arithmetic, not a guess. Disk needed is ingress rate × retention × replication factor, plus headroom:

  50 MB/s ingress × 604,800 s (7 days) = 30.2 TB of log
  × replication factor 3               = 90.7 TB across the cluster
  + ~30% headroom for bursts, rebalancing and a lost broker's data moving elsewhere

Two features change that arithmetic, and later lessons cover both. Log compaction keeps only the latest record per key instead of deleting by age — the right model for "current state" topics. Tiered storage (production-ready since Kafka 3.9) moves closed segments to object storage such as S3, so brokers keep only recent data on local disk while consumers can still read the old data.

Part 5 — What Kafka Guarantees, and What It Leaves to You

Kafka's guarantees are real but narrow, and each one depends on configuration. The honest version, with the condition that breaks each:

GuaranteeHolds whenBreaks when
Order within a partitionOne producer, idempotence on (default since 3.0)You compare records in different partitions; you add partitions, which remaps keys
An acknowledged write survives a broker lossacks=all, replication factor 3, min.insync.replicas=2acks=1 and the leader dies before replicating; min.insync.replicas=1 (the default) lets the ISR shrink to the leader alone

Two defaults deserve suspicion. min.insync.replicas=1 means that even with acks=all, a partition whose followers have all fallen behind accepts writes held by the leader alone — lose that broker and the "acknowledged" writes are gone. And auto.create.topics.enable=true on the broker means a typo in a topic name creates a new topic with default settings (often a replication factor of 1) instead of failing. Production clusters usually set min.insync.replicas=2 and disable auto-creation.

kafka-topics.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --create --topic orders \
  --partitions 12 --replication-factor 3 \
  --config min.insync.replicas=2

# producer (Java client, 3.0+ defaults shown explicitly so a reviewer can see them)
acks=all
enable.idempotence=true
🔑 "Exactly-once" in Kafka is scoped. Transactions make a consume-transform-produce loop atomic inside Kafka: the output records and the input offsets commit together. The moment a step writes to a database or calls an API, you are back to at-least-once and need idempotent handlers. The System Design lesson on queues covers the outbox pattern that closes that gap.

Part 6 — When Kafka Is the Right Tool, and When It Is Not

Kafka is excellent at a specific shape of problem and awkward outside it. The test is whether you need what the log gives you: retention, replay, many independent readers, or throughput beyond one machine.

WorkloadKafka?Why
Events consumed by several teamsYesEach team is one more consumer group, at no extra storage
Change data capture, audit trailsYesOrdered per key, replayable, retained
Log and metrics pipelines at high volumeYesBatching and partitioning are built for this
Task queue with per-job retry, delay, priorityUsually noNo per-message ack, delay or priority; one slow job blocks its partition. SQS or RabbitMQ fit better (Kafka's newer share groups are narrowing this gap — check their status in your version)
Request/response between servicesNoYou would rebuild RPC with correlation ids and reply topics; use HTTP or gRPC
A few hundred events a day, one consumerNoA database table with an outbox poller is cheaper to run and easier to reason about
Looking up an entity by keyNoA log has no index by key; use a database, fed from Kafka if you like

The cost is operational. A self-run cluster means controller quorums, broker disks, partition rebalancing, upgrades, ACLs and monitoring. Managed services (Amazon MSK, Confluent Cloud, Aiven and others) take much of that away but not the design work: partition counts, keys, schemas and consumer behaviour are still yours. Small teams with modest volume often do better starting with a managed service, or with a queue, and moving to Kafka when a concrete requirement — replay, fan-out, throughput — appears.

❌ Adopting Kafka as "the message bus" and then using it as a job queue. A team puts image-resize jobs on a 6-partition topic. One oversized image takes 20 minutes; every job behind it in that partition waits, a sixth of the throughput stops, and there is no way to retry just that job later without writing retry topics by hand. Adding consumers does nothing past six.

✅ Match the tool to the delivery model you need. Per-job leasing, retries and delays are queue features: use SQS, RabbitMQ, or a database-backed job table for that work, and keep Kafka for streams of facts that many readers consume in order. If jobs must flow through Kafka, design retry and dead-letter topics deliberately, and size partitions for the parallelism you need.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is Kafka, and how is it different from a traditional message queue?

Kafka is a distributed, replicated, append-only log. Producers append records to topics, which are split into partitions; each record gets an offset; consumers read by tracking their position. The central difference from a queue is that reading does not remove anything. Records stay until the topic's retention deletes them, regardless of who has read them.

That one difference changes most behaviours. In a queue, the broker tracks each message — leased, acknowledged, deleted — and can retry or dead-letter a single message. Kafka tracks only a committed offset per partition per consumer group, so it cannot retry one record on its own; a record your code cannot handle blocks the partition until the code deals with it. In exchange, fan-out is free (another team is another consumer group reading the same data), replay is possible (move the offset back), and the broker does very little bookkeeping, which is part of why it scales to very high throughput.

Parallelism also works differently. A queue can be drained by any number of consumers. In Kafka, each partition is read by exactly one member of a consumer group, so the partition count caps a group's parallelism — but in exchange you get strict ordering within each partition.

The short way to say it in an interview: a queue distributes work; Kafka distributes a history that many readers consume at their own pace. I would pick a queue for jobs that need per-message retries and Kafka for events that several systems need, possibly more than once.

Beginner: What is a partition, and why does Kafka split topics into them?

A partition is one ordered, append-only log. A topic is really just a name for a set of partitions, and each record lives in exactly one of them — chosen by hashing the record's key, or spread across partitions when there is no key.

Partitions exist for three reasons. Scale: different partitions can live on different brokers, so a topic's throughput and storage are not limited to one machine. Parallelism: a consumer group assigns each partition to one member, so twelve partitions allow up to twelve consumers working at once. Replication: each partition is copied to several brokers, with one leader and several followers, which is how Kafka survives a broker failing.

The cost is ordering. Kafka guarantees order only within a partition. Two records with the same key land in the same partition and stay in order; two records with different keys may land in different partitions, and there is no defined order between them. That is why choosing the key is a design decision — it decides both what stays ordered and how evenly the load spreads.

One practical warning: you can add partitions to a topic but never remove them, and adding them changes which partition each key hashes to. Records for an existing key can then appear in two partitions at once, so per-key ordering breaks at the moment of the change. It is worth choosing a partition count with headroom up front.

Mid: A consumer restarts after being down for ten days, reports no errors, and has clearly missed data. What happened?

Two separate expirations are likely at work, and both end in the same silent behaviour.

First, the committed offsets may be gone. The broker deletes a group's committed offsets after the group has had no members for offsets.retention.minutes — 7 days by default. After ten days down, the group comes back with no committed position at all.

Second, even if the offsets survived, the data may not have. With the default retention.ms of 7 days, the segments holding the records after the group's last position have been deleted, so the stored offset is now out of range.

In both cases the client falls back to auto.offset.reset. Its default, latest, moves the consumer to the end of the log. Everything between is skipped, and the only trace is an INFO-level log line about resetting the offset. No error, no exception. I would confirm it with kafka-consumer-groups.sh --describe (to see whether the group has offsets) and the topic's earliest offsets (kafka-get-offsets.sh --time -2).

Recovery depends on what is still retained: reset the group to a timestamp with --reset-offsets --to-datetime and let it reprocess, with idempotent handlers. Prevention is configuration: auto.offset.reset=earliest or none for consumers where missing data is a bug, retention longer than any plausible outage, and a lag alert so a stopped consumer is noticed in hours rather than days.

Mid: Kafka writes everything to disk. Why is it still fast?

Because it avoids the things that make disk-based systems slow: random I/O, per-record work and extra copies.

All writes are sequential appends to the active segment of each partition, and tailing consumers read sequentially. Sequential I/O runs close to a device's maximum throughput on both spinning disks and SSDs.

Kafka relies on the operating system's page cache instead of caching in its own heap. Writes go into the page cache and the kernel flushes them later; consumers that are keeping up read data written moments ago, which is still in memory. Durability comes from replicating to other brokers rather than from calling fsync on every write. That is also why brokers run a small heap and leave most RAM to the OS.

Zero-copy: on plaintext connections the broker sends data from the page cache to the socket with sendfile, without copying it into the JVM. Batching and compression end to end: producers send compressed batches, the broker stores them unchanged, consumers decompress. One request moves hundreds of records.

Each of these can be switched off without anyone noticing: TLS disables zero-copy; a topic compression setting that differs from the producer's forces recompression; an oversized heap starves the page cache; lagging consumers reading old data evict pages the tailing consumers need. When a cluster is slower than expected, I check those four first.

Senior: A team wants Kafka as the queue for image-processing jobs that need retries, delays and priorities. Evaluate the proposal.

I would push back, because the requirements are queue features and Kafka's delivery model lacks each of them.

Retries. Kafka commits a position, not individual messages. A failed job cannot be retried on its own without blocking the partition behind it, so you end up building retry topics — a 5-second topic, a 1-minute topic, a dead-letter topic — each with its own consumer. That works, but it gives up ordering and adds moving parts. Delays: Kafka has no delayed delivery; a retry topic's consumer has to pause until each record is due, which also blocks the records behind it. Priorities: none; the closest thing is separate topics with separate consumer capacity. Head-of-line blocking: image sizes vary widely, and one 20-minute job stalls its whole partition. Parallelism is capped by partition count, so bursty work needs over-partitioning up front.

What Kafka would add — retention, replay, fan-out to other readers — the proposal does not need. A job queue is usually read by one kind of worker, and once a job has succeeded nobody wants to replay it.

Kafka 4.x introduced share groups (KIP-932), which add queue-like, per-record acknowledgement to Kafka. I would evaluate their maturity in the version we would actually run before relying on them, and I would not choose Kafka for this workload on their strength alone today.

Recommendation: SQS (or RabbitMQ, if we run it already) for the jobs, with visibility timeouts, a dead-letter queue and delay queues built in. If other systems need to know that an image was processed, publish a small "image processed" event to Kafka after each job succeeds. The queue does the work distribution and Kafka carries the facts — each tool doing what it is built for.

Senior: A producer received acknowledgements, a broker then died, and the acknowledged records are missing. How is that possible, and what prevents it?

An acknowledgement means only what the configuration says it means. Three configurations produce exactly this failure.

1. acks=1. The leader acknowledges as soon as the record is in its own log. If it dies before any follower has fetched the record, a follower becomes leader without it, and the record is gone. The producer was told it succeeded.

2. acks=all with min.insync.replicas=1. acks=all waits for every replica in the ISR — but if the followers have fallen behind and been removed from the ISR, the ISR is just the leader. With the default min.insync.replicas=1, the leader keeps accepting acks=all writes on its own. Lose it, and the result is the same as case 1.

3. Unclean leader election. With unclean.leader.election.enable=true, an out-of-sync replica is allowed to become leader when no in-sync replica is available. Availability is restored, but everything the new leader never received is truncated away. The default is false, but it is sometimes turned on during an outage and forgotten.

The configuration that prevents it: replication factor 3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all (the producer default since 3.0), and unclean election disabled. Then an acknowledged record exists on at least two brokers, and if the ISR shrinks below two the leader refuses writes with NotEnoughReplicas rather than quietly accepting weaker durability. That trade — refusing writes rather than losing them — is the one to state explicitly to the business.

Recommendation: make RF=3 and min.insync.replicas=2 the cluster defaults, leave unclean election off, alert on under-replicated partitions and on ISR shrinks, and treat any acks=1 producer as a documented exception for data you can afford to lose.

    --group billing --property print.partition=true

# terminal C: a DIFFERENT group
$ kafka-console-consumer.sh --bootstrap-server localhost:9092 --topic orders \
    --group analytics --from-beginning --property print.partition=true

billing  orders  2          1               1               0    console-...-7c2   ...

Produce ten more keyed records. Terminal C (analytics) prints all ten. Terminals A and B together print the same ten, with each record in exactly one of them: A owns partitions 0 and 1, B owns partition 2. Start a third member of billing and it takes one partition from A; start a fourth and it gets nothing, because there are only three partitions to share.

Note one detail of the console tool: --from-beginning only applies when the group has no committed offsets. Run terminal C a second time with the same group and it resumes where it stopped instead of starting over — the committed offset takes precedence.

Note that the committed offset is the next offset to read, not the last one processed. A group at offset 3 on a partition holding offsets 0–2 is fully caught up. Getting this off by one in hand-written offset management is a classic way to reprocess or skip exactly one record after every restart.

4 (medium): Make a slow consumer fall off the end of the log, and compare auto.offset.reset=latest with earliest.
Solution 
# a topic with 1-minute retention and 10-second segments
$ kt --create --topic short --partitions 1 --replication-factor 1 \
    --config retention.ms=60000 --config segment.ms=10000

$ seq 1 100 | kafka-console-producer.sh --bootstrap-server localhost:9092 --topic short
$ kafka-console-consumer.sh --bootstrap-server localhost:9092 --topic short \
    --group slow --from-beginning --max-messages 10
# offset committed at 10; now produce more over the next few minutes
$ for i in $(seq 101 400); do echo $i; sleep 1; done | kafka-console-producer.sh \
    --bootstrap-server localhost:9092 --topic short

$ kafka-get-offsets.sh --bootstrap-server localhost:9092 --topic short --time -2

$ kafka-console-consumer.sh ... --group slow --consumer-property auto.offset.reset=latest
(prints nothing until new records arrive — it jumped to the end; offsets 10-235 deleted, 236 onward skipped)

$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh ... --group slow --reset-offsets --to-offset 10 --topic short --execute

5 (hard): A client container on the same Docker network as the broker bootstraps successfully, then every produce call times out. Reproduce it and fix it.
Solution 
$ docker network create kafkanet
$ docker run -d --name kafka --network kafkanet -p 9092:9092 apache/kafka:4.0.0

Bootstrap to kafka:9092 worked. The metadata response then told the client that node 1 lives at localhost:9092 — the image's default advertised listener — and inside the client container localhost is the client itself. The fix is a listener per network, each advertising an address that its clients can reach:

$ docker rm -f kafka

  -e KAFKA_PROCESS_ROLES=broker,controller \

  -e KAFKA_ADVERTISED_LISTENERS=PLAINTEXT://kafka:19092,PLAINTEXT_HOST://localhost:9092 \
  -e KAFKA_LISTENER_SECURITY_PROTOCOL_MAP=CONTROLLER:PLAINTEXT,PLAINTEXT:PLAINTEXT,PLAINTEXT_HOST:PLAINTEXT \
  -e KAFKA_CONTROLLER_QUORUM_VOTERS=1@kafka:9093 \
  -e KAFKA_CONTROLLER_LISTENER_NAMES=CONTROLLER \
  -e KAFKA_INTER_BROKER_LISTENER_NAME=PLAINTEXT \
  -e KAFKA_OFFSETS_TOPIC_REPLICATION_FACTOR=1 \
  -e KAFKA_TRANSACTION_STATE_LOG_REPLICATION_FACTOR=1 \
  -e KAFKA_TRANSACTION_STATE_LOG_MIN_ISR=1 \

6 (hard): A monthly reconciliation job ran today and processed only the last hour of events. Diagnose it and recover the month.
Solution 
$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --describe --group recon-monthly
Consumer group 'recon-monthly' has no active members.

GROUP          TOPIC     PARTITION  CURRENT-OFFSET  LOG-END-OFFSET  LAG
recon-monthly  payments  0          8812044         8812044         0
recon-monthly  payments  1          8790321         8790321         0

LAG 0 looks healthy, and that is the trap. The group was empty for about 30 days, far past offsets.retention.minutes (7 days), so its committed offsets were deleted. On this run the consumer found no offsets, applied its auto.offset.reset=latest, started at the end, read one hour of new data, and committed — so the offsets shown are from today's run, not last month's.

Recovery, if the data is still retained (check the topic's retention.ms first):

# the group must have no active members while its offsets are reset
$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --group recon-monthly \
    --topic payments --reset-offsets --to-datetime 2026-08-01T00:00:00.000 --dry-run
GROUP          TOPIC     PARTITION  NEW-OFFSET
recon-monthly  payments  0          7104551
recon-monthly  payments  1          7089930

$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh ... --reset-offsets --to-datetime 2026-08-01T00:00:00.000 --execute

Then rerun the job. It reprocesses the hour it already handled, so the job's writes must be idempotent — keyed upserts, not appends.

FixPrevents
Job seeks by timestamp itself (offsetsForTimes) at startDependence on committed offsets surviving a month
auto.offset.reset=noneA silent jump to the end — the job fails loudly instead
Topic retention of at least 40 daysThe source data expiring before the job runs

The underlying lesson: committed offsets are designed for continuously running consumers. A job that runs occasionally should decide its starting point explicitly — by timestamp or by a position it stores itself — rather than trusting a mechanism that expires while it is away.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Kafka is an append-only log, not a queue: reading moves a consumer's offset and never deletes a record.
  • Records live until retention removes them — 7 days by default — regardless of whether anyone read them.
  • A partition is the unit of ordering, parallelism and replication; order holds only inside one partition.
  • A consumer group uses at most one consumer per partition; extra consumers sit idle.
  • Fan-out is free: another team is another consumer group reading the same data, with no copies.
  • Consumers never slow producers down, so a dead consumer is silent — alert on lag.
  • Kafka 4.0 is KRaft-only: metadata is a replicated log kept by a controller quorum, and ZooKeeper clusters must migrate on 3.x first.
  • Clients connect to the addresses brokers advertise, so a wrong advertised.listeners means "bootstrap works, everything else times out".
  • Kafka is fast because of sequential appends, the page cache, zero-copy and end-to-end batching; TLS, recompression, huge heaps and lagging readers each quietly undo one.
  • Durability comes from replication, not fsync — which is why the replication settings are the durability settings.
  • Retention deletes whole, closed segments, so quiet topics can keep data longer than retention.ms.
  • auto.offset.reset=latest is the default, and it silently skips data when an offset is missing or out of range.
  • Committed offsets expire 7 days after a group empties, so occasional jobs should choose their start position explicitly.
  • min.insync.replicas=1 lets acks=all mean "the leader alone"; use RF 3 with min.insync.replicas=2.
  • Kafka's exactly-once stops at Kafka's edge; database writes and API calls still need idempotency.
  • Choose Kafka for retention, replay, fan-out or throughput — and a queue for per-job retries, delays and priorities.
