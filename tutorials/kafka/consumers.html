Consumers & Consumer Groups Foundations

What you'll master: what poll() really does — group membership, fetching ahead and handing you a batch — and why a KafkaConsumer must never be shared between threads; a shutdown that leaves the group cleanly instead of stalling it; how the group coordinator and assignors divide partitions, and why consumers beyond the partition count are idle; the fetch settings and the arithmetic that decides a consumer's memory; the difference between position, committed offset and lag, and why lag in time is the number to alert on; the max.poll.interval.ms budget your processing must fit inside; ways to go faster than one record at a time without losing order; and follower fetching, which can remove most of a cluster's cross-zone traffic bill. Prerequisites: Producers: Batching, Keys & Partitioners.

Part 1 — The Poll Loop

Every Kafka consumer is built around one loop: call poll(), process what it returns, repeat. It looks like a simple fetch, but poll() is where the consumer does nearly all of its work.

consumer.subscribe(List.of("orders"));
try {
    while (running) {
        ConsumerRecords<String, String> batch = consumer.poll(Duration.ofMillis(500));
        for (ConsumerRecord<String, String> r : batch) {
            handle(r);                        // your processing
        }
        consumer.commitSync();                // commit after processing (details: offsets lesson)
    }
} catch (WakeupException e) {
    // expected on shutdown
} finally {
    consumer.close();                         // leave the group NOW, not after a timeout
}
Inside poll()Why it matters
Joins the group and handles rebalancesThe first poll() is what actually makes the consumer a group member; partitions are assigned there
Proves the consumer is still making progressCalling poll() less often than max.poll.interval.ms (5 min) gets the consumer removed from the group
Returns records already fetched, and fetches aheadRecords are buffered in the client; the next fetch is usually in flight while you process
Commits offsets automatically, if auto-commit is onAuto-commit happens during poll(), for records the previous poll() returned
Returns at most max.poll.records (500)This caps how much work each loop iteration takes on

A KafkaConsumer is not thread-safe. Calling it from two threads throws ConcurrentModificationException. The only method safe to call from another thread is wakeup(), which makes a blocked poll() throw WakeupException — the standard way to stop the loop from a shutdown hook. The usual model is one consumer per thread, and several threads (or processes) in the same group to scale out.

Runtime.getRuntime().addShutdownHook(new Thread(() -> {
    running = false;
    consumer.wakeup();          // the ONLY consumer method safe from another thread
    mainThread.join(10_000);    // let the loop finish its batch, commit and close()
}));
🔑 close() is what makes a restart fast. A consumer that closes sends a "leave group" message, and the group reassigns its partitions immediately. A consumer that simply disappears — killed, crashed, or shut down without close() — is only noticed when its session times out (session.timeout.ms, 45 s by default since Kafka 3.0), and until then its partitions are assigned to nobody. On every rolling deploy, that is 45 seconds of those partitions not being read.

Part 2 — Groups and Partition Assignment

Consumers that share a group.id form a group. One broker acts as the group's coordinator: it tracks members and stores the group's committed offsets in the internal __consumer_offsets topic. When members join or leave, the group rebalances and the partitions are divided again.

topic "orders", 6 partitions, group "billing"

2 consumers:   C1 ◄ p0 p1 p2         C2 ◄ p3 p4 p5
3 consumers:   C1 ◄ p0 p1   C2 ◄ p2 p3   C3 ◄ p4 p5
6 consumers:   one partition each
8 consumers:   6 busy, C7 and C8 IDLE — a partition is never split between members of one group
AssignorHow it dividesWatch out for
Range (Java default, first in the list)Per topic, contiguous ranges to members in sorted orderWith many topics of few partitions, the first members get more partitions of every topic
Round-robinAll partitions dealt out in turnMoves many partitions on every rebalance
StickyBalanced, keeping previous assignments where possibleStill stops all consumption during a rebalance (eager)
Cooperative stickySticky, and only revoked partitions pauseEvery member must support it before switching; see the rebalancing lesson

Range's imbalance shows up with many topics. A group reading ten topics of three partitions each, with two members, gives the first member partitions 0 and 1 of every topic — 20 partitions — and the second only partition 2 of each, 10. Nothing is wrong per topic, but one consumer carries twice the load. Cooperative sticky balances across the whole subscription.

A newer group protocol exists. Kafka 4.0 made the next-generation consumer rebalance protocol (KIP-848) generally available: the broker computes assignments, and members change partitions incrementally, without the whole group stopping. Consumers opt in with group.protocol=consumer, and under it some familiar client settings (such as session.timeout.ms) move to the broker. The rebalancing lesson covers both protocols; the rest of this lesson applies to either.

Part 3 — Fetching, and the Memory It Costs

Consumers pull. A fetch request asks each broker for data from the partitions it leads, and the settings decide how long the broker may wait to fill the response and how big it may be.

SettingDefaultEffect
fetch.min.bytes1 byteBroker answers as soon as any data exists; raise it to get fewer, fuller responses
fetch.max.wait.ms500 msHow long the broker may wait to reach fetch.min.bytes
max.partition.fetch.bytes1 MBPer-partition limit in one response
fetch.max.bytes50 MBLimit for the whole response from one broker
max.poll.records500How many buffered records one poll() hands you; does not change what is fetched

A single large record is never stuck. If the first record batch in a partition is bigger than max.partition.fetch.bytes or fetch.max.bytes, the broker returns it anyway so the consumer can make progress. The limits are targets for normal traffic, not hard caps.

Consumer memory is fetch size times partitions. The client buffers fetched data, and a consumer assigned many partitions can hold a lot of it:

one consumer assigned 400 partitions (a small group reading a big topic)
worst case buffered ≈ min(partitions × max.partition.fetch.bytes, brokers × fetch.max.bytes)
                    = min(400 × 1 MB, 6 brokers × 50 MB) = min(400 MB, 300 MB) = 300 MB
plus decompressed copies of those records while you process them

a container with a 512 MB memory limit and a 256 MB heap → OutOfMemoryError under catch-up load,
but NOT in steady state, when each fetch is small. It fails exactly when lag is high.

❌ Sizing a consumer's memory from a quiet day. In steady state each fetch returns a few kilobytes and the heap is nearly empty. After an outage the consumer is far behind, every fetch comes back full, and the same consumer runs out of memory — so it restarts, stays behind, and fails again. The catch-up that should drain the backlog cannot happen.

✅ Size for catch-up. Compute the worst case from partitions per consumer and the fetch limits, then either give the heap that much room or lower max.partition.fetch.bytes and fetch.max.bytes. Test by resetting a consumer group far back and watching its memory while it catches up.

Lag in records is hard to judge; lag in time is not. A lag of 50,000 records is nothing on a topic receiving 100,000 per second and a disaster on one receiving 10 per minute. What matters to the business is how old the data being processed is. Measure the age of the record currently being processed (now minus its timestamp), or estimate time lag from the record lag and the partition's recent write rate. Tools such as Burrow or Kafka exporters for Prometheus compute this; the monitoring lesson goes further.

Look at lag per partition. The group's total lag hides the pattern that matters: lag on one partition points to a hot key or one slow or stuck consumer, while lag spread evenly across all partitions means the group as a whole cannot keep up.

Part 5 — Processing Speed and the Poll Deadline

A group's maximum throughput is roughly partitions × (records one consumer can process per second on one partition). When processing each record means a database write or an API call, that per-partition rate is the real limit — and there is a deadline attached.

max.poll.interval.ms = 300,000 (5 min): the longest allowed gap between two poll() calls
one poll() returns up to max.poll.records = 500

500 records × 20 ms each  = 10 s per loop     ✔ well inside 5 min
500 records × 800 ms each = 400 s per loop    ✘ over 300 s → the consumer is REMOVED from the group,
                                                its partitions go to another member, its commit fails,
                                                and the same records are processed again elsewhere

The Spring Boot track's Messaging lesson walks through how this becomes a repeating cycle of rebalances. The fixes are to make each loop shorter — lower max.poll.records so a batch fits comfortably, or make per-record work faster — and only then, if needed, raise max.poll.interval.ms, which also delays detection of a consumer that is genuinely stuck.

Way to go fasterKeeps per-key order?Cost
More partitions and consumersYesRepartitioning a keyed topic moves keys (see the partitions lesson)

Keyed parallelism inside a consumer is often the best option when processing is slow and the topic's partition count cannot easily change. The consumer hands each record to one of N workers chosen by hashing its key, so records for one key stay in order on one worker, while different keys run in parallel. The hard part is offsets: you may only commit up to the lowest offset that every worker has finished, or a crash skips records. Confluent's open-source parallel consumer library implements this; if you build it yourself, test the crash cases deliberately.

poll() ─► records ─► hash(key) % 8 ─► worker queues 0..7   (one key → always the same worker)
                                         │
committed offset per partition = the lowest offset NOT yet finished by any worker:
   finished: 100 101 102   104 105     (103 still running)
   commit 103  ◄── not 106: after a crash, 103 must be processed again

Pausing instead of blocking. If a downstream system is slow and the consumer must hold back, consumer.pause(partitions) stops fetching for those partitions while the loop keeps calling poll() — which keeps the consumer in the group. Blocking inside the loop instead (a long sleep, a queue that fills and blocks) risks exceeding the poll deadline.

Part 6 — Where Consumers Read From, and What It Costs

By default a consumer fetches each partition from its leader. In a cluster spread across three availability zones, leaders are spread evenly, so about two-thirds of every consumer's reads cross a zone boundary — and cloud providers charge for that traffic in both directions.

consumed volume: 100 MB/s per group × 3 groups = 300 MB/s
cross-zone share with leader-only fetching ≈ 2/3  → 200 MB/s
per month: 200 MB/s × 2,592,000 s = 518 TB
at $0.01/GB each way ($0.02/GB round), a typical inter-zone price: ≈ $10,400 per month

Follower fetching (KIP-392, Kafka 2.4) lets a consumer read from a replica in its own zone. Brokers set broker.rack and replica.selector.class=org.apache.kafka.common.replica.RackAwareReplicaSelector; consumers set client.rack to their zone. Reads still stop at the high watermark, so consistency is unchanged; the trade-off is slightly higher end-to-end latency, because a follower learns the new high watermark a little after the leader does.

SettingWherePurpose
broker.rackEach brokerIts zone; also spreads replicas across zones
replica.selector.classBrokersLets the leader point a consumer at a same-zone replica
client.rackEach consumerThe consumer's own zone
isolation.levelConsumersread_uncommitted (default) or read_committed for transactional topics

Subscribe versus assign. subscribe() uses group management: partitions are assigned, rebalanced and shared with other members. assign() takes specific partitions with no group coordination at all — useful for tools, for replaying one partition, or for systems that manage placement themselves. A consumer using assign() can still commit offsets under a group id, but nothing stops two assign() consumers from reading and committing the same partition.

Interview Questions

Beginner: How does a consumer group divide work?

Consumers with the same group.id share a topic's partitions: each partition is assigned to exactly one member of the group at a time, and each member may hold several partitions. A broker acting as the group coordinator tracks the members, and when one joins or leaves, the group rebalances and redistributes the partitions.

Two consequences follow. The partition count caps a group's parallelism — with six partitions, a seventh consumer gets nothing and sits idle. And ordering is preserved per partition, because a partition's records are only ever processed by one consumer at a time.

Different groups are completely independent: each group has its own committed offsets and receives every record. That is how several teams read the same topic without affecting each other — the billing group and the analytics group each see the whole stream, at their own pace.

How partitions are divided depends on the assignor. The Java client's default list starts with the range assignor; cooperative sticky assignment is the better choice for most groups because it balances across topics and lets unaffected partitions keep flowing during a rebalance.

Beginner: What is consumer lag, and how should you monitor it?

Lag is how far a consumer group is behind the end of a partition: the partition's end offset (the high watermark) minus the group's committed offset. It is measured per partition, and kafka-consumer-groups.sh --describe shows it.

Lag matters because Kafka never pushes back on producers. A stopped consumer does not slow anything down; its lag simply grows until retention deletes data it never read. Lag is the only signal that a consumer is falling behind.

Two refinements make it useful. First, look at it per partition: lag concentrated on one partition means a hot key or a stuck consumer, while even lag means the whole group is too slow. Second, express it in time rather than records. "8 minutes behind" means the same thing on every topic; "50,000 records behind" does not. Time lag can be measured from the timestamp of the record being processed, or estimated from the record lag and the write rate.

The alert I would set is on time lag per group, with a threshold derived from the business requirement — for example, alert when the order-confirmation consumer is more than two minutes behind — and a second alert if lag keeps growing for longer than a short window, which catches a consumer that has stopped entirely.

Mid: Why does a consumer get kicked out of its group while it is clearly still running?

Because a consumer has two separate liveness checks, and "running" only satisfies one of them.

The first is the session: the consumer sends heartbeats from a background thread, and if the coordinator hears nothing for session.timeout.ms (45 s by default), the member is removed. That catches crashed processes and network partitions. A consumer that is alive but busy still heartbeats, so it passes this check.

The second is progress: the consumer must call poll() again within max.poll.interval.ms (5 minutes). If processing the last batch takes longer, the client itself leaves the group, the partitions go to another member, and when the slow consumer finally tries to commit, the commit fails because it no longer owns those partitions. The records are processed again by the new owner.

So the usual cause is a batch that takes too long: 500 records at 800 ms each is 400 seconds. The fix is to fit the batch inside the budget — reduce max.poll.records, make per-record work faster or batch it — and only then consider raising max.poll.interval.ms. Other causes worth checking are long garbage-collection pauses and code in the loop that blocks on a downstream system instead of pausing partitions.

Mid: A consumer runs fine for weeks, then crashes with OutOfMemoryError every time it restarts after an outage. Explain.

The consumer's memory use depends on how much data each fetch returns, and that depends on lag. In steady state it is caught up, so each fetch returns only the few records produced since the last one. After an outage it is far behind, and every fetch comes back as large as the limits allow.

The worst case is roughly the smaller of partitions × max.partition.fetch.bytes and brokers × fetch.max.bytes. A consumer holding 400 partitions with 1 MB per-partition limits can buffer hundreds of megabytes, plus decompressed copies of records while they are processed. A heap sized from a quiet day cannot hold that, so the consumer dies, restarts, is still behind, and dies again — it can never catch up.

The fixes are to make the worst case fit: fewer partitions per consumer (more consumers in the group), lower max.partition.fetch.bytes and fetch.max.bytes, a lower max.poll.records if per-record processing holds on to memory, or simply a larger heap. The test that proves it is to reset a group far back in a staging environment and watch memory during catch-up.

The broader lesson is that consumers must be sized for their worst operating condition — catching up after an incident — because that is exactly when they are needed most.

Senior: A consumer group writes each record to Postgres, handles 50 records per second per partition, and needs to go ten times faster. What are the options?

50 per second per partition is about 20 ms per record, which is a synchronous database round trip per record. The options differ in what they cost and whether they preserve per-key ordering.

1. Batch the writes. Instead of one insert per record, write each poll() batch with one multi-row insert or COPY, then commit offsets. A batch of 500 rows often takes little longer than a single row, so this alone can give far more than ten times. Ordering is kept, and the only new complexity is handling a batch that partly fails — usually by retrying the whole batch with idempotent (upsert) writes.

2. Parallelise by key inside each consumer. Route records by key hash to a pool of workers, each with its own database connection. Per-key order holds, throughput scales with the pool size, and the topic does not change. The cost is offset management: commit only below the oldest record still being processed. A library such as Confluent's parallel consumer handles this.

3. Add partitions and consumers. Ten times the partitions gives ten times the parallel consumers. It is conceptually simple but changes the key-to-partition mapping for a keyed topic, needs ten times the consumer instances and database connections, and cannot be undone.

4. Check the database side. If Postgres itself is the bottleneck — lock contention, missing indexes, synchronous replication — none of the above will help much, and more parallelism can make contention worse.

Recommendation: start with batching, because it attacks the actual cost (round trips per record) and keeps the design simple; measure; add keyed parallelism if the batch approach is not enough; and treat repartitioning as the last resort. Throughout, make the writes idempotent, since every option reprocesses some records after a rebalance or crash.

Senior: The cloud bill shows Kafka's cross-zone data transfer costing more than the brokers. How do you reduce it?

First find which traffic crosses zones. There are three sources: producers writing to leaders in other zones, replication from leaders to followers (which, with replicas spread across three zones for resilience, is inherently cross-zone), and consumers fetching from leaders in other zones. With three zones and leaders spread evenly, about two-thirds of both produce and consume traffic crosses zones, and consumer traffic is multiplied by the number of consumer groups.

Consumers are usually the biggest and easiest part. Follower fetching lets each consumer read from a replica in its own zone: set broker.rack on brokers, replica.selector.class to the rack-aware selector, and client.rack on consumers. With three groups each consuming 100 MB/s, that removes roughly 200 MB/s of cross-zone traffic — about 518 TB a month. The cost is slightly higher end-to-end latency, since followers learn the high watermark a moment later than the leader; consistency is unaffected because reads still stop at the high watermark.

Replication traffic is the price of surviving a zone loss, and I would not reduce the replication factor to save it. Compression does reduce it — producers compressing with zstd or lz4 shrink every copy — so it is worth checking that producers actually compress and that batches are big enough for compression to work.

Producer traffic can be reduced only partly, because a partition's leader is in one zone. For unkeyed data, producers can prefer partitions led in their own zone; for keyed data the key decides.

Recommendation: enable follower fetching for every consumer group first, since it is a configuration change with a large effect; make sure producers compress effectively; keep replication across zones; and track cross-zone bytes as a metric so the next new consumer group does not undo the saving.

5 (hard): Speed up a slow consumer ten times with keyed parallel workers, without breaking per-key order or skipping records after a crash.
Solution 
// sketch: one partition shown; real code tracks every assigned partition
int W = 8;
ExecutorService[] workers = new ExecutorService[W];          // each single-threaded
for (int i = 0; i < W; i++) workers[i] = Executors.newSingleThreadExecutor();
ConcurrentSkipListSet<Long> inFlight = new ConcurrentSkipListSet<>();

while (running) {
    ConsumerRecords<String, String> batch = consumer.poll(Duration.ofMillis(200));
    for (ConsumerRecord<String, String> r : batch) {
        inFlight.add(r.offset());
        int w = Math.floorMod(r.key().hashCode(), W);        // same key → same worker → in order
        workers[w].submit(() -> { handle(r); inFlight.remove(r.offset()); });
    }
    long safe = inFlight.isEmpty() ? lastPolledOffset + 1 : inFlight.first();
    consumer.commitAsync(Map.of(tp, new OffsetAndMetadata(safe)), null);
    if (totalQueued() > 10_000) consumer.pause(consumer.assignment());   // backpressure
    else consumer.resume(consumer.paused());
}

Three rules make it correct:

RuleWithout it
Route by key to a single-threaded workerTwo events for one key run concurrently or out of order
Commit the lowest in-flight offset, not the highest finished oneAfter a crash, records still running below the commit are skipped
Pause fetching when queues grow, keep calling poll()Unbounded memory, or a missed poll deadline if you block instead

Also handle rebalances: when partitions are revoked, wait for their in-flight records to finish (or accept that they will be reprocessed) and commit before giving them up. Test with the process killed mid-batch and check that every record was processed at least once and every key stayed in order — that test is what finds the offset bugs. For production use, an existing implementation such as Confluent's parallel consumer saves you from writing this bookkeeping yourself.

Two cautions. The saving applies only to consumers that set client.rack, so make it part of your standard consumer configuration. And if a same-zone replica falls out of sync, the consumer is directed elsewhere, so a sudden return of cross-zone traffic can be a sign of an unhealthy replica — worth watching alongside under-replicated partitions.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • poll() does the real work: joining the group, rebalancing, fetching ahead, auto-committing and proving progress.
  • A KafkaConsumer is not thread-safe; use one per thread, and wakeup() to stop it from another.
  • close() makes a restart fast; a killed consumer's partitions sit unread until the session times out (45 s).
  • Each partition goes to exactly one member of a group, so consumers beyond the partition count are idle.
  • Different groups are independent and each sees every record at its own pace.
  • The range assignor unbalances groups that read many small topics; cooperative sticky balances across the subscription.
  • Kafka 4.0's new group protocol (KIP-848) moves assignment to the broker and rebalances incrementally; opt in with group.protocol=consumer.
  • Consumer memory is fetch limits times partitions, and it peaks during catch-up — size for that, not for a quiet day.
  • Position is in memory, the committed offset is in the group; a restart resumes at the committed offset and redelivers the rest.
  • Monitor lag per partition and in time, not as a total record count.
  • A batch must finish inside max.poll.interval.ms, or the consumer is removed and its records reprocessed elsewhere.
  • Shorten the loop before raising the interval: lower max.poll.records or make each record cheaper.
  • Use pause() for backpressure and keep polling; blocking the loop misses the deadline.
  • Batching writes is usually the cheapest tenfold speed-up for a consumer that writes to a database.
  • Keyed parallel workers keep per-key order as long as you commit below the oldest unfinished record.
  • Follower fetching with client.rack removes most consumer cross-zone traffic without changing consistency.
