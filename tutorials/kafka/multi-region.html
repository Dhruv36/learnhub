Multi-Region & Disaster Recovery Architecture

What you'll master: the four different reasons to go multi-region, and why each implies a different design; stretch clusters versus replication between clusters, and the cross-region round trip that lands in every acks=all write; how MirrorMaker 2 works — its three connectors, remote topic naming, and the heartbeat and checkpoint topics; why offsets do not match between clusters and what offset translation can and cannot promise; active-passive and active-active topologies, including cycle prevention; a failover runbook in which replication lag is your RPO; and failback, which is the part most plans omit. Prerequisites: Security: TLS, SASL, ACLs & Quotas; the AWS track's Resilience & DR lesson covers RPO and RTO generally.

Part 1 — Four Reasons, Four Designs

GoalWhat it needsWhat it does not need
Disaster recoveryA copy of the data in another region, and a tested failoverClients in both regions during normal operation
Data locality / latencyProducers and consumers near their users, per regionEvery topic everywhere
Data residencySome topics that never leave a jurisdictionGlobal replication of those topics — the opposite
AggregationRegional clusters feeding one analytical clusterBidirectional replication
MigrationTemporary replication and a cut-over planA permanent topology

Start from RPO and RTO, stated as numbers by the business: how much data may be lost (recovery point) and how long recovery may take (recovery time). "No data loss and instant failover" across regions is not purchasable at reasonable cost, and the honest conversation is which of the two to relax. Asynchronous replication means a non-zero RPO, always — and the size of it is the replication lag at the moment the region dies.

Part 2 — Stretch Cluster or Two Clusters?

STRETCH: one cluster whose brokers span regions
  broker.rack = region; replicas spread across regions; acks=all waits for a remote follower
  + no separate replication tooling, offsets are the same everywhere, failover is just leader election
  − EVERY acks=all produce pays the cross-region round trip
  − needs 3 locations for the controller quorum and for min ISR to survive one loss
  → viable only between nearby regions or availability zones with low, stable RTT

TWO CLUSTERS + replication (MirrorMaker 2 or a vendor tool)
  + regional latency unaffected; clusters upgrade and fail independently
  − asynchronous: a non-zero RPO
  − offsets differ between clusters; consumer failover needs translation
  − a second cluster to operate, and cross-region bandwidth to pay for
QuestionStretchReplicated
Produce latencyIncludes the inter-region RTT with acks=allLocal
Data loss on region failureNone for acknowledged writesReplication lag at that moment
Failover mechanismLeader election — automaticA procedure: promote, repoint clients, translate offsets
OffsetsIdentical: one clusterDifferent per cluster
Operational loadOne cluster, but a delicate oneTwo clusters plus replication
🔑 The RTT is the whole argument. Two zones 1 ms apart and two regions 80 ms apart are not variations of the same design. At 80 ms, an acks=all write to a partition whose ISR spans regions cannot complete faster than 80 ms, and min.insync.replicas decides whether losing the remote region stops writes. Stretch clusters belong between nearby, well-connected locations; across distant regions, replication between separate clusters is the standard answer.

Part 3 — How MirrorMaker 2 Works

MirrorMaker 2 is built on Kafka Connect and runs three connectors. Understanding which does what explains most of its behaviour.

ConnectorJobProduces
MirrorSourceConnectorCopies records and topic configurationRemote topics on the target, plus an offset-syncs topic
MirrorCheckpointConnectorTranslates consumer group offsets<source>.checkpoints.internal, and optionally writes into the target's __consumer_offsets
MirrorHeartbeatConnectorEmits heartbeats through the linkA heartbeats topic: the liveness and lag signal
# mm2.properties
clusters = us, eu
us.bootstrap.servers = kafka-us:9092
eu.bootstrap.servers = kafka-eu:9092

us->eu.enabled = true
us->eu.topics = orders|payments|customers          # a regex; be explicit, not ".*"
us->eu.emit.heartbeats.enabled = true
us->eu.sync.group.offsets.enabled = true           # write translated offsets into eu's __consumer_offsets
replication.factor = 3

default topic naming (DefaultReplicationPolicy): the SOURCE ALIAS is prefixed
  us cluster:  orders            →   eu cluster:  us.orders

this is what prevents replication cycles in active-active:
  eu's "us.orders" is not matched by the rule replicating eu's own "orders" back to us

IdentityReplicationPolicy keeps the SAME name (orders → orders)
  simpler for active-passive failover, because clients need no topic rename
  but you must prevent cycles yourself, and aggregation from several sources collides

Offsets do not match between clusters, and cannot. The target topic is a different log: it starts at its own offset, and records arrive as a copy whose numbering has no reason to align. A consumer failing over cannot simply reuse its source-cluster offsets.

source us.orders-0:  ... 918,273 918,274 918,275 ...
target eu us.orders-0: ...  4,012   4,013   4,014 ...     same records, different offsets

MirrorCheckpointConnector records pairs (source offset ↔ target offset) periodically, so a group's
committed offset can be TRANSLATED at failover — approximately, to the nearest checkpoint.
"Approximately" means the failed-over consumer reprocesses a little, or in some cases skips:
idempotent consumers are a requirement of any Kafka DR plan, not an optimisation.

Part 4 — Topologies

TopologyShapeWatch out for
Active-passiveOne region serves; the other receives a copyThe passive side is never exercised unless you drill it
Active-activeBoth regions produce; each replicates to the otherCycles, duplicate processing, and conflicting writes to the same key
Hub and spokeRegional clusters replicate into one aggregateThe hub's capacity; topic name collisions without prefixes
Fan-outOne source replicated to many read-only clustersBandwidth multiplied per target

Active-active is harder than it looks. Two regions both accepting writes for the same entity means the same key can change in both places at once, and Kafka has no conflict resolution — both versions exist, in different orders, in different clusters. It works when writes are naturally partitioned by region (European customers write in Europe), and it is a source of subtle bugs when they are not.

active-active with prefixed topics: each side reads BOTH its local and the remote topic

eu consumers subscribe to:  orders  AND  us.orders
us consumers subscribe to:  orders  AND  eu.orders

  ✔ no cycles: a record copied from us arrives as "us.orders" and is not replicated back
  ✔ each side sees everything
  ✘ consumers must handle two topics, and per-key ordering holds only WITHIN each source

❌ Active-active with IdentityReplicationPolicy and no cycle protection. orders in the US replicates to orders in the EU, which matches the EU's own replication rule and comes straight back. Each record loops, multiplying on every pass, until the topics fill and the link saturates.

✅ Prevent cycles structurally. Prefixed remote topics do it by construction — a replicated topic's name can never match the rule that replicates local topics. If identity naming is required, restrict each direction to an explicit list of locally produced topics, and verify with the heartbeat topics that nothing echoes.

Part 5 — Failover: the Runbook and the Arithmetic

1. DETECT        region unreachable; confirm it is the region, not the monitoring
2. DECIDE        failover is a business decision: it accepts the current RPO
                 replication lag NOW = the data you are choosing to lose
3. PROMOTE       stop replication INTO the target (or it will overwrite);
                 for identity naming, nothing to rename; for prefixed naming, consumers
                 must switch to the prefixed topic names
4. TRANSLATE     apply checkpointed offsets for each consumer group (or sync.group.offsets)
5. REPOINT       clients' bootstrap.servers → the DR cluster (config, DNS or service discovery)
6. VERIFY        producers accepted, consumer lag draining, business metrics recovering
7. RECORD        the exact time and offsets of the cut, for reconciliation and failback
NumberWhere it comes fromTypical
RPOReplication lag at failure — measure it continuously from heartbeatsSeconds to minutes, if the link is healthy
RTODetection + decision + the steps above, measured in a drillDominated by the decision and by client repointing, not by Kafka
Duplicate windowCheckpoint interval: offsets translate to the nearest checkpointWhatever emit.checkpoints.interval.seconds is

Client repointing is usually the slowest step, and it is not a Kafka problem. If bootstrap servers are baked into images, failover means redeploying every producer and consumer. A DNS name or a configuration service that can be changed in one place turns hours into minutes — decide this before the incident, and test that clients actually reconnect when the name changes.

Failback is the part plans omit. After the original region returns, it holds stale data, and the DR region holds everything written during the outage. Returning means replicating in the reverse direction until the original catches up, then performing the same cut-over again — ideally during a planned window. Teams that never rehearse failback end up running permanently in their DR region, which quietly becomes the primary with no DR of its own.

  which topics genuinely need to survive a region loss?  (orders yes, debug logs no)

An untested DR plan is a document, not a capability. The drill is the deliverable: once or twice a year, fail over a real workload, measure the actual RPO and RTO, and fail back. Every plan I have seen tested for the first time discovered something — an unreplicated internal topic, a client with a hard-coded bootstrap list, missing ACLs on the DR cluster, a consumer group that could not translate because checkpoints were not enabled for it.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is MirrorMaker 2 and what does it do?

It is Kafka's tool for replicating data between clusters, built on Kafka Connect. It runs three connectors: one copies records and topic configuration, one translates consumer group offsets between the clusters, and one emits heartbeats so you can see that the link is alive and how far behind it is.

By default it renames replicated topics with the source cluster's alias — orders from the "us" cluster appears as us.orders on the target. That is not cosmetic: it is what prevents replication loops in active-active setups, because a topic that arrived from elsewhere can never match the rule that replicates local topics.

Replication is asynchronous, so the target is always slightly behind, and that lag is your recovery point objective if the source region is lost.

The detail that catches people is offsets: the target topic is a separate log with its own numbering, so a consumer cannot reuse its source offsets after failover. MirrorMaker records checkpoints that map source offsets to target offsets, but the translation is approximate, which is why consumers that fail over must be idempotent.

Beginner: What is the difference between a stretch cluster and cluster replication?

A stretch cluster is one Kafka cluster whose brokers are spread across locations, with rack awareness placing a partition's replicas in different ones. There is no replication tooling: it is ordinary Kafka replication, so offsets are identical everywhere and failover is just leader election.

The cost is latency. With acks=all, every write waits for replicas in other locations, so the round trip between them is added to every produce. That is fine between availability zones a millisecond apart and unacceptable between regions 80 milliseconds apart.

Cluster replication runs two independent clusters and copies data between them asynchronously. Local writes stay local and fast, the clusters upgrade and fail independently, and you accept a non-zero recovery point — the replication lag — plus the complexity of translating offsets and repointing clients at failover.

So the rule of thumb is: stretch across zones or very close regions; replicate between distant regions. The deciding number is the round-trip time, not the word "region".

Mid: Why can't a consumer just use its old offsets after failing over to the DR cluster?

Because the DR cluster's topic is a different log. Records were copied into it by a producer — the replication tool — and its offsets start wherever that topic started and advance independently. Offset 918,273 on the source and offset 4,013 on the target may be the same record, or may be nothing alike.

MirrorMaker's checkpoint connector exists for this: it periodically records the correspondence between source and target offsets for each consumer group, so at failover a group's committed position can be translated to the nearest checkpointed equivalent. With sync.group.offsets.enabled it can even write those translated offsets directly into the target cluster's __consumer_offsets, so a failed-over consumer simply starts in roughly the right place.

"Roughly" is the operative word. The translation is as precise as the checkpoint interval, so a consumer typically reprocesses some records and, in edge cases around the cut, could miss some. That is why idempotent consumers are a requirement of any Kafka DR design rather than a nicety.

Some commercial replication products preserve offsets byte-for-byte, which removes this problem entirely and is a genuine reason teams pay for them.

Mid: How do you measure your actual RPO for a Kafka DR setup?

RPO is the replication lag at the moment the source is lost, so it has to be measured continuously, not estimated once.

The direct method uses the heartbeat topic MirrorMaker emits: each heartbeat carries a timestamp from the source, so the difference between now and the newest heartbeat on the target is the age of the freshest data that has arrived. That single number is the current RPO, and it belongs on a dashboard with an alert when it exceeds the agreed objective.

A second measurement compares end offsets: the source topic's latest offset against what has been mirrored, converted to time using the topic's append rate. It is less direct but catches per-topic differences — a large topic can lag while small ones keep up.

What makes the number move is usually bandwidth or the replication tool's capacity: a burst of traffic, a backfill, or a network problem between regions. So I would also track it during a load test, not only at idle, because the RPO that matters is the one during a busy period — which is exactly when a region is most likely to be lost and most expensive to lose.

Finally, I would verify the measurement in a drill: fail over deliberately, then reconcile what the DR cluster had against what the source had, and compare it with what the metric claimed.

Senior: Design DR for a payments platform with an RPO of 5 minutes and an RTO of 30 minutes.

Those numbers are achievable with asynchronous replication between two clusters, and they rule out needing a stretch cluster — which is good, because a stretch cluster would put a cross-region round trip into every payment write.

Topology: active-passive. The primary region serves all traffic; a DR cluster in a second region receives replication for the topics that must survive — payments, orders, the outbox topics, the schema registry's backing topic and consumer offsets via checkpoints. Debug and analytics topics are not replicated, which cuts cost significantly.

Meeting the RPO: measure replication lag continuously from the heartbeat topic and alert at 2 minutes — well inside the 5-minute objective, so there is time to react before an outage would breach it. Provision cross-region bandwidth for peak, not average, because lag grows exactly when traffic does.

Meeting the RTO: the 30 minutes will be spent on detection, decision and client repointing, not on Kafka. So: automated detection with a clear escalation path, a documented decision owner, clients that read their bootstrap servers from a config service or DNS name that changes in one place, and the DR cluster provisioned by the same automation as production so its ACLs, quotas, topics and schemas are already correct.

Correctness at failover: every consumer idempotent, because offset translation is approximate; every payment carrying a stable id; and a reconciliation job that compares the DR cluster's data against the source of truth once the source region returns.

Recommendation: active-passive replication with selective topics, lag alerting at 2 minutes, one-place client repointing, identical provisioning of both clusters, idempotent consumers, and a twice-yearly drill that measures RPO and RTO and includes failback. And I would write down explicitly that payments accepted in the final minutes before a region loss may be lost — that is what a 5-minute RPO means, and the business should agree to it in advance rather than during the incident.

Senior: Your company wants active-active across two regions so both can serve writes. What do you tell them?

That it is achievable, that Kafka will replicate the data happily, and that the hard part is the application semantics — which is where these projects usually fail.

What Kafka handles: bidirectional replication with MirrorMaker, cycle prevention through prefixed remote topic names, and consumers in each region reading both their local topic and the remote one.

What it does not handle: conflicts. If the same entity can be written in both regions, the two versions exist in both clusters in different orders, and nothing resolves them. Per-key ordering holds only within each source, so a consumer merging both streams sees an interleaving that differs between regions. Kafka has no last-writer-wins, no vector clocks, no conflict resolution at all.

So the design question is whether writes can be partitioned. If European customers are always written in Europe and American customers in America, active-active is straightforward: each region owns its keys, and the replication is for read locality and DR rather than for shared ownership. If any entity can be written in either region, you need application-level conflict handling — a version per entity with a deterministic resolution rule, or a designated owning region per key — and that is a significant piece of design, not a Kafka configuration.

I would also ask what problem active-active is solving, because the usual answers have cheaper solutions: lower write latency for distant users (route users to their region, with region-partitioned ownership), faster failover (active-passive with a drilled runbook is often within the same RTO), or using the second region's capacity (usually not worth the correctness cost).

Recommendation: active-active only with region-partitioned key ownership, prefixed topics and consumers that read both streams; otherwise active-passive with a well-rehearsed failover. And whichever is chosen, the duplicate and reordering behaviour must be handled by idempotent, version-aware consumers, as the event design lesson sets out.

6 (hard): Plan the failback after a real failover.
Solution 
situation: ran in the DR region (eu) for 3 days. The primary (us) is healthy again but holds
           data only up to the moment it failed. Three days of writes exist only in eu.

phase 1  REVERSE replication:  eu -> us for the topics that matter
         us receives "eu.orders" — a SEPARATE topic from its own stale "orders"
phase 2  WAIT for the reverse link to catch up; measure lag as before
phase 3  DECIDE how us consumers will read the merged history
         option A: consumers in us read BOTH "orders" (old) and "eu.orders" (recent) — no data moved
         option B: replay eu's data into a fresh topic "orders.v2" in us and cut consumers over
phase 4  PLANNED cut-over in a quiet window: stop producers in eu, wait for lag 0,
         repoint clients to us, restore the original us -> eu replication direction
phase 5  VERIFY and reconcile: counts per day against the business source of truth
TrapMitigation
Restoring us→eu replication too earlyIt would copy stale orders over the authoritative data; reverse first, cut over, then restore
The stale orders topic in us still existsDecide explicitly whether consumers read it, and mark it clearly; do not silently merge
Offsets translate again on the way backAnother duplicate window: idempotency matters in both directions
Nobody wants to schedule the cut-overSet the date during the failover itself, or DR quietly becomes primary forever

The deeper lesson is to design for symmetry: if both regions are provisioned identically and replication can run either way, failback is the same procedure as failover. Plans that treat DR as a one-way trip make the return a bespoke project, which is why so many teams never take it.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Decide why you are going multi-region — DR, locality, residency, aggregation or migration — because each implies a different design.
  • State RPO and RTO as numbers first; asynchronous replication always means a non-zero RPO.
  • A stretch cluster puts the inter-location round trip into every acks=all write, so it suits zones and nearby regions only.
  • Separate clusters plus replication keep writes local at the cost of lag, offset translation and a second cluster.
  • MirrorMaker 2 runs three connectors: source, checkpoint and heartbeat.
  • Remote topics are prefixed with the source alias by default, which is what prevents replication cycles.
  • Offsets never match between clusters; checkpoints translate them approximately.
  • Idempotent consumers are a requirement of Kafka DR, not an optimisation.
  • Replication lag is your live RPO — measure it from the heartbeat topic and alert well inside the objective.
  • Active-active needs region-partitioned key ownership; Kafka resolves no conflicts.
  • Replicate selectively: cross-region bytes are expensive, and not every topic must survive.
  • Provision the DR cluster from the same automation — ACLs, quotas, schemas and topics must already be right.
  • Client repointing is usually the slowest step; bootstrap servers must be changeable in one place.
  • Stop replication into a cluster before promoting it, or stale data overwrites new writes.
  • Drill failover and failback; untested plans fail on organisational and client-side gaps, not on Kafka.
