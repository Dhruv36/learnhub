Monitoring & Debugging Operations

What you'll master: which signals to alert on and which to keep for diagnosis, and why the distinction decides whether anyone trusts the pager; the handful of cluster alerts that matter, including ActiveControllerCount, which must sum to exactly one across the cluster; consumer lag measured the way it actually predicts trouble — per partition and in time — and the stalled consumer that reports zero lag; the client metrics that explain a producer or consumer without guessing; metric cardinality, which can cost more than the cluster; a triage flow for "the consumer is not receiving anything"; and the log files that reconstruct an incident timeline. Prerequisites: Performance: Throughput vs Latency.

Part 1 — Alert on Symptoms, Diagnose with Causes

Kafka exposes hundreds of metrics, and a monitoring setup that alerts on many of them trains people to ignore the pager. The split that works: page on what users feel, and keep everything else on dashboards for when someone is already investigating.

LayerSymptom (alert)Cause metrics (dashboard)
End-to-endEvent age at processing above the SLOPer-stage latency breakdown
ConsumersTime lag per group beyond its budgetRecords lag per partition, rebalance rate, poll interval
ProducersRecord error rate above zero; send failuresRetry rate, buffer available, request latency
Cluster durabilityUnder-min-ISR or offline partitionsISR shrink rate, under-replicated count, per-broker health
Cluster control planeNo active controller; quorum below majorityVoter lag, controller election rate
CapacityDays-to-full below a weekDisk growth rate, partition counts, cleaner health
🔑 The end-to-end age of a record is the one metric that catches everything. Stamp the event time in the payload, and have the final consumer report now − occurredAt as a histogram. Every failure mode in this track — a stuck partition, a rebalance storm, a slow follower, a paused consumer, a hanging transaction — shows up in it, and it is expressed in the units the business cares about. Component alerts tell you where to look; this one tells you whether anything is actually wrong.

ActiveControllerCount is the classic mis-monitored metric. Each node reports 1 if it is the active controller and 0 otherwise, so a per-node alert on "equals 0" fires on every healthy follower. The meaningful signal is the sum across all controller nodes: it must be exactly 1. Zero means no controller is active; two means two nodes believe they are, which is a serious condition.

sum(kafka_controller_kafkacontroller_activecontrollercount) != 1   for 1m   → page

and for the KRaft quorum specifically:
  the voter count reported by kafka-metadata-quorum.sh, or its JMX equivalent, below majority → page
  a voter's lag growing steadily → warn (a slow disk on that controller)

Part 3 — Consumer Lag, Measured Properly

Lag is the single most useful consumer signal, and the way it is usually measured hides the failures that matter.

MeasurementProblemBetter
Total lag per groupOne stuck partition is invisible among healthy onesMax lag across partitions, and alert on that
Lag in records50,000 means nothing without the rateLag in time: how old is the record being processed
Lag from committed offsets onlyA consumer that processes but never commits looks stuckCompare committed with the group's position and the log end
Lag aloneA group with no members reports whatever it last committedAlert on member count = 0 for groups that should always run
the stalled consumer that reports ZERO lag

$ kafka-consumer-groups.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 --describe --group nightly
Consumer group 'nightly' has no active members.
GROUP    TOPIC     PARTITION  CURRENT-OFFSET  LOG-END-OFFSET  LAG
nightly  payments  0          8812044         8812044         0        ◄── "caught up"

the group is not running at all. Its offsets happen to equal the log end because it last ran
when the topic was quiet — or because auto.offset.reset=latest moved it to the end (lesson 1).
Lag alone cannot distinguish "finished" from "not running".

Time lag is derivable if your tooling does not provide it: the consumer reports the timestamp of the record it is processing, so now − recordTimestamp is the honest number. Failing that, estimate it from records lag divided by the partition's recent append rate. Tools such as Burrow evaluate lag trends rather than thresholds, which avoids alerting on a brief spike during a rebalance.

Transactional topics need care. A read_committed consumer stops at the last stable offset, and control records occupy offsets, so record lag on a transactional topic includes markers and can never quite reach zero. Compare the consumer's progress against the LSO rather than the log end offset, or accept a small constant offset in the number — and remember the hanging-transaction case from the transactions lesson, where lag grows while everything else looks healthy.

❌ Exporting every Kafka client metric with every dimension. Client metrics carry client-id, and many carry topic and partition. A platform with 400 client instances, 300 topics and 12 partitions each produces millions of time series — the metrics bill exceeds the Kafka cluster's, dashboards time out, and nobody can find anything.

✅ Choose the dimensions deliberately. Keep per-partition series only for lag, where they are the point; aggregate the rest to client and topic level; drop per-partition request metrics entirely unless an investigation needs them temporarily. A JMX exporter's allowlist is the cheapest place to enforce this, and it is worth reviewing whenever a new client fleet is onboarded.

The broker's log files are the incident timeline. server.log holds ISR shrink and expand events, log cleaner errors and shutdown sequences; controller.log holds leader elections, broker registration and fencing; state-change.log records each partition's leadership transitions. During an incident, grepping ISR events and leader changes across brokers by timestamp reconstructs what happened far faster than metric dashboards, because it names the partitions and brokers involved.

Ownership is part of monitoring. A dead-letter topic with no owner, a consumer group nobody claims, an alert routed to a team that disbanded — each is a silent failure waiting to happen. A simple inventory (topic, owner, consumers, SLO, runbook) does more for reliability than another dashboard, and it is what makes "who do we call?" answerable during an incident.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What would you monitor on a Kafka cluster?

I would separate what pages someone from what helps diagnose.

Pages: offline partitions (unavailable data), under-min-ISR partitions (writes being refused right now), the controller — the cluster-wide sum of ActiveControllerCount must equal one — the KRaft quorum having a majority, consumer lag in time beyond each group's budget, producer error rates above zero, and disk days-to-full.

Dashboards for diagnosis: under-replicated partitions and ISR shrink rate, the broker request-time breakdown, request handler and network idle ratios, per-partition lag, rebalance rates, log cleaner health, and per-broker resource usage.

Above all I would measure end-to-end record age: the time between the event's timestamp and when the final consumer processed it. Every failure in the system shows up there, and it is in the units the business understands, so it makes a good SLO.

And monitoring includes the clients, not just brokers. Most incidents I have seen were consumer-side — rebalances, slow handlers, poison records — and a broker-only dashboard shows a perfectly healthy cluster throughout.

Beginner: How should consumer lag be measured?

Per partition, and in time rather than in records.

Per partition, because a group's total lag hides the failure that matters. One stuck partition among twelve adds a slowly rising total that looks like mild growth, while that partition is completely stalled. Alerting on the maximum lag across a group's partitions catches it immediately.

In time, because a lag of 50,000 records is nothing on a topic doing 100,000 per second and a disaster on one doing ten per minute. Time lag — how old is the record being processed — means the same thing everywhere, and maps directly to a business requirement.

Lag also needs a companion signal, because a consumer group with no members reports whatever it last committed, which can look like zero lag. I alert on member count for groups that should always be running, and on how long ago the consumer last polled.

On transactional topics there is a wrinkle: control records take offsets and read_committed consumers stop at the last stable offset, so lag never reaches exactly zero and an open transaction can make it grow while everything else is healthy.

Mid: A consumer group reports zero lag, but the downstream system is missing data. What do you check?

Zero lag means the group's committed offsets are at the end of the log. That is consistent with several situations that are not "everything is fine".

1. Is the group actually running? --describe --members: a group with no members still reports its last committed offsets. If it stopped hours ago, lag reflects history, not health.

2. Did it skip data? If the group's offsets expired, or it hit an out-of-range offset with auto.offset.reset=latest, it jumped to the end of the log and committed there — the silent skip from the first lesson. The consumer log shows an offset reset, and the gap is visible by comparing what was produced with what the sink holds.

3. Is it consuming but discarding? A filter, a schema mismatch handled by skipping, or a dead-letter path that nobody monitors. Records are consumed — so lag is zero — and dropped afterwards. The DLT rate and the handler's own counters answer this.

4. Is it committing without processing? Auto-commit with asynchronous handoff, the case from the offsets lesson: offsets advance while records sit in a queue that is lost on restart.

5. Is the data where you think? A key change or a repartition can send records to partitions this group does not read, or to a different topic entirely.

The check that settles it quickly is a reconciliation count: records produced in a window versus records the sink recorded for the same window, which turns "missing data" into a number and a time range.

Mid: Your metrics bill for Kafka clients exceeds the cluster's cost. What happened and what do you do?

Cardinality. Kafka client metrics are tagged with client-id, and many are also per topic and per partition. Multiply a few hundred client instances by a few hundred topics and their partitions, and the number of time series runs into millions — each one stored, indexed and queried.

I would start by measuring which metric families produce the most series, which most monitoring systems can report. Typically it is per-partition client metrics and per-topic request metrics from brokers.

Then reduce deliberately rather than uniformly. Keep per-partition series for consumer lag, because that is exactly where per-partition detail is the point. Aggregate producer and consumer throughput and latency metrics to the client and topic level. Drop per-partition broker request metrics entirely, keeping the per-request-type breakdown that the performance lesson relies on. Shorten retention for high-cardinality series, and keep aggregated ones long for trends.

A JMX exporter allowlist is the practical place to enforce it, checked into the same repository as the deployment so a new client fleet cannot quietly multiply the bill.

The trade-off to state openly is that dropped dimensions cannot be recovered retroactively. For the ones that matter occasionally — per-partition request times, say — I would keep a documented way to enable them temporarily during an investigation rather than collecting them always.

Senior: Design the monitoring and alerting for a platform with 40 teams' pipelines.

Two audiences, two sets of signals. The platform team owns the cluster: offline and under-min-ISR partitions, the controller and quorum, broker saturation, cleaner health, disk trends, capacity. Application teams own their pipelines: end-to-end record age, consumer lag in time, producer error rate, DLT rate, rebalance rate. Each team's alerts route to that team, not to the platform.

Standardise the application-side instrumentation so those signals exist without each team inventing them: a shared client library or sidecar configuration that emits end-to-end age, lag in time, handler duration and DLT counts with consistent names. Without that, half the teams will have no usable signal and the platform team becomes the default responder for everything.

An inventory is part of the design: topic, owner, consumers, retention, SLO, runbook. It answers "who owns this?" during an incident, and it makes unowned topics and groups visible before they fail.

Alert hygiene: every alert has a runbook with the first commands to run; alerts that fire without action are reviewed monthly and deleted or re-thresholded; and the alerts themselves are tested by injecting failures in staging on a schedule — a poison record, a paused consumer, a stopped broker.

Trends, not just states: partition budget used per broker, days-to-full, peak-to-average ratios, cost per TB ingested. Those drive quarterly capacity decisions rather than emergency ones.

Recommendation: symptom-based paging split by ownership, standardised client instrumentation, an inventory with SLOs and runbooks, cardinality controlled by an allowlist, and scheduled alert testing. The goal is a pager that is quiet and trusted — which is a property of what you choose not to alert on as much as what you do.

Senior: Reconstruct an incident: for 20 minutes, some producers saw timeouts and some consumers stalled, then it recovered by itself.

A partial, self-resolving incident is usually one broker misbehaving, and the reconstruction comes from logs more than from dashboards, because logs name partitions and brokers.

1. Establish the window and the blast radius. Which producers, which topics, which partitions. If the affected partitions share a broker — as leader or as an in-sync replica — that broker is the suspect.

2. Read the cluster's own account. controller.log for leader elections, broker registrations and fencing events in that window; server.log across brokers for "Shrinking ISR" and "Expanding ISR"; state-change.log for the leadership transitions. A broker that was fenced and re-registered, with ISR shrinks naming it on both sides of the window, is the story.

3. Correlate with that broker's host. GC pause logs, disk latency, CPU steal, network errors. A pause or disk stall longer than the session timeout explains fencing; the recovery explains why it resolved itself.

4. Explain the client symptoms. Producers with acks=all saw RemoteTimeMs rise while that replica was in the ISR but stalled, then timeouts when leadership moved. Consumers of partitions it led stalled until a new leader was elected, and any consumer group whose coordinator was on that broker rebalanced.

5. Write the timeline with evidence, then decide what to change. Usually: fix the host cause, and add the detection that would have caught it early — ISR shrink rate, GC pause duration, per-broker disk latency — plus an alert on end-to-end age so the next occurrence is noticed by the platform before the teams report it.

The wider point is that self-resolving incidents are the ones most often left uninvestigated, and they are the cheapest to learn from: the same broker will do it again, usually at a worse moment.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Alert on symptoms users feel; keep causes on dashboards — that split is what keeps a pager trusted.
  • End-to-end record age is the one metric that catches every failure mode and states it in business units.
  • Page on offline partitions, under-min-ISR, controller presence and quorum majority.
  • ActiveControllerCount must be alerted as a cluster-wide sum of exactly 1, never per node.
  • Alert on log cleaner health and days-to-full, not on a fixed disk percentage.
  • Measure lag per partition, not per group — one stuck partition hides in a total.
  • Express lag in time, so one threshold means the same thing on every topic.
  • A group with no members reports zero lag; alert on member count and last-poll age too.
  • Transactional topics never show exactly zero lag because control records take offsets.
  • Collect client metrics: most incidents are consumer-side and invisible on broker dashboards.
  • Metric cardinality can cost more than the cluster; allowlist at the exporter and keep per-partition detail only for lag.
  • Use the triage order: is data produced, does the group have members, is it assigned partitions, where is its offset, is it failing before your code.
  • Broker logs are the incident timeline — ISR events, controller fencing and state changes name the partitions and brokers.
  • Every alert needs a runbook and an owner, and an inventory of topics, consumers and SLOs.
  • Test alerts by injecting failures in staging; untested alerts fail in the incident they were written for.
