Replication, ISR & acks Delivery & Durability

What you'll master: how followers copy a leader — they are, in effect, consumers of it — and how the high watermark moves; the in-sync replica set, what gets a replica removed from it, and why one paused broker can stall every acks=all write to its partitions for 30 seconds; the acks × min.insync.replicas matrix that decides exactly how many failures you survive for reads, writes and durability; clean, preferred and unclean leader election, and why an "acknowledged" record is in memory on several machines, not on disk; surviving a zone outage; and the operational work — rolling restarts, reassignment with throttles, leader balance — that keeps replication healthy. Prerequisites: Consumers & Consumer Groups.

Part 1 — Followers Are Consumers of the Leader

Each partition has one leader and, with replication factor N, N − 1 followers. Producers write only to the leader. Followers keep up by sending the leader fetch requests — the same request consumers use — and appending what they receive to their own copy of the log.

producer ──(produce, acks=all)──► LEADER  broker 1   log: 0 … 1041   LEO 1042
                                     ▲   ▲
               fetch "from 1040" ────┘   └──── fetch "from 1042"
                                     │         │
                            FOLLOWER broker 2   FOLLOWER broker 3
                            LEO 1040            LEO 1042

A follower's fetch offset TELLS the leader how far it has replicated.
high watermark = min LEO across the ISR = 1040
the acks=all request for offset 1041 completes when broker 2 fetches from 1042 or later

The design has two useful properties. Followers can batch aggressively, because they fetch in the same large chunks as consumers; and the leader learns each follower's progress for free, from the offset each fetch asks for. The high watermark then travels back to followers in fetch responses, so they learn which of their records are committed.

SettingDefaultEffect
num.replica.fetchers1Fetcher threads per source broker; more helps followers keep up on busy brokers
replica.fetch.max.bytes1 MBPer-partition fetch size for replication
replica.fetch.wait.max.ms500 msHow long a leader may hold a follower fetch with no new data
replica.lag.time.max.ms30 s (10 s before 2.5)A follower not caught up within this time leaves the ISR

Leader epochs keep replicas consistent across failovers. Every leader change increments the partition's leader epoch, and each batch is stamped with the epoch it was written in. When a replica rejoins as a follower, it asks the new leader where its epoch ended and truncates anything past that point before fetching. This replaced an older, high-watermark-based scheme that could lose or diverge data in some double-failure cases (KIP-101), and it is why a returning replica sometimes logs that it is truncating its log — that is correct behaviour, not corruption.

Part 2 — The In-Sync Replica Set

The ISR is the set of replicas considered caught up: the leader plus every follower that has reached the leader's log end offset within the last replica.lag.time.max.ms. It is the set that acks=all waits for and the set a new leader is normally chosen from.

t=0      ISR = {1, 2, 3}         all three fetching normally
t=0      broker 3 pauses (a long GC pause, a frozen VM, a saturated disk)
t=0..30s acks=all writes to partitions with broker 3 in the ISR WAIT for it
         producers see latency climb to ~30 s; some requests hit request.timeout.ms (30 s) and retry
t=30s    leader removes broker 3:  ISR = {1, 2}      "Shrinking ISR from 1,2,3 to 1,2"
         pending writes complete; latency returns to normal
later    broker 3 recovers, catches up to the leader's LEO → "Expanding ISR from 1,2 to 1,2,3"
🔑 A replica that is slow but not dead is the worst case for latency. A crashed broker drops out of the ISR quickly once its session with the controller ends. A broker that is alive but stalled stays in the ISR for up to replica.lag.time.max.ms, and during that window every acks=all write to its partitions waits for it. Lowering the setting shortens the stall but makes replicas drop out on ordinary hiccups, which causes ISR churn and brief periods of weaker durability. Most clusters keep the 30 s default and fix the source of the stalls — GC, noisy neighbours, disks.

Part 3 — The acks × min.insync.replicas Matrix

Durability is decided by three numbers together: the topic's replication factor, its min.insync.replicas, and the producer's acks. min.insync.replicas only applies to acks=all writes: if the ISR is smaller than it, the leader refuses the write with NotEnoughReplicasException, which the producer retries until delivery.timeout.ms.

RF / min ISR / acksAcknowledged writes surviveWrites still accepted withVerdict
3 / 1 / allOnly if the ISR had more than the leader at the time2 brokers downDefault settings — durability can silently degrade to one copy
3 / 2 / allAny 1 broker loss1 broker downThe standard production choice
3 / 3 / allAny 2 broker losses0 brokers down — any single failure stops writesRarely worth it; a routine restart blocks producers
3 / 2 / 1Not guaranteed — leader-only acknowledgement2 brokers downmin.insync.replicas is ignored for acks=1
2 / 2 / allAny 1 broker loss0 down — every restart blocks writesWhy RF 2 is awkward
2 / 1 / allNot guaranteed while the ISR is the leader alone1 downAvailable, but weak
4 / 3 / allAny 2 broker losses1 broker downUsed for two-zone layouts (see Part 5)
The rule of thumb:     RF = min.insync.replicas + 1 (at least), acks = all

  writes tolerate          RF − min.insync.replicas     broker failures
  acknowledged data survives  min.insync.replicas − 1   broker failures (guaranteed)
  with RF 3 / min ISR 2:   1 and 1 — and a rolling restart never blocks producers

Consumers are unaffected by min.insync.replicas. A partition with only its leader in the ISR refuses acks=all writes but still serves reads up to the high watermark. So "writes failing, reads fine" is the expected signature of a partition below its minimum ISR.

❌ Relying on acks=all with the default min.insync.replicas=1. During a period when both followers have fallen out of the ISR — a slow disk, a network problem — the leader keeps accepting acks=all writes on its own and acknowledges them as fully replicated. If that broker is then lost, every write from that period is gone, and nothing reported a problem.

✅ Set min.insync.replicas=2 with replication factor 3, as a broker default and on existing topics. The leader then refuses writes it cannot replicate, the producer retries and eventually reports the failure, and the choice between "stop writing" and "risk losing data" is made by the configuration you chose rather than by accident.

Part 4 — Leader Election: Clean, Preferred and Unclean

When a leader fails, the controller chooses a new leader for each of its partitions. Normally the new leader must come from the ISR, so it has every committed record — a clean election.

Replicas: 1,2,3   Leader: 1   ISR: 1,2,3     broker 1 fails
controller: pick the first replica in the ISR that is alive → broker 2 leads, ISR {2,3}
committed data (below the high watermark) is on broker 2: nothing acknowledged is lost

Replicas: 1,2,3   Leader: 1   ISR: 1         (followers had fallen behind), broker 1 fails
unclean.leader.election.enable = false (default) → NO leader: partition OFFLINE until broker 1 returns
unclean.leader.election.enable = true            → broker 2 leads with what it has;
                                                  everything it never received is LOST, and when
                                                  broker 1 returns it truncates to match
ElectionWhenDataAvailability
CleanAn ISR member is aliveNo committed data lostRestored in seconds
Unclean (off by default)No ISR member alive, and enabledRecords the new leader lacks are lostRestored immediately
NoneNo ISR member alive, unclean disabledNothing lostPartition offline until an ISR member returns
PreferredRebalancing leadership back to the first listed replicaNone lostBrief leader move

Preferred leaders keep load balanced. The first replica in a partition's list is its preferred leader, and replica assignment spreads preferred leaders evenly. After failures, leaders pile up on the surviving brokers. With auto.leader.rebalance.enable=true (the default), the controller checks every 5 minutes and moves leadership back when a broker's share is more than 10% off; you can also trigger it with kafka-leader-election.sh --election-type preferred.

Eligible leader replicas. Kafka 4.x adds a refinement (KIP-966) that tracks replicas which were not in the ISR but are still known to hold all committed data, so a partition can recover more safely after certain multi-broker failures. It changes the fine print of which replicas may be elected; check whether it is enabled in the version you run before relying on it.

🔑 "Acknowledged" means in memory on several machines, not on disk. Kafka does not fsync each write; it relies on the page cache, and the kernel writes data to disk later. An acks=all record exists in the page cache of every in-sync replica. That survives any failure that leaves a replica's machine running — process crashes, a broker restart — and any failure of a single machine. It does not survive every replica's machine losing power at the same moment. That is why replicas should sit in different racks or zones: replication across independent failure domains is the durability mechanism, and flushing every write (flush.messages=1) costs far more than it adds for most workloads.

Part 5 — Surviving a Zone Outage

In a cloud region, the realistic large failure is losing an availability zone. Rack-aware placement (broker.rack set to the zone) puts each partition's replicas in different zones, so the question becomes what the replication settings do when one zone disappears.

3 zones, RF 3 (one replica per zone), min.insync.replicas = 2, controllers in all 3 zones

zone A lost:
  every partition keeps 2 replicas → ISR = 2 ≥ min ISR → reads AND writes continue
  leaders that were in zone A move to B or C within seconds
  controller quorum: 2 of 3 controllers remain → still has a majority ✔

zones A and B lost:
  ISR = 1 < min ISR → acks=all writes REFUSED; reads continue from the survivors
  controller quorum: 1 of 3 → no majority → no metadata changes, no new leader elections
LayoutOne zone lostNotes
3 zones, RF 3, min ISR 2Reads and writes continueThe standard layout; controllers spread 1 per zone
2 zones, RF 3, min ISR 2Depends which zone: the zone holding 2 replicas of a partition takes writes down with itUneven by construction
2 zones, RF 4, min ISR 3Writes stop (only 2 replicas left)Durable, not available
2 zones, RF 4, min ISR 2Reads and writes continue on 2 replicasAvailable; durability rests on the 2 surviving copies
1 zoneEverything lostReplication factor cannot help

The controller quorum needs its own placement. KRaft controllers use Raft, which needs a majority to operate. Three controllers in three zones survive one zone; three controllers in two zones can lose the zone holding two of them and with it the majority. Without a working quorum, brokers keep serving existing leaders, but no leader can be re-elected and no topic or configuration can change — so a second failure during that time cannot be repaired automatically.

Two-zone regions force a real trade-off. With only two zones there is no layout where losing either zone keeps both durability (two acknowledged copies) and writes. You choose: RF 4 with min ISR 3 keeps durability and stops writes; RF 4 with min ISR 2 keeps writing on the surviving copies. Either way the controller quorum needs a tiebreaker in a third location to survive losing a zone. Stretch clusters over two zones are covered further in the multi-region lesson.

Part 6 — Operating Replication

Most replication problems in practice come from routine operations done hastily: restarts, adding brokers, and moving partitions.

Rolling restarts. A broker shutting down cleanly (SIGTERM) first moves leadership of its partitions to other in-sync replicas — controlled shutdown — so producers see a leader change rather than errors. The rule for the next step is simple and often skipped:

for each broker:
    stop it cleanly (SIGTERM — never kill -9)
    start it
    WAIT until UnderReplicatedPartitions == 0 across the cluster   ◄── the step people skip
    (optionally) run a preferred leader election
next broker

Restarting the next broker while the previous one is still catching up means some partitions
have 2 of 3 replicas down: with min ISR 2 they refuse writes; with min ISR 1 they run on one copy.

Moving partitions. Adding a broker does not move any existing data to it; you reassign partitions explicitly. Reassignment copies each moved replica from scratch, which is a large amount of traffic competing with normal replication and client traffic, so throttle it:

$ kafka-reassign-partitions.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9092 \
    --reassignment-json-file move.json --execute --throttle 50000000       # 50 MB/s
$ kafka-reassign-partitions.sh ... --reassignment-json-file move.json --verify
   (verify also REMOVES the throttle once the moves finish — run it, or the throttle stays)

time to move 6 TB at 50 MB/s = 6,000,000 MB / 50 = 120,000 s ≈ 33 hours

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is the ISR?

The in-sync replica set: the partition's leader plus every follower that is caught up with it. A follower counts as in sync if it has reached the leader's log end offset within replica.lag.time.max.ms (30 seconds by default). Followers that fall further behind are removed and added back once they catch up.

The ISR matters for three things. acks=all writes are acknowledged once every ISR member has them. The high watermark — the limit of what consumers can read — is the lowest log end offset across the ISR. And when a leader fails, the new leader is chosen from the ISR, which is what guarantees that no acknowledged record is lost in a normal failover.

The ISR can shrink without anyone noticing, which is why min.insync.replicas exists. It sets the smallest ISR size at which the leader still accepts acks=all writes. With replication factor 3 and min.insync.replicas=2, a partition keeps accepting writes with one replica down, and refuses them rather than acknowledging with only one copy.

In monitoring terms, a partition whose ISR is smaller than its replica list is under-replicated, and that count should be zero in a healthy cluster.

Beginner: What do the producer's acks settings mean?

acks decides when the leader tells the producer a write succeeded.

acks=0: the producer does not wait for any response. Fastest, but any failure loses data silently, and the producer cannot even retry because it never learns of the failure. acks=1: the leader responds once the record is in its own log. If the leader fails before a follower copies it, the new leader does not have it, and an acknowledged write is lost. acks=all: the leader responds once every in-sync replica has the record. This is the default since Kafka 3.0.

acks=all is only as strong as the ISR it waits for, so it has to be paired with min.insync.replicas. With the default of 1, a leader whose followers have all dropped out would acknowledge acks=all writes on its own. With min.insync.replicas=2, it refuses them instead.

The cost of acks=all is latency: the write waits for the slowest in-sync follower. In a healthy cluster that is a few milliseconds more than acks=1; when a follower is struggling it can be much more, which is a signal to fix the follower rather than to weaken acks.

Mid: Producer latency with acks=all spikes to 30 seconds for some partitions, then recovers on its own. What is happening?

The number is the clue: 30 seconds is the default replica.lag.time.max.ms. The pattern fits a follower that stopped fetching without dying.

While a follower is in the ISR, every acks=all write to that partition waits for it. If its broker stalls — a long garbage-collection pause, a frozen VM, a disk so saturated that its fetch thread blocks — it stops fetching but is still considered in sync. Writes wait. After replica.lag.time.max.ms the leader removes it from the ISR, the pending writes complete with the remaining replicas, and latency returns to normal. When the broker recovers and catches up, it rejoins.

To confirm it, I would look at the broker logs for "Shrinking ISR" and "Expanding ISR" messages around the spikes, the IsrShrinksPerSec metric, and which broker is named in them. Then look at that broker: GC logs for long pauses, disk latency, CPU steal on the VM.

Only partitions with a replica on the stalled broker are affected, which is why it looks random from the producer's side. The fix is to remove the stalls — tune the broker's garbage collector, move it off a noisy host, fix the disk. Lowering replica.lag.time.max.ms would shorten each spike but make replicas drop out on routine hiccups, and weakening acks trades a latency problem for a durability one.

Mid: How do you do a rolling restart of a Kafka cluster without affecting producers?

One broker at a time, with a health check between each, and with a clean shutdown every time.

Stop each broker with SIGTERM. A clean shutdown triggers controlled shutdown: the broker moves leadership of its partitions to other in-sync replicas before it exits, so producers and consumers see a quick leader change instead of timeouts. Killing the process skips this, and clients wait for the controller to notice the broker is gone.

After restarting it, wait until the cluster reports zero under-replicated partitions before touching the next broker. The restarted broker needs time to catch up on everything written while it was down. If you restart the next broker before it has, some partitions have two of three replicas unavailable at once: with min.insync.replicas=2 they refuse writes, and with 1 they are running on a single copy.

After the last broker, leadership may be unevenly spread, because each restart moved leaders away. Automatic leader rebalancing fixes this within minutes, or you can run a preferred leader election explicitly.

For this to be invisible to producers, the topics need replication factor 3 and min.insync.replicas=2, producers need retries and a delivery.timeout.ms longer than a leader change (the defaults are fine), and the restart tooling should check under-replicated partitions automatically rather than relying on someone watching a dashboard.

Senior: During a large outage, someone proposes enabling unclean leader election to bring partitions back. How do you decide?

The decision is between availability now and acknowledged data later, and it should be made per topic with the business owner, not cluster-wide under pressure.

First establish the situation. Partitions are offline because every ISR member is down; the surviving replicas were out of sync, and each is missing some suffix of the log — anywhere from a few records to a lot, depending on how far behind it was. Enabling unclean election makes one of them leader immediately, and the records it lacks are gone for good: when the old ISR members return they truncate to match the new leader. Producers that already had those writes acknowledged are never told.

Then look at the alternative: when does an ISR member come back? If the brokers are restarting and will return in minutes, waiting costs minutes of unavailability and loses nothing. If a disk or machine is permanently gone, waiting may mean forever, and the question becomes whether the lost suffix can be reconstructed from elsewhere.

I would decide per topic. For metrics, logs and clickstream, losing a few seconds of data is acceptable and availability matters: enable unclean election on those topics only (it can be set per topic). For payments, orders or anything with an external side effect, keep it off and wait, or restore from a source of truth. Whatever is enabled must be recorded and switched back off after the incident, because leaving it on turns a future routine failure into silent data loss.

Recommendation: never enable it cluster-wide in the moment; classify topics in advance into "can lose a suffix" and "cannot", put that classification in the runbook, and enable unclean election only for the first group, with the change logged and reverted afterwards. The long-term fix is to make the situation rarer: replicas across zones, min.insync.replicas=2, and monitoring that alerts when partitions sit at their minimum ISR.

Senior: Design a Kafka deployment that survives the loss of an availability zone without losing acknowledged data.

Three zones are the foundation. Brokers in three zones with broker.rack set to the zone, so each partition's three replicas land in three different zones. Topics use replication factor 3 and min.insync.replicas=2, and producers use acks=all with idempotence. Losing a zone leaves two replicas of every partition, which meets the minimum ISR, so reads and writes continue and every acknowledged record exists in at least one surviving zone.

The controller quorum is placed the same way: three (or five) KRaft controllers, one per zone, so losing a zone keeps a majority and the cluster can still elect leaders and change metadata.

Capacity must absorb the loss. When a zone fails, its leaders move to the other two zones, which now carry all the traffic with a third fewer brokers. If brokers run at 70% utilisation normally, they cannot absorb that. I would size so the cluster runs comfortably on two-thirds of its brokers — roughly 50–60% utilisation at peak. Clients need the same thought: consumers in the lost zone are gone, and the remaining consumers must handle their partitions.

Clients must survive a leader move: producers with default retries and a delivery.timeout.ms well above leader-election time, consumers configured with client.rack for follower fetching (so normal operation is cheap), and bootstrap lists that include brokers in every zone.

Test it. A zone outage is rare enough that the first real one will find whatever was not tested: a topic created with RF 1, an internal topic with a low replication factor, a client with a bootstrap list pointing at one zone. I would run a scheduled game day that blocks traffic to one zone and check producer error rates and consumer lag.

Recommendation: three zones, RF 3, min ISR 2, rack-aware placement, one controller per zone, capacity for two-thirds operation, and a regular zone-failure test. If the region has only two zones, say plainly that the design must choose between writes and two-copy durability during a zone loss, and put a third-location tiebreaker in place for the controllers.

    --config min.insync.replicas=1
# leader = broker 1, follower = broker 2
$ docker compose stop kafka-2                           # follower goes away: ISR = {1}
$ seq 1 1000 | kafka-console-producer.sh ... --topic unclean --producer-property acks=all
                                                        # all 1000 acknowledged by broker 1 alone
$ docker compose stop kafka-1                           # the only ISR member dies
$ docker compose start kafka-2
$ kafka-topics.sh ... --describe --topic unclean
  Topic: unclean  Partition: 0  Leader: none  Replicas: 1,2  Isr: 1      ◄── offline, by design

$ kafka-configs.sh ... --alter --entity-type topics --entity-name unclean \
    --add-config unclean.leader.election.enable=true
$ kafka-topics.sh ... --describe --topic unclean
  Topic: unclean  Partition: 0  Leader: 2  Replicas: 1,2  Isr: 2         ◄── back online
$ kafka-get-offsets.sh ... --topic unclean
unclean:0:0                                             ◄── the 1,000 acknowledged records are gone

Every one of the 1,000 records was acknowledged under acks=all, and all are lost. Two settings allowed it: min.insync.replicas=1 let the leader acknowledge alone, and unclean election let an out-of-date replica take over. When broker 1 returns, it truncates its log to match broker 2, so the records do not reappear.

SettingIts role in the loss
min.insync.replicas=1Allowed acknowledging with one copy
unclean.leader.election.enable=trueAllowed a replica missing those records to lead

Repeat with min.insync.replicas=2 and RF 3: the producer is refused once the ISR drops to one, so there are no acknowledged-but-unreplicated records for unclean election to lose. Remember to remove the unclean setting afterwards.

6 (hard): Your region has only two availability zones. Design the replication settings and state exactly what happens when each zone fails.
Solution

With two zones, no layout keeps both writes and two acknowledged copies through the loss of either zone. Make the trade explicit per topic:

brokers:  3 in zone A, 3 in zone B, broker.rack set
topics:   RF 4 (2 replicas per zone, enforced by rack-aware placement)

option D (durable):     min.insync.replicas = 3
  zone lost → 2 replicas remain < 3 → acks=all writes REFUSED; reads continue
  every acknowledged record was on at least 3 replicas → at least 1 survives in the other zone

option A (available):   min.insync.replicas = 2
  zone lost → 2 replicas remain = 2 → writes CONTINUE on the surviving zone only
  records acknowledged during the outage exist in one zone only until the other returns

controllers: 3 voters — 1 in zone A, 1 in zone B, 1 in a THIRD location (another region or site)
  without the third, losing the zone holding 2 controllers stops all metadata changes
Topic classSettingDuring a zone loss
Payments, ordersRF 4, min ISR 3Writes stop; nothing acknowledged is at risk
Clickstream, logsRF 4, min ISR 2Writes continue with single-zone durability
Internal topics (__consumer_offsets, transactions)RF 4, min ISR 2 or 3 to match the most critical dataConsumer commits must keep working if consumers do

State the consequences in the design document: producers of durable topics need to handle refusals (buffer in an outbox, return errors, or degrade features) for the length of a zone outage; the cluster needs enough capacity in one zone to carry everything; and replication between two zones doubles cross-zone traffic compared with RF 2. If neither option is acceptable for a topic, the requirement is really for a third zone or a second region, and the multi-region lesson covers that.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Followers replicate by fetching from the leader, and each fetch offset tells the leader how far that follower has come.
  • The ISR is the leader plus followers caught up within replica.lag.time.max.ms (30 s); acks=all waits for all of them.
  • A stalled but living replica is the worst latency case: it holds up acks=all writes for up to 30 s before it is removed.
  • min.insync.replicas is what gives acks=all meaning; with the default of 1, "all" can be the leader alone.
  • RF 3 with min.insync.replicas=2 and acks=all survives one broker loss for both writes and acknowledged data.
  • min.insync.replicas equal to RF stops writes on any single failure, including a routine restart.
  • Below the minimum ISR, writes are refused but reads continue — the signature to recognise.
  • Clean elections pick from the ISR and lose nothing; unclean election trades acknowledged data for availability and is off by default.
  • Leader epochs let returning replicas truncate correctly; a truncation message on rejoin is normal.
  • Preferred leader election restores balance after failures; it runs automatically every 5 minutes by default.
  • "Acknowledged" means in the page cache of every in-sync replica, so replicas must sit in independent failure domains.
  • Three zones, rack-aware placement and one controller per zone survive a zone loss with reads and writes intact.
  • Two-zone regions force a choice between writes and two-copy durability, and need a third-location controller.
  • In a rolling restart, wait for zero under-replicated partitions before touching the next broker.
  • Throttle partition reassignments and finish with --verify, which is what removes the throttle.
  • Alert on under-replicated, at-min-ISR and under-min-ISR partitions, not only on offline ones.
