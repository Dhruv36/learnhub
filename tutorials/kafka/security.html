Security: TLS, SASL, ACLs & Quotas Operations

What you'll master: the four separate mechanisms people conflate — encryption in transit, authentication, authorization and quotas — and what each does not cover; listeners and security protocols, and how a client's principal is derived from a certificate or a SASL identity; the ACL a consumer needs that everyone forgets, and why a topic ACL alone leaves it failing; how quotas throttle rather than reject, and what that does to a noisy tenant; certificate rotation, which is the most common self-inflicted Kafka outage; and an honest account of what Kafka does not protect — including payloads at rest and per-field access. Prerequisites: Monitoring & Debugging.

Part 1 — Four Mechanisms, Four Jobs

MechanismAnswersDoes NOT give you
TLSCan anyone read or alter traffic in flight?Any notion of who the client is (unless mTLS), or protection on disk
SASL / mTLS authenticationWho is this client?Any permission at all
ACLs (authorization)What may this principal do?Protection from a client that is authorised but abusive
QuotasHow much may this principal use?Security; they are a fairness and stability control

Encryption at rest is not Kafka's job. Brokers write plaintext bytes to disk. Protecting them means full-disk or filesystem encryption on the broker hosts, which defends against a stolen disk and nothing else — anyone with broker access or the right ACLs still reads the data. For sensitive fields the answer is client-side encryption, as the event design lesson describes with crypto-shredding, which also gives you a deletion story.

🔑 Authentication without authorization is an open cluster with a login page. Enabling SASL proves who clients are; until an authorizer is configured, every authenticated client can read and write every topic. Conversely, ACLs without authentication protect nothing, because principals are unverified. The two are only useful together, and the order to enable them in is authentication first, then ACLs in a permissive mode, then enforcement.

Part 2 — Listeners, Protocols and Principals

The principal is what ACLs match, and it is not always what you expect. With mTLS the default principal is the certificate's full distinguished name, so an ACL written for User:orders-service never matches User:CN=orders-service,OU=payments,O=Acme,C=GB. Either write ACLs against the full DN or map it:

ssl.principal.mapping.rules=RULE:^CN=(.*?),.*$/$1/L,DEFAULT
   → CN=orders-service,OU=payments,O=Acme,C=GB   becomes   User:orders-service

verify what the broker actually sees before writing ACLs:
  authorizer logs show the principal on every denied request

Part 3 — ACLs: Least Privilege in Practice

An ACL grants a principal an operation on a resource. The resources and operations are more granular than most people expect, and the common failure is granting too few, not too many.

ClientNeedsOn
ProducerWrite, DescribeThe topic
ConsumerRead, DescribeThe topic
ConsumerReadThe consumer group — the one people forget
Transactional producerWrite, DescribeThe TransactionalId
Kafka Streams appRead/Write on input, output and internal topics; Create if it creates themPrefixed by application.id
Admin toolingDescribe, AlterConfigs, Create as neededCluster and topics
$ kafka-acls.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9094 --add \
    --allow-principal User:billing-service \
    --operation Read --operation Describe --topic payments
$ kafka-acls.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9094 --add \
    --allow-principal User:billing-service \
    --operation Read --group billing                 ◄── without this, the consumer cannot join

# without the group ACL:
org.apache.kafka.common.errors.GroupAuthorizationException: Not authorized to access group: billing

Prefixed ACLs make this manageable at scale. Granting a team everything under its own prefix turns per-topic ACL administration into one rule per team, and it pairs with a naming convention that encodes ownership:

$ kafka-acls.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9094 --add \
    --allow-principal User:team-orders \
    --operation All --topic orders. --resource-pattern-type prefixed
$ kafka-acls.sh ... --add --allow-principal User:team-orders \
    --operation Read --group orders. --resource-pattern-type prefixed

Part 4 — Quotas: Protecting the Cluster from Its Users

Quotas limit how much of the cluster a client may use. They are applied per user, per client id, or per both, and the broker enforces them by delaying responses rather than rejecting requests — so a throttled client slows down instead of failing.

QuotaLimitsProtects against
producer_byte_rateBytes produced per secondOne team's batch job saturating ingress
consumer_byte_rateBytes fetched per secondA backfill reading at full disk speed and evicting the page cache
request_percentageShare of request handler and network thread timeClients that are cheap in bytes but expensive in requests
controller_mutation_rateTopic create/delete/partition-change rateA loop that creates thousands of topics
$ kafka-configs.sh --bootstrap-server kafka:9094 --alter \
    --add-config 'producer_byte_rate=10485760,consumer_byte_rate=52428800,request_percentage=200' \
    --entity-type users --entity-name analytics-service

throttled clients see it in their own metrics, and the broker reports it per request:
  produce-throttle-time-avg / fetch-throttle-time-avg   (client)
  ThrottleTimeMs                                        (broker RequestMetrics)

❌ Discovering quotas only after an incident. A backfill job reads from the earliest offset at whatever rate the cluster allows. Disk reads saturate, the page cache is evicted, and every tailing consumer's p99 triples — the capacity lesson's catch-up scenario. Nobody did anything wrong; the platform simply had no limit.

✅ Give every principal a default quota when it is onboarded, generous enough not to matter in normal operation and low enough to bound the worst case. Then alert on throttle time, which tells you both that a client hit its limit and that the limit did its job.

Quotas are fairness, not security. They stop a client from consuming the whole cluster; they do not stop it from reading data it should not see. That is what ACLs are for, and the two are often confused when someone says a tenant is "isolated".

Part 5 — Operating It: Rotation, Credentials and Audit

Expired certificates are the most common self-inflicted Kafka outage. They expire on a known date, on every broker and every client at once, and the failure is total.

rotating the CA or broker certificates without downtime

1. add the NEW CA to every truststore (brokers and clients) — both CAs trusted   ◄── weeks ahead
   rolling restart; nothing is using the new certificates yet
2. issue new broker certificates from the new CA; roll them in, one broker at a time
3. issue new client certificates; roll clients at their own pace (both CAs are trusted)
4. once nothing presents an old certificate, remove the old CA from truststores

the order matters: trust FIRST, then present. Reversed, a broker offers a certificate
nobody trusts, and every client fails at the same moment.

A practical baseline for a production cluster: TLS on every listener including inter-broker; SASL_SSL or mTLS for clients with a principal mapping you have verified; an authorizer enabled with allow.everyone.if.no.acl.found=false; per-team prefixed ACLs applied from version control; default quotas for every principal; secrets from a secret manager; certificate expiry alerting; and personal data either absent from topics or encrypted client-side.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What are the layers of Kafka security?

Four, and they are independent. Encryption in transit with TLS protects data on the network. Authentication — SASL mechanisms such as SCRAM, Kerberos or OAuth, or mutual TLS — establishes who a client is. Authorization with ACLs decides what that principal may do. Quotas limit how much of the cluster it may use.

The independence matters. Enabling TLS alone encrypts traffic while leaving the cluster open to anyone who can reach it. Enabling SASL without an authorizer means every authenticated client can read and write every topic, which surprises teams who think they have "turned on security". ACLs without authentication are meaningless, because principals are unverified.

What is missing from the list is encryption at rest: brokers write plaintext to disk, so protecting stored data means disk encryption on the hosts, or encrypting sensitive fields in the client before they are ever produced.

A sensible order to introduce them is authentication first, then ACLs in a permissive mode so you can see what would be denied, then enforcement, then quotas.

Beginner: Which ACLs does a consumer need?

Two, and the second is the one that is usually missed: Read on the topic, and Read on the consumer group. Describe on the topic comes with Read in practice, since reading requires describing.

The group ACL exists because a consumer group is itself a resource: joining it, committing offsets and being assigned partitions are operations on that group. Without it, the client authenticates, is allowed to read the topic, and then fails with GroupAuthorizationException when it tries to join — an error that points at the group, not the topic, which is the clue.

Related cases follow the same pattern: a transactional producer also needs Write on its TransactionalId resource, and a Kafka Streams application needs permissions on its internal changelog and repartition topics, which are prefixed with its application.id.

At scale I would grant these with prefixed ACLs — a team's principal gets rights on everything under its topic and group prefix — so the rules stay few and reviewable rather than accumulating per topic.

Mid: A client authenticates successfully but every request is denied. What do you check?

Authentication succeeding while authorization fails means the principal Kafka sees is not the principal your ACLs name.

First, find the actual principal. Enable authorizer logging for denials; the log line names the principal, the operation and the resource for each denied request. That one line usually solves it.

The most common cause with mTLS is the distinguished name. By default the principal is the certificate's full DN — User:CN=orders-service,OU=payments,O=Acme,C=GB — while the ACL was written for User:orders-service. The fix is either to write ACLs against the full DN, or to configure ssl.principal.mapping.rules to extract the common name, and then verify in the logs that the mapping produced what you expected.

Other candidates: the missing group ACL for consumers; a resource pattern mismatch, where a literal ACL was created but the client uses a prefixed name or vice versa; a deny ACL taking precedence over a broad allow; ACLs created against the wrong resource type; or the client connecting on a different listener from the one you configured.

I would also check that allow.everyone.if.no.acl.found is false as intended — if a cluster was working "fine" before ACLs were added, that setting being true may be why, and turning it off is what exposed every missing rule at once.

Mid: How do quotas work, and what should they protect against?

Quotas cap how much of the cluster a principal may use, defined per user, per client id or both. There are byte-rate quotas for producing and consuming, a request quota expressed as a percentage of broker thread time, and a controller mutation quota for topic creation and deletion rates.

Enforcement is by delay, not rejection: when a client exceeds its quota, the broker holds its responses long enough to bring the average back to the limit. The client simply runs slower, and sees it in its throttle-time metrics, so a well-behaved application does not fail — it is paced.

What they protect against is a noisy neighbour: a backfill consumer reading at full disk speed and evicting the page cache that tailing consumers rely on, a batch producer saturating ingress, or a buggy loop creating topics. The request-percentage quota catches the case that byte quotas miss — a client that sends very many tiny requests is cheap in bytes and expensive in handler time.

The two things to be clear about: quotas are a fairness and stability control, not a security boundary — ACLs decide access — and they should be set proactively at onboarding rather than in response to the incident that proves they were needed.

Senior: Design the security model for a multi-tenant cluster used by 40 teams.

Identity: one principal per service, not per team or per person — so that permissions and audit trails map to something that actually connects. I would prefer OAUTHBEARER against the company identity provider, so credentials are short-lived and rotation is automatic; mTLS is a reasonable alternative where a certificate infrastructure already exists, with principal mapping configured and verified.

Naming and ACLs: a naming convention that encodes ownership — <domain>.<entity>.<event> — so prefixed ACLs work. Each team's principal gets rights on its own topic prefix and group prefix; cross-team access is an explicit, reviewed grant on a specific topic. All ACLs live in version control and are applied by automation, so the current state is reviewable and reproducible, with allow.everyone.if.no.acl.found=false and super users limited to the brokers and a break-glass admin.

Quotas: every principal gets defaults at onboarding — produce and fetch byte rates, a request percentage, and a controller mutation rate — sized so normal work is unaffected and the worst case is bounded. Throttle time is monitored per principal, both to spot clients hitting limits and to justify raising them.

Sensitive data: topics classified by sensitivity, with personal data kept out of events where possible and encrypted client-side where not, since a topic ACL is all-or-nothing per record.

Operations: certificate and credential expiry alerting well ahead, secrets from a secret manager, authorizer denial logs retained for audit, and a periodic review that compares granted ACLs with the service inventory.

Recommendation: per-service identities, prefixed ACLs from version control, default quotas, classified topics with client-side encryption for sensitive fields, and rehearsed rotation. The hardest part is not the configuration — it is the automation and review that keep it true a year later.

Senior: Every client lost access at 02:00 with TLS handshake failures. What happened, and how do you prevent it?

Simultaneous, total failure at a round time points at an expiry: a broker certificate, the CA, or a client certificate that every instance shares. TLS handshake errors in both broker and client logs confirm it, and the certificate's notAfter date settles it in seconds.

Immediate recovery is to issue and deploy a replacement certificate. That is a rolling restart under pressure at 02:00, which is why the real work is prevention.

Prevention has three parts. First, alerting: monitor the expiry date of every certificate in the estate — broker, client and CA — and alert 30 days ahead, as a ticket, and again at 7 days as a page. Certificates fail on a schedule, which makes this the most preventable outage in the list.

Second, a rotation procedure that is safe by construction: distribute trust before presenting new certificates. Add the new CA to every truststore and roll; then issue broker certificates from it and roll; then clients, which can move at their own pace because both CAs are trusted; finally remove the old CA. Doing it in the other order is what causes an outage during a rotation.

Third, shorten the cycle. Long-lived certificates make rotation a rare, unpractised event. Short-lived credentials — OAUTHBEARER tokens, or automated certificate issuance with agents that renew — turn it into something that happens continuously and is therefore known to work.

Recommendation: expiry alerting on everything, a documented trust-then-present rotation rehearsed in staging, and a move towards automatically renewed short-lived credentials so the 02:00 version of this incident cannot recur.

Solution

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • TLS, authentication, ACLs and quotas are four independent mechanisms — each useless alone for what the others do.
  • SASL without an authorizer leaves every authenticated client able to read and write everything.
  • Kafka does not encrypt payloads at rest; use disk encryption plus client-side field encryption.
  • Separate listeners for clients, inter-broker and controller traffic, each with its own protocol.
  • With mTLS the principal is the full certificate DN unless ssl.principal.mapping.rules says otherwise.
  • A consumer needs Read on the topic AND Read on the group — the missing group ACL is the classic error.
  • Transactional producers also need Write on their TransactionalId.
  • Prefixed ACLs plus an ownership naming convention keep authorization manageable across many teams.
  • Keep allow.everyone.if.no.acl.found=false and super users to brokers and break-glass only.
  • Quotas throttle by delaying responses, so clients slow down rather than fail.
  • Set default quotas at onboarding — they are how a backfill stops being everyone's incident.
  • Quotas are fairness, not isolation; ACLs decide access.
  • Expired certificates are the most preventable Kafka outage: alert 30+ days ahead.
  • Rotate trust first, then certificates — the reverse order breaks every client at once.
  • Keep ACLs in version control, log denials, and review permissions against the service inventory.
