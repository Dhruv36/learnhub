Rebalancing & Group Membership Delivery & Durability

What you'll master: every event that triggers a rebalance, including the ones nobody expects; the classic protocol's JoinGroup/SyncGroup rounds and why an eager rebalance stops the whole group; why one slow member can hold every other member idle for up to max.poll.interval.ms; cooperative incremental rebalancing and the single rolling bounce that switches a default-configured group to it; static membership and its trade-off; the new broker-driven protocol that became generally available in Kafka 4.0; the autoscaling feedback loop in which scaling on lag creates more lag; and the metrics that tell you how much rebalancing actually costs you. Prerequisites: Idempotence & Transactions and Consumers & Consumer Groups.

Part 1 — What Triggers a Rebalance

A rebalance redistributes a group's partitions among its current members. It is necessary — a group must adapt when members come and go — but each one interrupts consumption, so the first step in taming rebalances is knowing what causes them.

TriggerTypical causeAvoidable?
A member joinsScale-out, a deploy starting a new pod, a restartRestarts: yes, with static membership
A member leaves cleanlyScale-in, a deploy stopping a pod, close()Restarts: yes, with static membership
Session timeoutCrash, network partition, a GC pause longer than session.timeout.ms (45 s)Partly: fix pauses; tune the timeout
Poll timeoutProcessing a batch took longer than max.poll.interval.ms (5 min)Yes: fit batches inside the budget
Subscription changeA regex subscription matches a newly created topicYes: explicit topic lists
Partition count changePartitions added to a subscribed topicPlanned, so schedule it

A rolling deploy is a rebalance storm by default. Replacing ten consumers one at a time, each stop and each start triggers a rebalance: twenty rebalances in a few minutes. With the eager protocol, each one pauses the whole group. The rest of this lesson is largely about reducing that number or the cost of each one.

Each completed rebalance starts a new generation of the group, numbered upwards. The generation is part of every commit and every transactional offset commit, which is how the coordinator rejects work from members of an older generation — the zombie protection seen in the offsets and transactions lessons.

Part 2 — The Classic Protocol, and the Stop-the-World Rebalance

In the classic protocol (every Kafka version, and still the default client behaviour), the brokers do not compute assignments. One consumer, the group leader, does it, in two rounds:

coordinator                  consumer A (leader)      consumer B            consumer C (new)
    │  ◄──────────────── JoinGroup ───────────────── JoinGroup ─────────── JoinGroup
    │    waits until every known member has rejoined, up to the REBALANCE TIMEOUT
    │    (= each member's max.poll.interval.ms)
    │  ───── JoinGroup response: member list + subscriptions (to the leader only) ─────►
    │                         leader runs the ASSIGNOR, client-side
    │  ◄──────── SyncGroup(assignment) ──── SyncGroup ─────────── SyncGroup
    │  ───── each member receives its partitions ──────────────────────────────►
    │                         generation N+1 begins

With an eager assignor (range, round-robin, sticky), every member revokes all of its partitions before rejoining, and nobody consumes anything until the new assignment is delivered. The pause covers the whole group, not just the partitions that move.

eager rebalance when consumer C joins a group of A and B (6 partitions)

before:   A: p0 p1 p2        B: p3 p4 p5
revoke:   A: —               B: —              ◄── ALL consumption stops
assign:   A: p0 p1           B: p2 p3          C: p4 p5
          (4 of 6 partitions never needed to stop at all)
🔑 The slowest member sets the length of everyone's pause. The coordinator waits for every known member to send JoinGroup, and a member only does so on its next poll(). If one consumer is 90 seconds into processing a large batch, every other member — which has already revoked its partitions and rejoined — sits idle for those 90 seconds. The wait can last up to max.poll.interval.ms, 5 minutes by default. Raising max.poll.interval.ms to "fix" slow batches therefore also lengthens the worst-case stall of every rebalance. Part 3 — Cooperative Incremental Rebalancing

The cooperative protocol (KIP-429, Kafka 2.4) keeps the classic JoinGroup/SyncGroup rounds but changes what members give up. Members keep their partitions while rejoining, and only partitions that must move are revoked — in a second, follow-up rebalance.

cooperative rebalance when consumer C joins (6 partitions)

round 1:  A keeps p0 p1 p2, B keeps p3 p4 p5 while rejoining (still consuming)
          assignor decides: move p2 → C and p5 → C
          A revokes ONLY p2, B revokes ONLY p5
round 2:  C receives p2 p5
          p0 p1 p3 p4 were consumed throughout
Eager (range, round-robin, sticky)Cooperative sticky
Partitions pausedAll, for the whole rebalanceOnly those moving
RoundsOneTwo when anything moves
onPartitionsRevoked receivesEverything the member ownedOnly the partitions leaving it
BalanceDepends on the assignorBalanced, moving as little as possible

Switching is a one-bounce change for default configurations. Since Kafka 3.0 the Java consumer's default partition.assignment.strategy is [RangeAssignor, CooperativeStickyAssignor]: the group uses range (the first strategy every member supports) while advertising cooperative support. A single rolling bounce that removes RangeAssignor from the list moves the group to cooperative sticky.

today (default):   partition.assignment.strategy = [RangeAssignor, CooperativeStickyAssignor]  → range is used
rolling bounce:    partition.assignment.strategy = [CooperativeStickyAssignor]
                   from the FIRST restart the group selects cooperative-sticky — the only strategy every
                   member now shares. Members still listing range keep EAGER revocation (a consumer uses the
                   rebalance protocol all of its own assignors support) until they restart too.

❌ Switching an explicitly configured eager group straight to cooperative in one bounce. If members currently list only RangeAssignor, a restarted member listing only CooperativeStickyAssignor shares no strategy with the rest, and it fails to join with InconsistentGroupProtocolException — or, depending on timing, the group runs with members that disagree about revocation semantics.

✅ Two bounces when starting from an explicit eager list: first add the cooperative assignor after the eager one ([range, cooperative-sticky]) on every member, then do a second bounce removing the eager one. Groups on the 3.0+ default list already completed the first step. Also review rebalance listeners: code that flushed everything in onPartitionsRevoked now only needs to handle the partitions passed to it.

Part 4 — Static Membership

With dynamic membership, a consumer that restarts is a new member: its departure triggers one rebalance and its return another. Static membership (KIP-345, Kafka 2.3) gives each consumer a persistent identity, group.instance.id, and the coordinator keeps that member's partitions reserved while it is briefly away.

group.instance.id = orders-consumer-3        (stable per instance: e.g. the StatefulSet pod name)

dynamic member restarts:   leave → REBALANCE → (restart) → join → REBALANCE
static member restarts:    (goes silent) → restart within session.timeout.ms → rejoins with the same id
                           → gets its old partitions back → NO rebalance
DynamicStatic
Restart within the session timeoutTwo rebalancesNone
Clean shutdown (close())Leaves at once; partitions move immediatelyDoes not leave; partitions wait for the session timeout
Instance truly goneDetected after the session timeoutDetected after the session timeout
Typical session.timeout.ms45 sRaised to cover a restart, e.g. 2–5 minutes

The trade-off is detection time. To survive a restart without rebalancing, the session timeout must be longer than the restart takes. But the same timeout decides how long a genuinely dead instance's partitions go unread. A static group with a 5-minute session timeout that loses a node for good stops reading that node's partitions for 5 minutes. Choose the timeout from how long restarts actually take, and alert on lag so a dead static member is noticed.

Ids must be unique among running instances. If two consumers start with the same group.instance.id, the coordinator fences the older one with FencedInstanceIdException. That is correct behaviour for a replaced pod, but it also means that a deployment which briefly runs old and new pods side by side with the same ids (a surge) will fence one of them. StatefulSets, which replace a pod only after the old one is gone, fit static membership naturally.

Part 5 — The New Consumer Group Protocol (KIP-848)

Kafka 4.0 made a redesigned protocol generally available. It removes the group-wide synchronisation barrier altogether: the broker computes assignments, and each member converges on its target assignment independently through its regular heartbeats.

classic:   every member stops at a barrier (JoinGroup), the leader assigns, everyone resumes (SyncGroup)
KIP-848:   the group coordinator holds a TARGET assignment and updates it when membership changes
           each member's heartbeat response says "release p2" or "you now own p5"
           members reconcile one at a time; there is no point at which the whole group waits
AspectClassic protocolKIP-848 (group.protocol=consumer)
Who assignsThe leader consumer, client-sideThe group coordinator, broker-side
SynchronisationGroup-wide barrierNone; incremental per member
Slow member effectDelays everyone's rebalanceDelays only the partitions it must release
Session and heartbeat settingsClient (session.timeout.ms, heartbeat.interval.ms)Broker (group.consumer.session.timeout.ms, group.consumer.heartbeat.interval.ms)
Choosing an assignorpartition.assignment.strategyBroker default, or group.remote.assignor (e.g. uniform, range)

Adopting it: brokers must run a version with the protocol enabled (it is on by default in 4.0), and consumers opt in with group.protocol=consumer. Client settings that the broker now owns — the session timeout, heartbeat interval and client-side assignor — no longer apply and should be removed from the consumer configuration. Brokers can convert an existing classic group as its members roll over to the new protocol, governed by the broker's migration policy setting, so a group can move without being stopped. Test the upgrade path on a staging group first: custom client-side assignors and some older frameworks do not carry over.

The new protocol addresses the biggest structural cost of classic rebalances. It does not remove the need for the rest of this lesson: a consumer that exceeds its poll interval still loses its partitions, and moving a partition still means its new owner starts from the last commit.

Part 6 — Rebalances in Production: Measuring and Avoiding Storms

Rebalances are cheap when rare and expensive when constant. The consumer reports what they cost, in the consumer-coordinator-metrics group:

rebalance-rate-per-hour        how often — should be near zero outside deploys
rebalance-latency-avg / max    how long each takes, from start to new assignment
failed-rebalance-total         rebalances that failed and had to restart
last-rebalance-seconds-ago     has the group been stable lately?
partition-revoked-latency-avg  time spent in onPartitionsRevoked (your listener's cost)

The autoscaling feedback loop. Scaling consumers on lag seems natural, but each scaling action is a membership change, and each membership change is a rebalance that pauses consumption and increases lag:

lag rises → autoscaler adds 2 pods → 2 rebalances (eager: whole group paused ~20 s each)
          → lag rises further during the pauses → autoscaler adds 2 more pods → 2 more rebalances
          → pods beyond the partition count are idle, but still trigger rebalances when they come and go
          → when lag finally falls, scale-in causes another round of rebalances
MeasureWhy it breaks the loop
Cap replicas at the partition countExtra members add rebalances and no capacity
Scale on lag in time, with long stabilisation windowsA brief spike during a rebalance does not trigger another scale event
Scale in slowly and in stepsScale-in is not urgent; each step is a rebalance
Cooperative sticky or KIP-848Each rebalance pauses only the moving partitions
Static membership for restartsDeploys and pod restarts stop causing rebalances

❌ Treating frequent rebalances as normal background noise. A group showing forty rebalances an hour looks like it "recovers each time", so nobody investigates. In fact it spends a large share of every hour paused, reprocesses records after each move, and rebuilds any local caches it keeps per partition. The lag graph shows a sawtooth that everyone has learned to ignore.

✅ Alert on rebalance-rate-per-hour outside deploy windows, and find the trigger in the consumer logs: members leaving for poll timeouts point to slow batches; session timeouts point to GC pauses or network trouble; joins and leaves point to autoscaling or crash loops. Each has a specific fix, and none is "rebalances are just how Kafka works".

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is a consumer group rebalance, and what triggers one?

A rebalance is how a consumer group redistributes partitions among its members. The group's coordinator starts one whenever the membership or the subscription changes, and at the end each member has a new set of partitions and the group has a new generation number.

Triggers include a member joining (a new instance, a restart), a member leaving cleanly, a member being removed because it stopped heartbeating for session.timeout.ms or did not call poll() within max.poll.interval.ms, a change in the subscribed topics (for example, a regex subscription matching a new topic), and partitions being added to a subscribed topic.

Rebalances matter because they interrupt consumption. With the classic eager protocol, every member stops consuming every partition until the rebalance completes. Partitions that move also restart from the last committed offset, so some records are processed again.

So the goals are to have fewer of them — avoiding poll timeouts, using static membership for restarts — and to make each cheaper, with cooperative rebalancing or Kafka 4.0's new group protocol.

Beginner: How does cooperative rebalancing differ from eager rebalancing?

In an eager rebalance, every member revokes all its partitions at the start, the group computes a new assignment, and then members start consuming their new partitions. All consumption stops for the duration, even for partitions that end up with the same owner.

In a cooperative rebalance, members keep their partitions while the group computes the new assignment. Only partitions that need to move are revoked from their current owners, and a follow-up rebalance gives them to their new owners. Everything else is consumed throughout.

The cost is an extra round when partitions move, and listener code must cope with receiving only the partitions that are leaving rather than all of them. The benefit is large for big groups and frequent membership changes, such as rolling deploys.

In the Java client it is the CooperativeStickyAssignor. Since Kafka 3.0 the default assignor list includes it after the range assignor, so a group on defaults can switch with one rolling bounce that removes range from the list.

Mid: A group of 30 consumers pauses for several minutes whenever one pod is replaced. Why, and how do you fix it?

Several minutes is far longer than a rebalance normally takes, which suggests the classic protocol's barrier: the coordinator waits for every member to rejoin, and a member only rejoins on its next poll(). If some members are in the middle of long batches, everyone else waits for them — up to max.poll.interval.ms. If that setting has been raised to accommodate slow processing, the wait can be many minutes. With eager assignment, the whole group consumes nothing during it.

And a pod replacement causes two rebalances: one when the old pod leaves, one when the new pod joins.

The fixes, in order of impact: shorten the poll loop — reduce max.poll.records so each batch finishes in seconds, then lower max.poll.interval.ms back towards its default, which bounds the wait. Switch to cooperative sticky, so members keep consuming their unaffected partitions during the rebalance. Use static membership with a session timeout longer than a pod restart, so replacing a pod causes no rebalance at all. And evaluate the KIP-848 protocol on Kafka 4.0, which has no group-wide barrier.

I would confirm the diagnosis first from rebalance-latency-max and the consumer logs, which show how long each member took to rejoin.

Mid: What is static membership, and what does it cost?

Static membership gives each consumer a persistent identity with group.instance.id. The coordinator remembers which partitions belong to that id, and if the consumer disconnects and comes back with the same id within session.timeout.ms, it gets the same partitions back without a rebalance. Rolling restarts and brief network interruptions stop disturbing the rest of the group.

The cost is detection time. The session timeout has to be longer than a restart takes, often a few minutes, and during that time a consumer that has genuinely died still holds its partitions and nobody reads them. Even a clean close() does not hand partitions over immediately, because a static member does not send a leave request.

There are also identity rules. Ids must be stable across restarts of an instance and unique between running instances; a second consumer starting with the same id fences the first. That fits StatefulSets well, but not deployments that run old and new pods side by side.

I would use it for groups where restarts are frequent and partitions carry expensive local state — a cache or a Kafka Streams state store — where avoiding a move is worth more than faster failure detection, and pair it with a lag alert tuned to the session timeout.

Senior: Consumers autoscale on lag, and during traffic peaks the group rebalances constantly and lag gets worse. Explain and redesign.

The autoscaler and the rebalance protocol are fighting each other. Every scale-out adds members, and every member added triggers a rebalance. With eager assignment each rebalance pauses the whole group, so lag rises during it; the autoscaler sees higher lag and adds more pods, which triggers more rebalances. Pods beyond the partition count add no capacity but still cause rebalances when they start and stop. When the peak passes, scale-in triggers another series.

Redesign, in layers. First, make the scaling signal robust: scale on lag measured in time, over a window long enough to ignore the spike a single rebalance causes, with a stabilisation period after each action, and scale in slowly. Second, bound it: maximum replicas equal to the partition count. Third, make each rebalance cheap: cooperative sticky assignment or the KIP-848 protocol, so adding a member moves a few partitions while everything else keeps flowing.

Fourth, question autoscaling itself. Consumer capacity is bounded by partitions, and a group sized for the peak — with each instance processing efficiently — often costs little more than one that scales, without the operational instability. For predictable daily peaks, scheduled scaling ahead of the peak avoids scaling in the middle of it.

Recommendation: move the group to cooperative rebalancing (or KIP-848 on 4.0), cap replicas at the partition count, scale on time lag with long windows and slow scale-in, and measure rebalance-rate-per-hour before and after. If peaks are predictable, replace reactive scaling with a schedule.

Senior: Should a team move its consumer groups to the KIP-848 protocol now? How would you plan it?

The protocol removes the classic design's main weakness — the group-wide barrier where the slowest member holds everyone up — and moves assignment to the broker, which also makes rebalances easier to observe and reason about centrally. For large groups with frequent membership changes, the benefit is real.

Preconditions: brokers on Kafka 4.0 or later with the protocol enabled, and consumers on a client version that supports group.protocol=consumer. Anything that relies on the classic protocol's specifics needs checking: custom client-side assignors, frameworks that manage rebalances themselves, and tools that read group state. Kafka Streams applications have their own rebalance protocol work, separate from this one, and should be treated separately.

Plan: start with a low-risk group in staging; remove client settings that the broker now owns (session timeout, heartbeat interval, assignment strategy) and set the broker-side equivalents deliberately; roll the members over and confirm the group converts without stopping; compare rebalance latency, lag during a rolling deploy, and error rates against the classic baseline. Then move production groups one at a time, starting with those that suffer most from rebalances.

Recommendation: yes for groups where rebalances are a measured problem, after a staging trial; no hurry for small, stable groups that rarely rebalance, where cooperative sticky assignment already captures most of the benefit. Keep the rollback — switching members back to the classic protocol — tested until the new protocol has run through a few production deploys.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Rebalances follow membership and subscription changes: joins, leaves, session and poll timeouts, regex matches, partition additions.
  • A rolling deploy causes two rebalances per instance unless membership is static.
  • Each rebalance starts a new generation, which is how commits from zombies are rejected.
  • The classic protocol has a group-wide barrier: the leader consumer assigns partitions after every member rejoins.
  • Eager assignors revoke every partition, stopping the whole group even for partitions that do not move.
  • The slowest member sets everyone's pause, up to max.poll.interval.ms; raising it lengthens rebalances.
  • Cooperative sticky revokes only moving partitions and keeps the rest flowing, at the cost of a second round.
  • The 3.0+ default assignor list allows a one-bounce switch to cooperative; explicit eager lists need two bounces.
  • Under cooperative assignment, onPartitionsRevoked receives only leaving partitions.
  • Static membership avoids rebalances on restarts within session.timeout.ms, but delays detection of dead members by the same amount.
  • Static ids must be stable per instance and unique among running instances; duplicates are fenced.
  • Kafka 4.0's KIP-848 protocol moves assignment to the broker and removes the group-wide barrier; opt in with group.protocol=consumer.
  • Under KIP-848, session, heartbeat and assignor settings move to the broker.
  • Autoscaling on lag can feed itself; cap replicas at partitions and scale on time lag with long windows.
  • Alert on rebalance-rate-per-hour; frequent rebalances are a defect with a findable cause.
