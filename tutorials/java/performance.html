Performance & Profiling JVM & Performance

What you'll master: why Amdahl's law caps your speedup before you start, so optimising a 5% term can never win; latency versus throughput versus utilisation, and why averages actively lie — including tail amplification, where a request made of ten subcalls has a p99 far worse than the subcalls' p99; Little's Law as the sizing tool; safepoint bias, the flaw that makes most JVM profilers systematically blame the wrong methods, and the tools that avoid it; how to read a flame graph and which profiling mode answers which question — CPU, allocation, lock, and the wall-clock mode that is often the only one showing the problem; the findings that actually recur in production; and when the JIT has already done the optimisation you are about to break. Prerequisites: JVM Architecture and Garbage Collection.

Part 1 — The Discipline Before the Tools

  THREE RULES, IN ORDER. MOST WASTED OPTIMISATION EFFORT BREAKS ONE.

  1. MEASURE, DO NOT GUESS.
     Human intuition about where time goes is reliably wrong. The
     hot method is routinely something nobody suspected — a
     toString() in a log statement, a regex recompiled per call, an
     exception constructed on a common path.

  2. OPTIMISE THE BIGGEST TERM.
     AMDAHL'S LAW bounds the whole exercise:

       speedup = 1 / ((1 - p) + p/s)

       p = fraction of time in the part you improve
       s = how much faster you make it

     If a function is 5% of runtime and you make it INFINITELY
     fast:

       speedup = 1 / 0.95 = 1.05     — a 5% win, at best, ever.

     If it is 80% and you make it twice as fast:

       speedup = 1 / (0.2 + 0.4) = 1.67   — a 40% win.

     So the profile decides what is worth touching, before any
     cleverness.

  3. STOP WHEN IT IS FAST ENOUGH.
     Performance work has a target. Without one you will trade
     readability for microseconds nobody notices.

  AND THE RULE THAT DOMINATES ALL THREE:

    ALGORITHMS AND ARCHITECTURE BEAT MICRO-OPTIMISATION BY ORDERS
    OF MAGNITUDE.

      O(n²) → O(n log n) on 1M items:  ~50,000× fewer operations
      caching a 200 ms call:            200 ms → 0.1 ms
      removing an N+1 query:            101 round trips → 1
      replacing a String concat loop:   O(n²) bytes → O(n)

    versus a hand-tuned inner loop, which might buy you 20%.

  THE HONEST ORDER OF OPERATIONS:

    1. is the algorithm right?
    2. are you doing work you could avoid entirely (caching,
       batching, laziness)?
    3. are you waiting on something (I/O, locks, GC)?
    4. only then, is the code itself slow?

  Step 4 is where most people start, and it is the smallest term in
  almost every real system.

Part 2 — Latency, Throughput and Why Averages Lie

  THREE DIFFERENT NUMBERS, ROUTINELY CONFUSED:

    LATENCY      how long ONE operation takes           (ms)
    THROUGHPUT   how many operations per unit time      (ops/s)
    UTILISATION  what fraction of capacity is in use    (%)

  They are not the same goal and often conflict. Batching improves
  throughput and WORSENS latency. Adding threads improves throughput
  until contention makes latency collapse.

  THE AVERAGE IS THE WORST SINGLE STATISTIC YOU CAN REPORT.

    99 requests at 10 ms + 1 request at 5,000 ms
    mean = 59.9 ms

  That 59.9 ms describes nothing that happened. No user waited
  59.9 ms; 99 waited 10 ms and one waited five seconds. Response
  time distributions are heavily right-skewed, so the mean sits in
  an empty region between the bulk and the tail.

  USE PERCENTILES:

    p50    the typical experience
    p95    where degradation starts to be felt
    p99    the tail that generates support tickets
    p999   what your largest customer sees constantly
    max    the worst case, and where timeouts must be set

  *** TAIL AMPLIFICATION — THE ARITHMETIC PEOPLE MISS ***

  If one request makes 10 independent subcalls and must wait for
  all of them, the chance that NONE hits the subcall p99 is:

    0.99^10 = 0.904

  So ~10% of requests hit at least one p99 subcall. YOUR p90 IS
  THE SUBCALLS' p99. The more fan-out, the worse:

    10 subcalls   → ~10% hit a p99 subcall
    100 subcalls  → ~63% do

  This is why microservice architectures have tail-latency problems
  that no individual service's dashboard shows. Each service looks
  healthy at p99; the composed request does not.

  LITTLE'S LAW IS THE SIZING TOOL:

    L = λ × W     (concurrency = arrival rate × time in system)

    1,000 req/s × 0.2 s = 200 concurrent requests

  Use it to size pools, predict queue depth, and sanity-check
  capacity. It also tells you the leverage: halving W halves the
  concurrency you need, which is usually cheaper than doubling
  capacity.

  ONE MORE TRAP — COORDINATED OMISSION. A load generator that
  waits for a response before sending the next request STOPS
  MEASURING during a stall, so the pause is missing from the
  histogram. Reported p99 can be an order of magnitude optimistic.
  Use a tool that sends at a fixed rate regardless of responses.

Part 3 — Why Most Profilers Lie

  TWO KINDS OF PROFILER, WITH DIFFERENT FAILURE MODES:

    INSTRUMENTING   inserts code at method entry/exit. Exact call
                    counts, but 10-100× overhead, and it DEFEATS
                    INLINING — so the JIT optimises differently and
                    you profile a program that does not exist.
    SAMPLING        periodically captures stacks. ~1-2% overhead,
                    statistically representative — IF the sampling
                    is unbiased.

  *** SAFEPOINT BIAS — WHY NAIVE SAMPLERS BLAME THE WRONG CODE ***

  Most JVM profilers sample via JVMTI's GetStackTrace, which can
  only run when a thread is at a SAFEPOINT — a point where the JVM
  knows the exact object layout of the frame. Safepoints are placed
  at method returns, loop back-edges (non-counted loops), and
  allocation sites.

  THE CONSEQUENCES:

    - a hot method the JIT INLINED may contain no safepoint at all,
      so it is never sampled — it becomes invisible
    - a COUNTED LOOP (int index, known bounds) has its safepoint
      poll optimised away, so a tight loop spinning for
      milliseconds is never sampled
    - samples pile up at whatever safepoint follows the real work,
      attributing time to the WRONG method

  Measured effect: safepoint-biased profilers routinely disagree
  with unbiased ones about which method is hottest. This is not a
  small correction.

  THE TOOLS THAT DO NOT HAVE THIS PROBLEM:

    async-profiler   uses AsyncGetCallTrace (and perf_events for
                     kernel frames), sampling at arbitrary points.
                     Low overhead, shows native and JIT frames.
    JFR              built into the JDK, ~1% overhead, designed for
                     always-on production use. Its execution
                     sampler is not safepoint-biased.

  DO NOT PROFILE WITH:
    - a debugger attached (it disables JIT optimisations)
    - an IDE's built-in instrumenting profiler for hot-path work
    - System.currentTimeMillis() around a block (see Benchmarking
      & JMH for why that measures almost nothing useful)

  THE MODES, AND WHICH QUESTION EACH ANSWERS:

    CPU          "what is burning cores?"   — useless if you are
                 waiting rather than computing
    ALLOCATION   "what is creating garbage?" — often the real cause
                 of GC pressure
    LOCK         "what am I blocked on?"    — contention by monitor
    WALL CLOCK   "where does REAL TIME go?" — includes waiting, and
                 is frequently the ONLY mode that shows the problem

  THE CLASSIC MISTAKE: profiling a slow service in CPU mode, finding
  nothing above 3%, and concluding the profiler is broken. The
  service was waiting on a database. Wall-clock mode shows it
  instantly.

Part 4 — Reading a Flame Graph

  A FLAME GRAPH AGGREGATES THOUSANDS OF STACK SAMPLES:

    ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────┐
    │                  main                        │  ← bottom = root
    ├───────────────────────┬──────────────────────┤
    │   handleRequest       │   backgroundTask     │
    ├──────────┬────────────┼──────────────────────┤
    │ validate │  loadUser  │       flush          │
    ├──────────┼─────┬──────┼──────────────────────┤
    │          │ sql │ map  │                      │
    └──────────┴─────┴──────┴──────────────────────┘

    X-AXIS = fraction of samples (NOT time order — it is sorted
             alphabetically so identical stacks merge)
    Y-AXIS = stack depth
    WIDTH  = the only thing that matters. Wide is hot.
    COLOUR = usually meaningless; some tools colour by frame type
             (Java / native / JIT-compiled)

  HOW TO READ ONE, IN ORDER:

    1. Look for WIDE PLATEAUS at the top. A wide frame with nothing
       above it is code actually executing — that is your hot spot.
    2. Ignore wide frames LOW in the graph. main() is always 100%
       wide and tells you nothing.
    3. Look for the SAME FRAME IN MANY PLACES. A function called
       from five paths may be individually narrow but collectively
       dominant — that is what an inverted (icicle) graph or a
       "focus on function" filter reveals.
    4. Look for things that SHOULD NOT BE THERE AT ALL: regex
       compilation, reflection, exception fill-in, string
       formatting, class loading in a steady-state process.

  DIFFERENTIAL FLAME GRAPHS compare two profiles and colour by
  delta. This is by far the fastest way to answer "what changed
  after the deploy" — much faster than reading either profile.

  WHAT A FLAME GRAPH CANNOT TELL YOU:

    - anything about TIME ORDER (it is aggregated)
    - whether a wide frame is slow or merely called often
    - latency distribution — a p99 problem may be invisible in a
      profile dominated by the p50 path

  For those, you need traces (per-request timelines) or a histogram,
  not a profile.

Part 5 — What Profiling Actually Finds

  THE SAME HANDFUL OF THINGS, ACROSS MOST CODEBASES.

  1. N+1 QUERIES — the most common and the most expensive.

     for (Order o : orders) o.getCustomer().getName();  // lazy load
     // 1 query for orders + 100 for customers

     101 round trips at 1 ms each = 101 ms of pure latency. Fix with
     a join fetch, an entity graph, or a batch load. This is usually
     the single biggest win available in an ORM-backed service.

  2. LOGGING IN A HOT LOOP.

     log.debug("processing " + order);      // ❌ concatenates even
                                            // when debug is OFF
     log.debug("processing {}", order);     // ✓ formats lazily

     The first builds a string and calls toString() on every
     iteration regardless of log level. Under a profiler this shows
     up as time in StringBuilder and in your own toString().

  3. REGEX COMPILED PER CALL.

     if (s.matches("[a-z]+"))               // ❌ compiles the
                                            // pattern EVERY call
     private static final Pattern P =
         Pattern.compile("[a-z]+");         // ✓ compile once
     if (P.matcher(s).matches())

     Compilation is orders of magnitude more expensive than
     matching. String.matches, split, and replaceAll all hide it.

  4. STRING CONCATENATION IN A LOOP.

     for (…) result += item;     // O(n²) — a new String each time

     Use StringBuilder, or a Collector. On 10,000 items this is the
     difference between milliseconds and many seconds.

  5. BOXING IN A TIGHT LOOP.

     Map<Integer,Integer> counts;  // every entry allocates
     long sum = 0; for (Integer i : list) sum += i;  // unbox each

     Use primitive arrays, IntStream, or a primitive-specialised
     collection where it is measurably hot.

  6. AN UNBOUNDED CACHE that became a memory leak, so the real
     symptom is GC pressure rather than CPU.

  7. EXCEPTIONS ON A COMMON PATH. Constructing one captures the
     stack trace, which costs microseconds — fine for exceptional
     cases, disastrous at 10,000/second.

  8. WORK THAT DID NOT NEED DOING: serialising a field nobody
     reads, computing a value discarded by the caller, sorting a
     collection that is then filtered to one element.

  NOTICE THAT ALMOST NONE OF THESE ARE "THIS ALGORITHM IS
  INEFFICIENT". They are avoidable work, hidden allocation, and
  round trips.

Part 6 — What the JIT Already Did

  BEFORE HAND-OPTIMISING, KNOW WHAT YOU MAY BE UNDOING.

  INLINING — the enabling optimisation. A small hot method is
  copied into its caller, after which constant folding, dead code
  elimination and escape analysis can all see across the boundary.
  Rough thresholds: <35 bytecodes always, <325 if hot.
  So a "wasteful" getter costs nothing, and manually inlining it
  buys nothing while making the code worse.

  ESCAPE ANALYSIS — if an object provably never escapes its
  method, the JIT may SCALAR REPLACE it: no allocation at all, just
  registers.

    Point p = new Point(x, y);     // may never be allocated
    return p.x() + p.y();

  This is why "avoid creating objects" is bad blanket advice, and
  why a StringBuilder in a local variable costs the same as manual
  char handling.

  LOCK ELISION — a lock on a provably thread-local object is
  removed entirely. This is why StringBuffer in a local variable
  performs like StringBuilder.

  LOOP OPTIMISATIONS — unrolling, range-check elimination when
  bounds are provable, and vectorisation of simple loops into SIMD
  instructions.

  DEOPTIMIZATION — the JIT speculates ("this call site has only
  ever seen ArrayList") and compiles a fast path guarded by a check.
  If the assumption breaks, it falls back to the interpreter and
  recompiles. This is why introducing a second implementation of an
  interface on a hot path can cause a real, measurable slowdown:
  a monomorphic call site became polymorphic.

  WHAT THIS MEANS PRACTICALLY:

    ✓ WRITE CLEAR CODE. The JIT optimises idiomatic code best,
      because that is what it was tuned against.
    ✗ DO NOT hand-inline, cache values in locals "to help", or
      avoid small objects on principle.
    ✓ DO reduce ALGORITHMIC work and AVOIDABLE work — the JIT
      cannot fix an N+1 query or an O(n²) loop.

  MECHANICAL SYMPATHY, for the cases where the code really is the
  bottleneck:

    CACHE LINES     64 bytes. Sequential access is ~10× faster than
                    random. An array beats a linked list for
                    traversal even at equal complexity.
    LAYOUT          array-of-structs vs struct-of-arrays: iterating
                    one field of many objects touches a cache line
                    per object; parallel primitive arrays touch one
                    per 8-16 values.
    BRANCH PREDICTION  a well-predicted branch is ~1 cycle, a
                    mispredict ~15-20. Sorting data before a
                    branchy loop can make it several times faster.
    FALSE SHARING   two hot fields on one cache line, written by
                    different cores — see Concurrency Patterns.

  These matter in the innermost 1% of code. Reach for them last.

Interview Questions

Beginner: Why are percentiles better than averages for latency?

Because response-time distributions are heavily right-skewed, so the mean lands in an empty region between the bulk and the tail.

99 requests at 10 ms + 1 request at 5,000 ms  →  mean = 59.9 ms

No user waited 59.9 ms. Ninety-nine waited 10 ms and one waited five seconds. The average describes nothing that happened, and it hides both the typical experience and the problem.

PercentileTells you
p50The typical experience
p95Where degradation starts to be felt
p99The tail that generates support tickets
p999 / maxWhat your largest customer sees, and where timeouts belong

A useful reframing: at 1,000 requests per second, p99 is ten requests every second — not a rare edge case. And a user making 10 requests to load a page has a good chance of hitting it at least once.

Beginner: What does Amdahl's law tell you about where to optimise?

That your maximum possible speedup is capped by the fraction of time you are not touching.

speedup = 1 / ((1 - p) + p/s)

If a method is 5% of runtime and you make it infinitely fast, the ceiling is 1 / 0.95 = 1.05 — a 5% improvement, forever. If it is 80% and you merely double its speed, you get 1 / (0.2 + 0.4) = 1.67, a 40% win.

The practical consequence is that the profile decides what is worth touching, before any cleverness. Optimising the code you find interesting rather than the code that dominates is the most common way performance effort is wasted.

It also explains why algorithmic and architectural changes dominate: they usually target the biggest term, and they change it by orders of magnitude rather than percentages.

Mid: What is safepoint bias and why does it matter?

Most JVM profilers sample stacks through JVMTI's GetStackTrace, which can only run when a thread is at a safepoint — a point where the JVM knows the frame's exact object layout. Safepoints sit at method returns, non-counted loop back-edges, and allocation sites.

The result is systematic, not random, error:

  • A method the JIT inlined may contain no safepoint, so it is never sampled and becomes invisible.
  • A counted loop (int index, known bounds) has its safepoint poll optimised away, so a tight loop spinning for milliseconds is never observed.
  • Samples pile up at whatever safepoint follows the real work, attributing time to the wrong method.

This is not a small correction — safepoint-biased and unbiased profilers routinely disagree about which method is hottest.

The fix is the tool. async-profiler uses AsyncGetCallTrace and perf_events, sampling at arbitrary points; JFR's execution sampler is likewise not safepoint-biased and is built for always-on production use at around 1% overhead.

Mid: How do you read a flame graph?

Width is sample count — the only dimension that matters. The x-axis is not time; stacks are sorted alphabetically so identical ones merge. The y-axis is stack depth, and colour is usually meaningless.

  1. Look for wide plateaus at the top. A wide frame with nothing above it is code actually executing — the hot spot.
  2. Ignore wide frames at the bottom. main is always 100% wide.
  3. Look for one frame appearing in many places. Individually narrow, collectively dominant — an inverted graph or a focus filter reveals it.
  4. Look for what should not be there: regex compilation, reflection, exception fill-in, class loading in a steady-state process.

Differential flame graphs compare two profiles and colour by delta, which is the fastest way to answer "what changed after the deploy".

What it cannot tell you: anything about time order, whether a wide frame is slow or merely called often, or anything about the latency distribution — a p99 problem can be invisible in a profile dominated by the p50 path. For those you need traces or histograms.

Senior: A service's p99 is 2s while p50 is 20ms. How do you investigate?

A 100× gap means the slow requests are doing something structurally different — this is not "the code is slow", so a CPU profile will mostly show the fast path and mislead you.

HypothesisSignatureCheck
GC pausesSpikes correlate across all endpoints simultaneously-Xlog:gc*; align pause times with slow requests
Lock contentionSlow only under concurrencyLock profile; thread dumps during a spike
Pool exhaustionLatency ≈ a timeout value, or steps in multiples of itConnection-pool wait-time metrics
Tail amplificationFan-out endpoints onlyDistributed traces of slow requests
Data skewCorrelates with specific inputs or tenantsLog request parameters on slow requests
Cold pathFirst request after idle; class loading in the trace-XX:+PrintCompilation

The right tool is distributed tracing, not profiling. A profile aggregates and so is dominated by the 99% of fast requests; a trace shows one slow request's timeline and where its two seconds went. Capture traces filtered to slow requests specifically.

Tail amplification deserves an explicit check when there is fan-out. Ten subcalls at p99 = 1% each means about 10% of requests hit at least one — so your p90 is your dependencies' p99, and each individual service's dashboard looks healthy.

Fixes differ entirely by cause: a smaller heap or a different collector for GC; a shorter critical section for contention; a larger or better-sized pool for exhaustion; hedged requests or fewer calls for amplification. Guessing wrong here costs weeks, which is why identifying the signature first matters more than the fix.

Senior: When should you not micro-optimise?

Almost always — and the reasons are concrete rather than stylistic.

Amdahl's law caps the payoff. Micro-optimisation targets small terms by definition, so even a perfect result is a few percent.

The JIT has usually done it already. Inlining removes call overhead, escape analysis can eliminate an allocation entirely, lock elision removes uncontended locks, and loops get unrolled and vectorised. Hand-inlining, caching fields in locals, and avoiding small objects mostly make the code worse without making it faster — and idiomatic code is what the JIT was tuned against.

You can make it slower. Introducing a second implementation of an interface on a hot path turns a monomorphic call site polymorphic and defeats inlining — a real, measurable regression from a change that looks like good design.

The costs are real: less readable code, more bugs, and time not spent on the architectural change that would have won 10×.

When it is justified: the profile shows this code is a dominant term; the algorithm is already right; you have a measured target; and you can prove the improvement with JMH rather than asserting it. That combination is rare and usually confined to a library's inner loop or the innermost 1% of a hot path.

The order I actually work in: is the algorithm right → can the work be avoided entirely (cache, batch, laziness) → are we waiting on something (I/O, locks, GC) → and only then, is the code slow. Most people start at step four, which is the smallest term in nearly every real system.

🏋️ Graded Exercises

1 (easy): A method is 4% of runtime. You make it 10× faster. What is the overall improvement?
Solution 
speedup = 1 / ((1 - 0.04) + 0.04/10)
        = 1 / (0.96 + 0.004)
        = 1.037                    → about 3.7%

And the ceiling if you made it infinitely fast is 4.2% (1 / 0.96). Ten times faster captured almost all of a payoff that was tiny to begin with.

Fraction of runtimeMax possible speedup
4%1.04×
20%1.25×
50%2×
80%5×
95%20×

The lesson is where to look, not how hard to try. Effort spent on a 4% term is capped before you start, no matter how clever the optimisation.

It also explains why architectural changes dominate. Removing an N+1 query or adding a cache targets a term that is often 80% of the time — and changes it by an order of magnitude rather than a factor.

2 (easy): Three problems in this loop. 
for (Order o : orders) {
    log.debug("processing order " + o);
    if (o.getRef().matches("^ORD-\\d{6}$")) {
        summary += o.getId() + ",";
    }
}
Solution
ProblemCost
String concatenation in the log callBuilds a string and calls toString() on every iteration even when debug is off
String.matchesCompiles the regex on every call — compilation is far more expensive than matching
summary +=O(n²) — a new String and a full copy each iteration
private static final Pattern REF = Pattern.compile("^ORD-\\d{6}$");

var summary = new StringBuilder();
for (Order o : orders) {
    log.debug("processing order {}", o);        // lazy — no work if off
    if (REF.matcher(o.getRef()).matches()) {    // compiled once
        summary.append(o.getId()).append(',');  // O(n)
    }
}

The logging one is the most common in real code and the most surprising, because the line looks harmless. The concatenation happens before debug() is called, so the log level cannot prevent it — including whatever Order.toString() does, which may itself be expensive.

String.matches, split and replaceAll all hide the same compilation, which is why they show up in profiles as time in Pattern.compile rather than in your code.

3 (medium): A service is slow. A CPU profile shows nothing above 3%. What is happening and what do you do?
Solution

The service is waiting, not computing. A CPU profile only samples threads that are running, so time spent blocked on I/O, locks or GC is invisible to it.

ModeAnswers
CPUWhat is burning cores — nothing here
Wall clockWhere real time goes, including waiting
LockWhich monitor is blocking, and for how long in total
AllocationWhat is creating GC pressure
# async-profiler, wall-clock mode, all threads
./profiler.sh -d 60 -e wall -t -f wall.html <pid>

Then confirm with a thread dump. Three dumps a few seconds apart separate the causes quickly: many BLOCKED on one monitor is lock contention; many RUNNABLE in socket reads is a slow downstream (RUNNABLE covers blocking I/O); threads waiting for a connection is pool exhaustion.

Also rule out GC with -Xlog:gc* — long pauses show up as slowness with no CPU attributable to your code.

The general lesson: "the profiler shows nothing" almost always means you asked the wrong question. In most services the dominant term is waiting, and only wall-clock profiling shows it.

4 (medium): Each downstream service reports a p99 of 100ms. Your endpoint calls 8 of them in parallel and waits for all. What is your latency, roughly?
Solution

Your p90 is roughly their p99 — tail amplification. Waiting for all means you inherit the slowest of the eight.

P(no call hits its p99) = 0.99^8 = 0.923
→ about 7.7% of your requests contain at least one 100 ms call
Fan-outRequests hitting ≥1 p99 subcall

8~8%

This is why every dependency's dashboard can look healthy while the composed request does not. Nobody is violating their SLO; the arithmetic of fan-out is doing it.

What actually helps:

ApproachEffect
Reduce fan-outBatch or denormalise — the largest lever
Hedged requestsSend a duplicate after p95 elapses, take the first response
Per-call timeouts below your budgetBounds the worst case; needs a degraded response
Return partial resultsDo not let one optional call gate the page

Hedging is the underused one — a second request costs a little capacity and converts "wait for the tail" into "wait for the faster of two", which is disproportionately effective when tail latency is caused by transient conditions like GC.

5 (hard): After a deploy, p99 doubled. CPU, memory and request rate are unchanged. Investigate.
Solution

Unchanged resources with worse latency means the extra time is spent waiting, or the work per request changed shape rather than volume.

HypothesisCheck
An N+1 query introduced by a lazy associationQuery counts per request; database round trips
A new synchronous call added to the pathDistributed traces before vs after
A cache stopped being hit (key change, TTL change)Cache hit-rate metric
New lock contentionLock profile; thread dumps under load
Higher allocation → more frequent GCGC log frequency, not pause length
A hot call site became polymorphicA second implementation added to an interface on the hot path
A dependency's own latency changedTheir metrics — the deploy may be a coincidence

The fastest single tool is a differential flame graph: profile the old and new versions and diff them. It answers "what is new" directly, rather than making you read two profiles and compare mentally.

Query count is the highest-yield first check in an ORM-backed service. Adding a field to a response or changing a fetch type silently converts one query into 101, and it costs latency without costing CPU or memory — exactly the signature described.

The polymorphic-call-site row is worth knowing because it is invisible in review: a refactor that adds a second implementation of an interface defeats the JIT's speculative inlining, and a well-intentioned design change becomes a measurable regression.

If nothing is found within a bounded time, roll back and bisect. With a reproducible signal, bisecting commits against a load test is slow but certain — and usually faster than theorising.

    final int size = orders.size();          // cached for speed
    StringBuilder sb = new StringBuilder(size * 64);
    for (int i = 0; i < size; i++) {
        Order o = orders.get(i);             // avoid iterator alloc
        final long id = o.getId();           // avoid repeated calls
        final String n = o.getName();

Solution

One of these optimisations is real; the rest are noise the JIT already handles.

"Optimisation"Verdict
Pre-sized StringBuilderGenuinely useful — avoids repeated array copies on growth
Caching size()No effect — inlined and hoisted already
Index loop to "avoid iterator allocation"No effect — escape analysis scalar-replaces the iterator
Caching getId() / getName() in localsNo effect — getters inline to field reads
final on localsNo effect on generated code
n.length() > 0Works; !n.isEmpty() reads better
public String process(List<Order> orders) {
    var sb = new StringBuilder(orders.size() * 64);   // keep this
    for (Order o : orders) {
        String name = o.getName();
        if (name != null && !name.isEmpty()) {
            sb.append(o.getId()).append(':').append(name).append('\n');
        }
    }
    return sb.toString();
}

The index loop is arguably a regression. It is correct for an ArrayList and O(n²) for a LinkedList, because get(i) walks the list — so the "optimisation" introduces a dependency on the caller's implementation choice.

The real question is upstream. Building a large string in memory and returning it caps the size you can handle; streaming to the consumer avoids holding it all at once. That is an architectural improvement worth far more than any of the loop tweaks.

The general rule this illustrates: the JIT optimises idiomatic code best. Hand-tuning that fights it costs readability and buys nothing — the only change here that helps is the one the JIT cannot make, because it needs knowledge of the expected output size.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Measure first — intuition about where time goes is reliably wrong.
  • Amdahl's law caps your payoff: a 5% term yields at most a 5% win even if made infinitely fast.
  • Algorithms and architecture beat micro-optimisation by orders of magnitude.
  • Averages describe values nobody experienced — report p50/p95/p99/max.
  • Tail amplification means your p90 is your dependencies' p99 at a fan-out of ten.
  • Little's Law (L = λW) sizes pools and predicts queue depth, and shows that reducing latency reduces required concurrency.
  • Coordinated omission makes closed-loop load tests report optimistic p99s by an order of magnitude.
  • Safepoint bias makes most profilers blame the wrong methods — inlined code and counted loops are invisible.
  • Use async-profiler or JFR; never profile with a debugger attached or before warmup.
  • Pick the profiling mode that matches the question — wall-clock is often the only one that shows a waiting service.
  • In a flame graph, width is everything, and wide plateaus at the top are the hot spots.
  • The recurring findings are N+1 queries, eager log concatenation, per-call regex compilation, string concatenation in loops, boxing and unbounded caches — not inefficient algorithms.
  • Exceptions on a common path cost microseconds each because of stack capture.
  • Inlining, escape analysis and lock elision already did your micro-optimisation — and idiomatic code is what the JIT optimises best.
  • Adding a second implementation to a hot interface can measurably slow it by making the call site polymorphic.
  • Mechanical sympathy — cache lines, layout, branch prediction — matters in the innermost 1%, and belongs last.
