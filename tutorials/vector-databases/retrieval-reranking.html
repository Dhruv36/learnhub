Retrieval Pipelines & Reranking RAG in Production

What you'll master: why retrieval is a funnel rather than a single search, and how to size each stage; the bi-encoder/cross-encoder distinction that explains why reranking works at all and why it cannot replace retrieval; how to choose a reranker and what it actually costs in latency and money; the query-side transformations — rewriting, decomposition, HyDE — and the honest evidence on which earn their latency; how context assembly determines whether the LLM uses what you retrieved, including the lost-in-the-middle effect; and how to build the whole pipeline so each stage is independently measurable. Prerequisites: Metadata Filtering & Hybrid Search, Chunking & Ingestion.

Part 1 — Retrieval Is a Funnel

A production retrieval pipeline is not one search. It is a sequence of stages, each cheaper-and-broader than the next, narrowing thousands of candidates to the handful the LLM actually reads.

  2,000,000 chunks
        │  ANN search + filters          ~5 ms      cheap, approximate
        ▼
      100 candidates
        │  fusion (RRF)                  ~0 ms      combines dense + lexical
        ▼
       50 candidates
        │  cross-encoder rerank         ~80 ms      expensive, accurate
        ▼
       10 candidates
        │  assembly: dedupe, order, budget
        ▼
        5 chunks → LLM

The structure exists because accuracy and cost move together. The only model good enough to rank the final five is far too slow to run over two million; the only method fast enough for two million is too crude to pick the final five. So each stage buys precision with the budget the previous stage saved.

StageOptimisesCost per itemFailure if under-sized
ANN retrievalRecall~microsecondsThe answer never enters the funnel — unrecoverable
FusionCoverage across retrieversFreeLexical-only or dense-only failures persist
RerankingPrecision~1–2 msGood chunks sit below the cutoff
AssemblyUsable contextFreeThe LLM ignores what you retrieved
🔑 Recall early, precision late — and the asymmetry is absolute. A chunk missed at the ANN stage cannot be recovered by any downstream stage, however good. A chunk retrieved but badly ranked can be rescued by the reranker. That is why stage one should be generous to the point of feeling wasteful: retrieve 100 when you need 5. The cost of over-retrieving is milliseconds; the cost of under-retrieving is a wrong answer.

The usual mistake is a pipeline with one stage — retrieve 5, send 5. It is fast and it fails silently: the answer sits at position 7 and nothing in the system ever looks there.

Part 2 — Why Reranking Works: Bi-Encoders vs Cross-Encoders

Everything about reranking follows from one architectural difference, and it is worth understanding precisely rather than as a slogan.

BI-ENCODER (retrieval)                    CROSS-ENCODER (reranking)
──────────────────────                    ─────────────────────────
query  → [encoder] → vector ─┐            [query + document] → [encoder] → score
                             ├─ cosine
doc    → [encoder] → vector ─┘            the model sees BOTH TOGETHER, with
                                          full attention between their tokens
docs encoded ONCE, at ingestion
query encoded once, at search time        every (query, doc) pair needs a
→ 2M docs, one comparison each            forward pass → 2M forward passes

The bi-encoder's document vector is computed without knowing the query. It must be a summary good enough for every possible question — which is exactly the compression that dilution exploits. The cross-encoder never has that problem: it reads the query and the document together, so attention can align "retry limit" in the query with "5 attempts" in the document.

query: "can I use my key after rotating it?"

                                        bi-encoder    cross-encoder
doc A: "Rotating a key invalidates the
        previous key after one hour."      0.61          0.94      ← the answer
doc B: "API keys are rotated from the
        dashboard security page."          0.72          0.31      ← about rotation, not this

The bi-encoder ranks B above A because B is topically closer to the query's vocabulary. The cross-encoder sees that A actually answers the question. That inversion — topical similarity versus answering the question — is the entire value of reranking.

Bi-encoderCross-encoder
Documents encodedOnce, at ingestionEvery query, every candidate
Can pre-indexYesNo — this is the whole constraint
Query–document interactionNone until the dot productFull attention across both
ThroughputMillions of comparisons/second~50–500 pairs/second
JobRecall over the corpusPrecision over a shortlist

So a cross-encoder cannot replace retrieval — scoring 2M pairs at 500/second is 66 minutes per query. It can only ever refine what retrieval already found, which is why the funnel is structurally necessary rather than merely convenient.

Late-interaction models (ColBERT-style) sit between the two: they store one vector per token rather than per document and compute a MaxSim between token sets at query time. That recovers much of the cross-encoder's interaction while remaining indexable — at the cost of 10–100× the storage, since a 400-token chunk becomes 400 vectors. Worth knowing; rarely the first thing to reach for.

Part 3 — Reranking in Practice

from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder

reranker = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2", max_length=512)

def rerank(query, candidates, top_k=5):
    pairs  = [(query, c.content) for c in candidates]
    scores = reranker.predict(pairs)                    # ONE batched call
    ranked = sorted(zip(candidates, scores), key=lambda x: -x[1])
    return [c for c, s in ranked[:top_k]]

The candidate count is the parameter that matters, and it has a knee like every other recall/cost curve in this track:

Retrieve N, rerank to top-5, measure whether the answer is in the final 5:

  N candidates   accuracy@5   rerank latency   total
  ────────────   ──────────   ──────────────   ─────
       10          0.71            18 ms        24 ms
       25          0.83            41 ms        48 ms
       50          0.89            78 ms        86 ms      ← the knee
      100          0.91           152 ms       162 ms
      200          0.915          301 ms       313 ms

Above ~50 the reranker is mostly re-sorting chunks that were never going to win. The ceiling is set by retrieval recall@N: if the answer is not in the 50, the reranker cannot conjure it — which is the funnel's asymmetry restated in numbers.

RerankerLatency (50 pairs)QualityUse when
MiniLM cross-encoder (L-6)~60–90 ms CPUGoodThe default — self-hosted, cheap
Larger cross-encoder (L-12, BGE)~150–300 ms CPUBetterQuality matters more than 100 ms
Hosted reranker API~100–200 ms + networkBest-in-classNo GPU, and per-query cost is acceptable
LLM-as-reranker~500–2000 msExcellent, expensiveSmall candidate sets, high-stakes queries
Late interaction (ColBERT)~10–20 msBetween the twoLatency-critical, storage available

A GPU changes the calculus entirely — the same MiniLM reranker does 50 pairs in ~8 ms on a modest GPU versus ~80 ms on CPU. If reranking is on your critical path and you already have GPU capacity for embeddings, use it.

Two implementation details cost more than people expect. First, truncation: a cross-encoder with max_length=512 silently truncates the query-plus-document pair, so a 600-token chunk loses its tail — and if the answer was in the tail, the reranker scores the chunk as irrelevant. Keep chunks comfortably under the limit, or the reranker's judgement is made on partial text.

# check what fraction of pairs is being truncated — this should be ~0
tok = reranker.tokenizer
over = sum(len(tok(q, c.content)["input_ids"]) > 512 for q, c in pairs)
print(f"{over}/{len(pairs)} pairs truncated")

Second, score calibration. Cross-encoder scores are logits, not probabilities, and their scale differs per model. A threshold of score > 0.5 copied from a different model is meaningless:

ms-marco-MiniLM raw scores:  relevant ≈  +6 to +11
                             irrelevant ≈ −8 to −2

# so a "relevance threshold" must be calibrated on YOUR data
scores = reranker.predict(pairs)
probs  = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-scores))          # sigmoid, if you want [0,1]

Thresholding is genuinely useful, though, and under-used: if nothing clears the bar, the honest response is to return no context and let the model say it does not know, rather than feeding it five irrelevant chunks and inviting a confident fabrication.

Part 4 — Query-Side Transformations

Everything so far improves how documents are ranked. The other half of the problem is that the user's query is often a poor search key.

TechniqueWhat it fixesCostVerdict
Conversational rewritingFollow-ups: "and the second one?"1 LLM callEssential for chat
DecompositionMulti-part questions1 call + N retrievalsWorth it when queries are compound
Multi-query expansionVocabulary mismatch1 call + N retrievalsModest gain; hybrid search overlaps it
HyDEQuestion/answer asymmetry1 LLM callGood for jargon-heavy corpora; can hallucinate off-target
Step-back promptingOverly specific queries1 callNiche

Conversational rewriting is the one that is not optional, because in a chat interface the raw query is frequently meaningless in isolation:

User: "How do webhook retries work?"
Bot:  "...retried 5 times with exponential backoff..."
User: "And how do I turn that off?"

embedding of "And how do I turn that off?" → matches nothing useful

rewritten: "How do I disable webhook delivery retries?"

REWRITE = """Rewrite the user's latest message as a standalone search query.
Resolve all pronouns and references using the conversation. Output only the query.

Conversation:
{history}

Latest: {question}
Standalone query:"""

def rewrite(history, question):
    if not history:
        return question                     # skip the call on the first turn
    return llm(REWRITE.format(history=format_recent(history, n=4),
                              question=question)).strip()

Decomposition handles the compound query, which single-vector retrieval handles badly because it averages two questions into one point in space:

"What are the rate limits and how do I request an increase?"
        │
        ├──▶ "What are the API rate limits?"
        └──▶ "How do I request a rate limit increase?"
                    │
              retrieve each, merge, dedupe

HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) addresses a real asymmetry: questions and answers are written differently, so a question's embedding may sit far from the answer's. HyDE asks the LLM to write a fake answer and embeds that:

query:      "how long does an old API key work after rotation?"
hypothetical: "After rotating an API key, the previous key remains valid for a
               grace period of one hour before being permanently revoked."
              → embed this instead; it looks like the document you want

The honest assessment: HyDE helps most where the corpus uses vocabulary the user does not (medical, legal, internal jargon), typically 3–8 points of recall there. It helps little on well-matched corpora, adds a full LLM call of latency, and it can hallucinate a plausible answer in the wrong domain and drag retrieval with it. Measure before adopting; hybrid search often solves the same problem for less.

❌ Stacking every technique because each one sounds like an improvement. Rewrite, expand to 5 queries, HyDE each, retrieve, fuse, rerank twice. The result is 6 LLM calls before retrieval, 4 seconds of latency, and a system where nobody can say which stage helps — so nothing can be removed and every regression is a mystery.

✅ Add one stage at a time and measure it against the stage below. Baseline, then +hybrid, then +rerank, then +rewrite, each with its own recall and latency number. Most pipelines find that hybrid retrieval plus a cross-encoder reranker captures nearly all the available gain, and the remaining techniques buy single digits for hundreds of milliseconds. Keep what pays.

Part 5 — Context Assembly

The last stage is the one teams skip, and it decides whether the LLM uses what you worked to retrieve.

Position matters more than most people assume. Models attend more reliably to the beginning and end of a long context than to its middle — the "lost in the middle" effect:

answer placed at position...   accuracy (10 chunks in context)
  1  (first)                        0.76
  3                                 0.61
  5  (middle)                       0.53      ← the trough
  8                                 0.64
 10  (last)                         0.71

So order by relevance outward from the ends, rather than simply descending:

def assemble(chunks):
    """Best chunk first, second-best last, rest toward the middle."""
    front, back = [], []
    for i, c in enumerate(chunks):          # chunks are ranked best-first
        (front if i % 2 == 0 else back).append(c)
    return front + back[::-1]

ranked:    [A, B, C, D, E]      (A best)
assembled: [A, C, E, D, B]      A first, B last — the two strongest at the edges

Deduplicate before assembling. With overlap, small-to-big, or multi-query retrieval, the same passage arrives several times and quietly consumes the budget:

def dedupe(chunks, threshold=0.92):
    kept = []
    for c in chunks:                        # ranked best-first
        if all(cosine(c.vector, k.vector) < threshold for k in kept):
            kept.append(c)
    return kept

Budget by tokens, not by chunk count. "Top 5 chunks" is 600 tokens or 6,000 depending on what chunking produced — and the second case silently truncates whatever the model's limit is:

def fit_budget(chunks, max_tokens=3000):
    out, used = [], 0
    for c in chunks:
        n = count_tokens(c.content)
        if used + n > max_tokens:
            break
        out.append(c); used += n
    return out

Include the metadata the answer needs to be verifiable — source, section, date — because a citation the user can check is most of what makes RAG trustworthy:

def format_context(chunks):
    return "\n\n".join(
        f"[{i}] {c.source} > {c.heading} ({c.updated_at:%Y-%m-%d})\n{c.content}"
        for i, c in enumerate(chunks, 1))

# and instruct the model to cite: "Cite the [n] of each source you use.
# If the context does not contain the answer, say so."

The date is load-bearing in a corpus with versioned content. Without it the model has no way to prefer the current document over the two-year-old one that says the opposite, and it will happily average them.

Part 6 — Building and Measuring the Pipeline

The whole thing, with each stage independently switchable — which is what makes it measurable:

async def retrieve(query, history=None, filters=None, k=5, config=CONFIG):
    if config.rewrite and history:
        query = await rewrite(history, query)                    # ~200 ms

    vec = await embed(query)                                     # ~15 ms
    dense, lexical = await asyncio.gather(                       # ~6 ms, concurrent
        store.vector_search(vec, filter=filters, limit=config.candidates),
        store.text_search(query, filter=filters, limit=config.candidates),
    )

    fused = rrf([ids(dense), ids(lexical)])[:config.candidates]  # free
    docs  = load(fused)

    if config.rerank:
        docs = reranker_rank(query, docs, top_k=k * 2)           # ~80 ms
        docs = [d for d in docs if d.score > config.min_score]   # may return []

    docs = dedupe(docs)
    docs = fit_budget(docs, config.max_context_tokens)
    return assemble(docs[:k])

Measure each stage against the one below it, on the same judged query set, or you cannot justify any of it:

                                recall@5   nDCG@5   p50 latency   answer quality
  dense only                      0.62      0.58        21 ms         3.4
  + hybrid (RRF)                  0.79      0.71        24 ms         3.9
  + cross-encoder rerank          0.79      0.86       104 ms         4.3
  + query rewriting (chat)        0.86      0.91       310 ms         4.5
  + HyDE                          0.87      0.91       690 ms         4.5   ← drop it
  + multi-query expansion         0.88      0.92       980 ms         4.5   ← drop it

Read the two columns separately — they say different things. Reranking left recall unchanged at 0.79 and moved nDCG from 0.71 to 0.86: it did not find anything new, it reordered what was already there. That is exactly what a reranker is supposed to do, and a team looking only at recall would have concluded it did nothing.

And the last two rows cost 670 ms for +0.01 nDCG and no answer-quality change. That is a clear drop — but you only know it because each stage was measured separately.

SymptomLikely stageFix
Answer not in the candidates at allRetrieval / chunkingMore candidates, hybrid, better chunking
In the candidates, not in the final kRerankingBetter reranker; check truncation
In the final context, ignored by the LLMAssemblyReorder, dedupe, cut context size
Correct chunks, wrong answerGenerationPrompt, citations, model
Follow-up questions failQuery rewritingAdd conversational rewriting

That table is the debugging discipline in one place: find the stage, then fix the stage. The measurement that makes it possible is the subject of the next lesson.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is reranking and why would you add it after retrieval?

Reranking is a second, more accurate scoring pass over a shortlist that retrieval already produced. You retrieve 50 candidates cheaply, score all 50 with an expensive model, and keep the best 5.

The reason it helps is that retrieval's scoring is deliberately crude. A vector search compares two independently-computed embeddings — the document's vector was created at ingestion, long before your query existed, so it has to be a summary that works for every possible question.

query: "can I use my key after rotating it?"
                                             retrieval   reranker
"Rotating a key invalidates the previous
 key after one hour."                          0.61        0.94    ← the answer
"API keys are rotated from the dashboard."      0.72        0.31    ← just topical

Retrieval ranked the wrong one first because it is topically closer. The reranker reads the query and the document together and can tell that only one of them answers the question.

The trade is cost. Retrieval scores millions of documents in milliseconds; a reranker scores maybe 50–500 per second. That is precisely why it goes second — it is only affordable over a shortlist.

And it cannot rescue bad retrieval. If the answer is not among the 50 candidates, no reranker will find it. Recall is retrieval's job; ranking is the reranker's.

Beginner: What is the difference between a bi-encoder and a cross-encoder?

It is where the query and the document meet.

BI-ENCODER:     query → vector ┐
                                ├→ cosine similarity
                doc   → vector ┘        (they meet only at the dot product)

CROSS-ENCODER:  [query + doc] → model → score
                                         (they meet inside the model, with
                                          attention across all their tokens)
Bi-encoderCross-encoder
Documents encodedOnce, at ingestionPer query, per candidate
Pre-indexableYesNo
SpeedMillions/second~50–500 pairs/second
AccuracyGoodBetter

That "pre-indexable" row is the whole story. Because a bi-encoder's document vector does not depend on the query, you can compute all of them in advance and build an ANN index. A cross-encoder's score does not exist until the query arrives, so there is nothing to index — every score requires a forward pass at query time.

Which is why you need both. A cross-encoder over 2M documents at 500 pairs/second is over an hour per query. A bi-encoder narrows 2M to 50 in milliseconds; the cross-encoder then picks the best 5 out of 50 in under 100 ms. Neither architecture can do the other's job.

There is a middle option worth naming: late-interaction models like ColBERT store one vector per token and compare token sets at query time. That keeps some query–document interaction while staying indexable, at 10–100× the storage.

Mid: How many candidates should you retrieve before reranking, and how would you decide?

By measuring, because it is a knee-shaped curve and the knee depends on the corpus. The shape is always the same though:

retrieve N → rerank → top-5, accuracy that the answer is in the final 5:

   N=10    0.71     18 ms
   N=25    0.83     41 ms
   N=50    0.89     78 ms      ← knee
   N=100   0.91    152 ms
   N=200   0.915   301 ms

Two forces set the knee. Below it, retrieval recall@N is the binding constraint — the answer simply is not in the candidate set, and the reranker cannot invent it. Above it, recall@N is already near its ceiling and the extra candidates are documents that were never going to win, so you pay linear latency for nothing.

The diagnostic that tells you which side you are on is to measure retrieval recall@N separately from post-rerank accuracy:

N=50:  retrieval recall@50 = 0.93,  post-rerank accuracy@5 = 0.89
       → the reranker is capturing most of what retrieval found;
         to improve, fix RETRIEVAL (chunking, hybrid, embeddings)

N=50:  retrieval recall@50 = 0.97,  post-rerank accuracy@5 = 0.74
       → retrieval is fine; the RERANKER is the bottleneck

Practical starting point: 10–20× your final k, so 50–100 candidates for a top-5. Then sweep. Latency scales linearly with N, so this is also the first dial to turn when you need to cut p99.

Two adjustments worth making. Batch the pairs into a single reranker call rather than looping — the difference is large. And if latency is critical, consider an adaptive N: rerank 25 by default, and escalate to 100 only when the top scores are low or close together, which is a cheap signal that the shortlist is weak.

Mid: What is query rewriting and which query transformations are actually worth their cost?

Query transformations change the query before retrieval, because the user's words are often a poor search key. They vary enormously in value.

Conversational rewriting is the one that is genuinely required in any chat interface:

User: "How do webhook retries work?"
Bot:  "...retried 5 times..."
User: "And how do I turn that off?"     ← embeds to nothing useful

rewritten → "How do I disable webhook delivery retries?"

Without it, every follow-up turn retrieves noise. This is not an optimisation; it is the difference between a chat RAG system working and not.

TechniqueFixesCostVerdict
Conversational rewriteFollow-ups, pronouns1 LLM callEssential for chat
DecompositionCompound questions1 call + N retrievalsWorth it if queries are compound
HyDEQuestion/answer asymmetry1 callJargon corpora only; measure it
Multi-query expansionVocabulary mismatch1 call + N retrievalsOverlaps hybrid search; usually redundant

Decomposition matters because a compound query averages badly. "What are the rate limits and how do I request an increase?" is two questions; one vector for both sits between them and may retrieve neither well. Splitting and retrieving separately fixes it.

HyDE is the one to be sceptical about. The idea is sound — questions and answers are phrased differently, so embed a hypothetical answer instead — and it delivers 3–8 points on corpora whose vocabulary the user does not share (medical, legal, internal). On ordinary documentation it buys little, adds a full LLM call, and can hallucinate a plausible answer in the wrong domain that drags retrieval with it.

My rule is that each transformation must justify its latency against the stage below it. Most pipelines find hybrid retrieval plus a reranker captures nearly everything, and conversational rewriting is the only query-side addition that consistently pays.

Senior: Design the retrieval pipeline for a customer-support RAG assistant with a 2-second latency budget.

I would design it as a funnel with an explicit latency budget per stage, because with 2 seconds total and generation taking most of it, retrieval has roughly 400 ms.

StageBudgetNotes
Query rewrite (chat only)150 msSmall fast model; skipped on turn 1
Embed query20 msCached for repeats
Hybrid retrieval, 50 candidates30 msDense + BM25, concurrent
Rerank 50 → 1080 msMiniLM cross-encoder, GPU if available
Assembly5 msDedupe, order, token budget
Retrieval total~285 msLeaves ~1.7 s for generation

1. Hybrid retrieval is non-negotiable for support, because support queries are full of error codes, product versions and ticket ids — exactly where dense retrieval fails and BM25 excels. Both branches run concurrently and take the same filters (tenant, product, locale).

2. Rerank 50 → 10, then threshold. The threshold is the part I would insist on: if nothing clears it, return no context and let the assistant say it does not know rather than confabulating from five irrelevant chunks. In support, a confident wrong answer is worse than an escalation.

3. Rewrite only when there is history, and with a small model. Skipping it on the first turn saves 150 ms on the most common request shape.

4. Assembly details that matter here: dedupe (the corpus has near-identical articles per product version), order best-first/second-last to avoid the attention trough, budget by tokens, and include updated_at and product version in each chunk header — support corpora are full of superseded articles, and without dates the model averages the old and new answers.

5. Caching does most of the latency work in support, because the query distribution is extremely head-heavy: cache query embeddings, and cache the whole retrieval result keyed by (rewritten query, filters) with a short TTL. A 40% hit rate on common questions removes the entire funnel for those.

6. Degradation path under load, decided in advance: drop the reranker first (accuracy falls, the system stays up), then drop rewriting. Never drop retrieval itself — answering with no context is better than answering from the wrong context.

What I would leave out initially: HyDE and multi-query expansion. Each is an extra LLM call inside a 400 ms budget, and support queries are mostly short and specific — the shape that hybrid retrieval already handles well. I would add them only if the per-stage measurement showed a gap they address.

Senior: Your RAG system returns the right documents but the answers are still wrong. How do you find where it is failing?

"The right documents" is usually an assumption rather than a measurement, so I would first make the pipeline observable at every stage boundary and then attribute failures to a stage.

1. Log the full trace per query, so any failure can be replayed:

{"query": ..., "rewritten": ...,
 "retrieved_ids": [...50...], "retrieval_scores": [...],
 "reranked_ids": [...10...],  "rerank_scores": [...],
 "context_ids": [...5...],    "context_tokens": 2840,
 "answer": ..., "cited_ids": [...]}

2. Take 100 failures and locate each one at the first stage where the needed chunk disappeared:

  not in retrieved_50           38   → retrieval / chunking
  in retrieved, not reranked_10 21   → reranker
  in context, not cited          9   → assembly or prompt
  in context, cited, wrong        32   → generation

That distribution is the entire diagnosis, and it usually contradicts the team's assumption. Here the premise "we retrieve the right documents" holds for only 41% of failures.

3. For the generation bucket — the interesting one — check the specific causes:

CauseSignalFix
Lost in the middleCited chunks skew to positions 1 and lastReorder; cut context size
Contradictory chunksTwo versions of the same article in contextDedupe; filter by updated_at
Context too longFailures correlate with token countFewer, better chunks
No instruction to abstainConfident answers with weak contextPrompt + score threshold
Fragmented chunksCited chunk ends mid-sentenceFix chunking; small-to-big

4. Run the controlled experiment that isolates generation. Feed the model the gold-standard chunks directly, bypassing retrieval entirely:

gold context → correct answer 94%   → generation is fine; retrieval is the problem
gold context → correct answer 61%   → the prompt or model is the problem

That single test settles the argument faster than any amount of analysis, and it is cheap.

5. Check the two assembly bugs that produce exactly this symptom. First, contradictory context: a support corpus with per-version articles will happily supply both the v1 and v2 answer, and the model splits the difference — the fix is deduplication plus recency filtering, not a better model. Second, position: if cited chunks cluster at positions 1 and 5 of 5, the middle is being ignored and the context is too long.

6. Then fix the stage the data pointed at — and re-run the same 100 failures to confirm the distribution moved. The discipline that matters is attributing before changing; a team that tunes the prompt because generation "feels" wrong will usually be tuning the 32% while the 38% retrieval bucket goes untouched.

3 (medium): Sweep the candidate count against end-to-end accuracy.
Solution

Build a corpus with distractors so retrieval genuinely has to work, then measure the two things separately: whether retrieval found the answer, and whether reranking kept it.

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, CrossEncoder
import numpy as np, time

bi    = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
cross = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2")

answers = {
  "how many times is a webhook retried":
     "Webhook delivery is retried up to 5 times with exponential backoff.",
  "how long is an old API key valid":
     "The previous API key remains valid for one hour after rotation.",
  "what does exceeding the rate limit return":
     "Exceeding the rate limit returns HTTP 429 with a Retry-After header.",
  "when are invoices generated":
     "Invoices are generated on the first day of each month.",
}
# 400 topical distractors — same vocabulary, no answers
distractors = [f"The {w} system is configured in the dashboard settings panel "
               f"under section {i}." for i, w in
               enumerate(["webhook","api key","rate limit","invoice","billing",
                          "retry","backoff","endpoint"] * 50)]
corpus = list(answers.values()) + distractors
E = bi.encode(corpus, normalize_embeddings=True)

def run(n_candidates, final_k=3):
    ret_hits = rr_hits = 0
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    for q, gold in answers.items():
        qv = bi.encode([q], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
        cand = np.argsort(-(E @ qv))[:n_candidates]
        ret_hits += corpus.index(gold) in cand                    # retrieval recall@N

        scores = cross.predict([(q, corpus[i]) for i in cand])
        top = [cand[i] for i in np.argsort(-scores)[:final_k]]
        rr_hits += corpus.index(gold) in top                      # accuracy@k after rerank
    ms = (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000 / len(answers)
    return ret_hits/len(answers), rr_hits/len(answers), ms

print(f"{'N':>4}  {'recall@N':>9}  {'acc@3':>7}  {'ms/query':>9}")
for n in (3, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200):
    r, a, ms = run(n)
    print(f"{n:4d}  {r:9.2f}  {a:7.2f}  {ms:9.1f}")

   N   recall@N    acc@3   ms/query
   3       0.50     0.50       12.1
  10       0.75     0.75       28.9
  25       1.00     1.00       58.4      ← retrieval ceiling reached
  50       1.00     1.00      106.2
 100       1.00     1.00      198.7
 200       1.00     1.00      384.1

Two regimes, clearly separated. Below N=25, acc@3 tracks recall@N exactly — the reranker is perfect at its job and retrieval is the binding constraint. Above N=25, recall is already 1.00 and extra candidates buy nothing but latency.

That comparison is the diagnostic worth remembering: when acc@k ≈ recall@N, improve retrieval; when acc@k << recall@N, improve the reranker.

REWRITE_PROMPT = """Rewrite the user's latest message as a standalone search query.
Resolve pronouns using the conversation. Output only the query.

Conversation:
{history}

Latest: {question}
Standalone query:"""

        return question                      # skip the call on turn 1
    if llm:

            history="\n".join(f"{r}: {t}" for r, t in history[-4:]),
            question=question)).strip()
    return "How do I disable webhook delivery retries?"    # stand-in

5 (hard): Build the full pipeline with per-stage measurement.
Solution 
from dataclasses import dataclass
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, CrossEncoder
import numpy as np, time

@dataclass
class Config:
    candidates: int = 50
    final_k: int = 5
    rerank: bool = True
    hybrid: bool = True
    rewrite: bool = False
    min_score: float = -2.0
    max_context_tokens: int = 1500

class Pipeline:
    def __init__(self, corpus, cfg=Config()):
        self.corpus, self.cfg = corpus, cfg
        self.bi    = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
        self.cross = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2")
        self.E     = self.bi.encode(corpus, normalize_embeddings=True)
        self.tokens = [d.lower().split() for d in corpus]

    def _dense(self, q, n):
        qv = self.bi.encode([q], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
        return list(np.argsort(-(self.E @ qv))[:n])

    def _lexical(self, q, n):                       # crude BM25 stand-in
        qt = set(q.lower().split())
        overlap = [len(qt & set(t)) for t in self.tokens]
        return [i for i in np.argsort(-np.array(overlap))[:n] if overlap[i] > 0]

    def _rrf(self, rankings, k=60):
        s = {}
        for r in rankings:
            for rank, i in enumerate(r, 1):
                s[i] = s.get(i, 0) + 1 / (k + rank)
        return sorted(s, key=s.get, reverse=True)

    def run(self, query, trace=None):
        t = {}; cfg = self.cfg
        t0 = time.perf_counter()

        rankings = [self._dense(query, cfg.candidates)]
        if cfg.hybrid:
            rankings.append(self._lexical(query, cfg.candidates))
        cand = self._rrf(rankings)[:cfg.candidates]
        t["retrieve"] = (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000

        if cfg.rerank and cand:
            t1 = time.perf_counter()
            sc = self.cross.predict([(query, self.corpus[i]) for i in cand])
            pairs = [(i, s) for i, s in zip(cand, sc) if s > cfg.min_score]
            cand = [i for i, _ in sorted(pairs, key=lambda x: -x[1])]
            t["rerank"] = (time.perf_counter() - t1) * 1000

        t2 = time.perf_counter()
        out = self._assemble(cand[:cfg.final_k])
        t["assemble"] = (time.perf_counter() - t2) * 1000
        t["total"] = (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000
        if trace is not None:
            trace.append({"query": query, "ids": out, "timings": t})
        return out

    def _assemble(self, ids):
        """Dedupe by similarity, budget by tokens, order best-first/second-last."""
        kept = []
        for i in ids:
            if all(float(self.E[i] @ self.E[j]) < 0.92 for j in kept):
                kept.append(i)
        used, budgeted = 0, []
        for i in kept:
            n = len(self.corpus[i].split())
            if used + n > self.cfg.max_context_tokens: break
            budgeted.append(i); used += n
        front, back = [], []
        for pos, i in enumerate(budgeted):
            (front if pos % 2 == 0 else back).append(i)
        return front + back[::-1]

6 (hard): Attribute 100 failures to their stage and act on the distribution.
Solution

The scenario: a RAG assistant with ~30% wrong answers. The team wants a better LLM. The deliverable is evidence about where the failures actually are.

1. Instrument every stage boundary so a failure can be located rather than guessed:

def traced_query(pipeline, query, gold_chunk_ids):
    tr = {"query": query, "gold": set(gold_chunk_ids)}
    tr["retrieved"] = pipeline.retrieve(query, n=50)
    tr["reranked"]  = pipeline.rerank(query, tr["retrieved"], k=10)
    tr["context"]   = pipeline.assemble(tr["reranked"], k=5)
    tr["answer"], tr["cited"] = pipeline.generate(query, tr["context"])
    return tr

def locate(tr):
    """First stage at which the gold chunk disappeared."""
    if not (tr["gold"] & set(tr["retrieved"])): return "retrieval"
    if not (tr["gold"] & set(tr["reranked"])):  return "reranking"
    if not (tr["gold"] & set(tr["context"])):   return "assembly"
    if not (tr["gold"] & set(tr["cited"])):     return "generation-ignored"
    return "generation-wrong"

from collections import Counter
traces = [traced_query(p, q, gold) for q, gold in failing_queries]
print(Counter(locate(t) for t in traces))

Counter({'retrieval': 38, 'generation-wrong': 32, 'reranking': 21,
         'generation-ignored': 9})

The premise was wrong. The team believed retrieval was fine; it accounts for 38 of 100 failures, and a better LLM addresses at most the 32.

2. Break down the retrieval bucket, because "retrieval" is not an actionable diagnosis:

for t in [t for t in traces if locate(t) == "retrieval"]:
    gold_text = corpus[min(t["gold"])]
    t["subcause"] = ("answer-split"  if is_fragment(gold_text)

31 of 38 retrieval failures are chunking problems, not embedding problems — the answer was split across two chunks, or the chunk lacked the context to be findable. That points at chunking and contextual headers, which is hours of work, not a model migration.

3. Isolate generation with the gold-context test:

              for t in traces)

91% correct when handed the right chunks. The model is not the problem — that single number ends the "upgrade the LLM" proposal.

             if c in tr["context"]]

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Retrieval is a funnel: cheap-and-broad narrows to expensive-and-precise, because accuracy and cost move together and no single method does both.
  • Recall early, precision late — a chunk missed by ANN search cannot be recovered downstream, so over-retrieve deliberately.
  • A bi-encoder embeds query and document independently, so documents can be pre-indexed; a cross-encoder scores them together and cannot be indexed at all.
  • That difference is why reranking works: the cross-encoder can tell "topically similar" from "answers the question", which a dot product of two independent vectors cannot.
  • A cross-encoder can never replace retrieval — scoring millions of pairs per query is hours of compute.
  • Retrieve 10–20× your final k and rerank down; the knee is usually 25–100 candidates, above which you pay linear latency for nothing.
  • Compare retrieval recall@N with post-rerank accuracy@k: close together means fix retrieval, far apart means fix the reranker.
  • Batch reranker calls — looping over pairs is ~7× slower for identical scores.
  • Watch cross-encoder truncation: a chunk longer than the model's limit is scored on partial text.
  • Cross-encoder scores are uncalibrated logits, so thresholds must be fitted on your own data — and a threshold that returns empty context is better than padding with irrelevant chunks.
  • Conversational rewriting is essential in chat: "and how do I turn that off?" retrieves nothing on its own.
  • Skip the rewrite call when there is no history, and keep the history window short so a topic shift is not merged away.
  • Decomposition helps compound queries because one vector for two questions sits between them; HyDE helps mainly on jargon-heavy corpora and costs a full LLM call.
  • Add one stage at a time and measure it against the stage below, or you end up with seconds of latency and no way to attribute any of it.
  • Context assembly decides whether the LLM uses what you retrieved: order best-first and second-best last to avoid the lost-in-the-middle trough.
  • Deduplicate before assembling, budget by tokens rather than chunk count, and include source and date so answers are checkable and stale docs do not outrank current ones.
  • Track recall and nDCG separately — reranking improves ranking without changing recall, and a recall-only view makes it look useless.
  • Attribute failures to a stage before fixing anything; the gold-context test (feed the model the right chunks) settles "is it retrieval or generation?" in one experiment.
