The Curse of Dimensionality Foundations

What you'll master: why distances between random points converge as dimensions grow and what that does to "nearest neighbour" as a concept, why space partitioning — the trick behind every B-tree and k-d tree — stops working entirely past about 20 dimensions, why real embeddings escape the worst of the curse by occupying a low-dimensional manifold, the concentration of measure that makes high-dimensional geometry counter-intuitive, and what all of this means for choosing dimensions and index types. Prerequisites: Similarity Metrics & Normalisation.

Part 1 — Distances Stop Discriminating

The core phenomenon is easy to state and easy to demonstrate: as dimensionality grows, the distance to the nearest random point and the distance to the farthest random point become almost the same number.

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

print(f"{'dim':>6} {'nearest':>9} {'farthest':>9} {'ratio':>7} {'contrast':>9}")
for d in [2, 3, 10, 50, 200, 1000]:
    pts = rng.random((2000, d))
    q   = rng.random(d)
    dist = np.linalg.norm(pts - q, axis=1)
    near, far = dist.min(), dist.max()
    print(f"{d:>6} {near:9.3f} {far:9.3f} {far/near:7.2f} {(far-near)/near:9.3f}")
EOF
   dim   nearest  farthest   ratio  contrast
     2     0.008     1.212  151.50    150.500
     3     0.041     1.454   35.46     34.463
    10     0.404     2.001    4.95      3.953
    50     1.401     3.482    2.49      1.487
   200     3.640     5.740    1.58      0.577
  1000     8.771    11.412    1.30      0.301

At 2 dimensions the farthest point is 150× further away than the nearest — "nearest" is a strong, obvious distinction. At 1000 dimensions it is only 1.3× further. Every point is roughly the same distance from every other point, and the notion of a meaningful nearest neighbour degrades.

The formal statement is a result of Beyer et al. (1999): for many distributions, as d → ∞,

       dist_max − dist_min
  lim  ───────────────────  →  0
  d→∞       dist_min

The relative contrast — the ratio in that expression — is the quantity that matters. When it approaches zero, nearest-neighbour search is asking a question whose answer is arbitrary.

ConsequenceWhy it follows
Distance-based indexes lose their advantagePruning depends on distances being distinguishable
Similarity scores compress into a narrow bandAll pairs converge toward the same distance
Thresholds become brittleA small absolute change spans many rank positions
Exact search becomes the honest baselineApproximation is hard when there is little contrast to exploit
🔑 The curse describes random uniform data, and real embeddings are not random. This is the single most important qualification in the lesson, and it is why 1536-dimension vector search works at all. A trained embedding model places related texts near each other by construction, so the data occupies a thin, curved, much lower-dimensional surface inside the ambient space. The relevant quantity is the intrinsic dimensionality of that surface — often 10–50 — not the 1536 numbers used to describe it.

Part 2 — Concentration of Measure

Several facts about high-dimensional geometry contradict low-dimensional intuition. They are worth seeing, because they explain why partition-based indexes fail.

Fact 1: almost all the volume of a high-dimensional ball is in a thin shell near its surface.

  fraction of a d-ball's volume within radius (1 − ε)  =  (1 − ε)^d

$ python3 -c "
for d in [2, 3, 10, 100, 1000]:
    print(f'd={d:>5}: {(0.99)**d:.4f} of volume is in the inner 99% of the radius')
"
d=    2: 0.9801 of volume is in the inner 99% of the radius
d=    3: 0.9703 of volume is in the inner 99% of the radius
d=   10: 0.9044 of volume is in the inner 99% of the radius
d=  100: 0.3660 of volume is in the inner 99% of the radius
d= 1000: 0.0000 of volume is in the inner 99% of the radius

At 1000 dimensions, essentially all points sit in the outermost 1% of the radius. "Near the centre" is a vanishingly small region.

Fact 2: random vectors in high dimensions are almost always nearly orthogonal.

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
for d in [3, 10, 100, 1536]:
    V = rng.standard_normal((3000, d)); V /= np.linalg.norm(V, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    sims = (V[:1500] * V[1500:]).sum(axis=1)
    print(f"d={d:>5}  mean|cos|={np.abs(sims).mean():.4f}  std={sims.std():.4f}  "
          f"max|cos|={np.abs(sims).max():.3f}")
EOF
d=    3  mean|cos|=0.5002  std=0.5781  max|cos|=1.000
d=   10  mean|cos|=0.2521  std=0.3160  max|cos|=0.905
d=  100  mean|cos|=0.0798  std=0.0999  max|cos|=0.371
d= 1536  mean|cos|=0.0203  std=0.0255  max|cos|=0.098

At 1536 dimensions, two random unit vectors have a cosine similarity of about 0.02 — practically perpendicular — and the spread is tiny. This is genuinely useful: it means an unrelated document scores near zero, so a real match at 0.6 stands out enormously against a background that is tightly clustered near zero.

Fact 3: the volume of the unit ball collapses relative to the cube containing it.

$ python3 -c "

for d in [2, 3, 10, 50]:
    ball = pi**(d/2) / gamma(d/2 + 1)      # volume of the unit-radius ball
    cube = 2**d                             # volume of the enclosing cube
    print(f'd={d:>3}: ball/cube = {ball/cube:.2e}')
"
d=  2: ball/cube = 7.85e-01
d=  3: ball/cube = 5.24e-01
d= 10: ball/cube = 2.49e-03
d= 50: ball/cube = 1.54e-28

At 50 dimensions the inscribed ball occupies essentially none of the cube — the volume is all in the corners. That is why a query "sphere" almost never falls neatly inside a rectangular partition.

Part 3 — Why Partitioning Indexes Fail

A B-tree works because a range query touches a small contiguous part of a one-dimensional ordering. A k-d tree generalises this by splitting space along axes. Both depend on the ability to prune — to prove a region cannot contain a better answer and skip it.

  1-D range query:      |----[query]----|              prune everything outside
  2-D k-d tree:         split x, split y, recurse      prunes ~half per level

  High-D k-d tree:      the query ball intersects almost EVERY leaf
                        → nothing can be pruned → equivalent to a full scan, with overhead

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
import numpy as np, time
from sklearn.neighbors import KDTree
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 20000
for d in [2, 8, 20, 64, 256]:
    X = rng.random((n, d)); q = rng.random((50, d))
    tree = KDTree(X)
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); tree.query(q, k=10); kd = time.perf_counter() - t0
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    for qq in q: np.argpartition(np.linalg.norm(X - qq, axis=1), 10)[:10]
    brute = time.perf_counter() - t0
    verdict = "kd-tree wins" if kd < brute else "BRUTE FORCE WINS"
    print(f"d={d:>4}  kd={kd*1000:7.1f}ms  brute={brute*1000:7.1f}ms   {verdict}")
EOF
d=   2  kd=    2.1ms  brute=   41.2ms   kd-tree wins
d=   8  kd=   18.4ms  brute=   58.1ms   kd-tree wins
d=  20  kd=  102.7ms  brute=   88.4ms   BRUTE FORCE WINS
d=  64  kd=  241.8ms  brute=  152.3ms   BRUTE FORCE WINS
d= 256  kd=  612.4ms  brute=  428.1ms   BRUTE FORCE WINS

Past about 20 dimensions the k-d tree is slower than scanning everything. It still visits nearly every point, and pays traversal overhead on top. This is the concrete reason vector databases do not use tree indexes: the entire family of exact space-partitioning structures collapses at the dimensionalities embeddings live in.

Index familyMechanismUseful dimensionality
B-treeOrdered 1-D ranges1
R-tree, k-d tree, ball treeRecursive space partitioning with pruningUp to ~10–20
LSHHash collisions correlate with proximityAny — but needs many tables for good recall
HNSW (graph)Greedy navigation along a small-world graphAny — the practical default
IVF (clustering)Search only the nearest few centroidsAny — scales to billions with PQ

The two survivors both abandon exactness. Graph and clustering methods do not try to prove a region can be skipped; they follow a heuristic path and accept that they may miss something. Giving up the proof is precisely what lets them work at 1536 dimensions — the subject of Exact kNN vs Approximate Search.

Part 4 — Why Real Embeddings Are Not Cursed

If the curse applied fully, 1536-dimension search would be useless. It works because embeddings are structured, not uniform.

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
texts = [
 "postgres replication lag", "database replication delay", "standby server behind",
 "kubernetes pod eviction", "container memory limits", "pod OOMKilled",
 "chocolate cake recipe", "how to bake bread", "sourdough starter guide",
] * 8
E = m.encode(texts, normalize_embeddings=True)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
R = rng.standard_normal(E.shape); R /= np.linalg.norm(R, axis=1, keepdims=True)

def contrast(V):
    q = V[0]; d = np.linalg.norm(V[1:] - q, axis=1)
    return (d.max() - d.min()) / d.min()

print(f"random uniform, d=384 : relative contrast = {contrast(R):.3f}")
print(f"real embeddings, d=384: relative contrast = {contrast(E):.3f}")
EOF
random uniform, d=384 : relative contrast = 0.212
real embeddings, d=384: relative contrast = 3.418

16× more contrast on real data at the same dimensionality. The nearest neighbour is genuinely, measurably nearer — because the model put it there.

The reason is the manifold hypothesis: meaningful data occupies a low-dimensional surface embedded in the high-dimensional space. You can estimate that intrinsic dimensionality:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
import numpy as np
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
texts = [f"database topic number {i} about replication and indexing" for i in range(300)]
E = m.encode(texts, normalize_embeddings=True)

# PCA: how many components explain 95% of variance?
C = E - E.mean(axis=0)
s = np.linalg.svd(C, compute_uv=False)
var = np.cumsum(s**2) / np.sum(s**2)
print(f"ambient dimensions   : {E.shape[1]}")
print(f"components for 90% var: {int(np.searchsorted(var, 0.90)) + 1}")
print(f"components for 95% var: {int(np.searchsorted(var, 0.95)) + 1}")
print(f"components for 99% var: {int(np.searchsorted(var, 0.99)) + 1}")
EOF
ambient dimensions   : 384
components for 90% var: 24
components for 95% var: 41
components for 99% var: 88

384 numbers, but 95% of the variation lives in 41 directions. The effective dimensionality is well inside the range where nearest-neighbour search behaves sensibly — which is why ANN indexes achieve high recall on embeddings while failing on random data of the same width.

❌ "High dimensions are cursed, so we should always reduce to 50 with PCA." The curse applies to uniformly-distributed data; real embeddings already live on a low-dimensional manifold. Aggressive reduction discards the directions that distinguish genuinely similar documents from merely-related ones, and the loss shows up as worse recall on exactly the hard queries you care about.

✅ Measure, then decide. Run recall@k at full dimensionality and at each candidate reduction on your own eval set. Frequently a Matryoshka truncation from 3072 to 1024 costs almost nothing while saving two-thirds of the storage, and a reduction to 128 costs 10 points of recall. Dimensionality reduction is a cost optimisation to be validated, not a correctness fix.

Part 5 — What It Means for Index Choice

The curse does not stop ANN indexes from working, but it shapes how they behave and what tuning costs.

Effect of higher dimensionalityPractical consequence
Distance computation is O(d)Query cost scales linearly — 3072-dim is 8× the work of 384
Storage is 4d bytes per vectorMemory is usually the binding constraint before CPU
Graph indexes need more edges for the same recallHigher M in HNSW → more memory and slower builds
Clustering needs more probesHigher nprobe in IVF → less speedup
Quantisation error grows relativelyReranking on full vectors becomes more necessary
$ python3 - <<'EOF'
import numpy as np, time
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 200_000
for d in [384, 768, 1536, 3072]:
    V = rng.standard_normal((n, d), dtype=np.float32)
    V /= np.linalg.norm(V, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    q = V[0]
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    for _ in range(3): np.argpartition(-(V @ q), 10)[:10]
    ms = (time.perf_counter() - t0) / 3 * 1000
    print(f"d={d:>5}  scan={ms:7.1f} ms   memory={n*d*4/1e9:.2f} GB")
    del V
EOF
d=  384  scan=   48.2 ms   memory=0.31 GB
d=  768  scan=   94.7 ms   memory=0.61 GB
d= 1536  scan=  191.4 ms   memory=1.23 GB
d= 3072  scan=  382.1 ms   memory=2.46 GB

Perfectly linear in both time and memory. The cost of dimensions is concrete and predictable, which means the question "is 3072 worth 8× the cost of 384?" has a measurable answer on your data — and frequently the answer is no.

Part 6 — Living With It

Three practical habits follow from everything above.

1. Measure intrinsic dimensionality, not ambient. If 95% of your corpus's variance fits in 40 components, a Matryoshka truncation is likely cheap. If it takes 800, be careful.

2. Expect scores to compress, and calibrate accordingly. In high dimensions, unrelated pairs cluster tightly near zero and relevant pairs sit in a narrow band above. That is workable — but it means thresholds need more precision than they would at low dimensions:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
docs = ["postgres replication", "kubernetes scheduling", "sourdough bread"]
D = m.encode(docs, normalize_embeddings=True)
q = m.encode(["database replica lag"], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
s = D @ q
print("scores:", np.round(s, 3))
print(f"best={s.max():.3f}  next={np.sort(s)[-2]:.3f}  gap={s.max()-np.sort(s)[-2]:.3f}")
EOF
scores: [0.681 0.142 0.031]
best=0.681  next=0.142  gap=0.539

A wide gap between the top result and the rest is the signature of a healthy retrieval setup. When that gap narrows across your eval set, the embedding model is not discriminating well on your corpus — a useful early signal, and one that Evaluating Retrieval formalises.

3. Choose the index for the dimensionality you actually have. Below ~20 dimensions, a k-d tree or even a B-tree on a derived key can be exact and fast. At embedding dimensionalities, only graph and clustering methods are viable, and the choice between them depends on scale and update patterns rather than on dimension count.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is the curse of dimensionality?

It is a family of effects where high-dimensional space behaves unlike the 2-D and 3-D space our intuition is built on. For vector search, the important one is that distances stop discriminating.

As dimensionality grows, the distance from a query to its nearest random point and to its farthest random point converge:

   dim    nearest  farthest   ratio
     2      0.008     1.212  151.50
    10      0.404     2.001    4.95
  1000      8.771    11.412    1.30

At 1000 dimensions the farthest point is only 1.3× further away than the nearest. "Nearest neighbour" becomes an almost arbitrary distinction.

The quantity that matters is relative contrast — (max − min) / min. When it approaches zero, nearest-neighbour search is answering a question with no meaningful answer.

The crucial qualification is that this describes uniformly random data. Real embeddings are not random: a trained model deliberately places related texts near each other, so the data occupies a much lower-dimensional surface within the space. That is why 1536-dimension search works well in practice while the theory sounds fatal.

Beginner: Why do vector databases not use B-trees or k-d trees?

Because both depend on pruning — proving a region of the data cannot contain a better answer, and skipping it — and pruning stops working in high dimensions.

A B-tree works on a one-dimensional ordering: a range query touches a small contiguous slice. A k-d tree generalises that by recursively splitting space along axes. In two or three dimensions each split eliminates roughly half the remaining candidates.

In high dimensions the query region intersects almost every partition, so nothing can be pruned and the tree visits nearly every point — plus traversal overhead:

d=  2  kd=  2.1ms  brute= 41.2ms   kd-tree wins
d= 20  kd=102.7ms  brute= 88.4ms   BRUTE FORCE WINS
d=256  kd=612.4ms  brute=428.1ms   BRUTE FORCE WINS

Past roughly 20 dimensions the tree is slower than simply scanning everything.

The underlying geometric reason is that volume concentrates near the boundary: at 50 dimensions the inscribed ball occupies about 10⁻²⁸ of the enclosing cube, so a spherical query region essentially never fits neatly inside a rectangular partition.

The methods that do work — HNSW and IVF — abandon exactness. They do not attempt to prove a region can be skipped; they follow a heuristic path and accept that they might miss something. Giving up the proof is exactly what makes them viable at embedding dimensionalities.

Mid: If the curse is real, why does 1536-dimension search work so well?

Because the curse describes uniformly-distributed data, and embeddings are the opposite of uniform.

The manifold hypothesis is the explanation: meaningful data occupies a thin, low-dimensional surface within the ambient space. A model trained contrastively puts related texts near each other and unrelated texts far apart by construction, which is precisely the structure the curse assumes is absent.

You can measure the difference directly:

random uniform, d=384 : relative contrast = 0.212
real embeddings, d=384: relative contrast = 3.418      # 16x better

And you can estimate the intrinsic dimensionality with PCA:

ambient dimensions    : 384
components for 95% var:  41

384 numbers describing variation that genuinely lives in about 41 directions. That is well inside the range where nearest-neighbour search behaves sensibly.

Two practical consequences follow. First, benchmarking an ANN index on random vectors gives pessimistic, unrepresentative results — always benchmark on real embeddings. Second, the fact that intrinsic dimensionality is low is exactly why quantisation and Matryoshka truncation work: there is genuine redundancy to compress.

The curse still exerts pressure at the margins — higher dimensions need more graph edges or more probes for the same recall, and cost scales linearly with d — but it does not break the approach.

Mid: Should you reduce embedding dimensionality with PCA?

Sometimes, as a validated cost optimisation — never reflexively "because of the curse".

The argument for: cost is linear in dimensions. Storage is 4d bytes per vector and query time is O(d), so halving dimensions halves both. At 10M vectors that is the difference between 61 GB and 31 GB, which can decide the instance size.

The argument against: reduction discards variance, and the discarded directions are often the ones distinguishing genuinely similar documents from merely-related ones. The loss appears on exactly the hard queries you care about, while easy queries look fine — so a casual check misses it.

The method is to measure rather than argue:

for dim in [3072, 1536, 1024, 512, 256]:
    print(dim, recall_at_10(truncate(embeddings, dim), evalset))

3072  0.891
1536  0.887      # essentially free
1024  0.879
 512  0.841
 256  0.762      # 13 points lost

Here 1536 is free and 256 is not. That is a decision, not a guess.

Prefer Matryoshka truncation over PCA where the model supports it. Matryoshka models are trained so the leading dimensions are themselves a valid embedding, so truncation degrades gracefully and requires no extra fitted transform, no separate artefact to version, and no risk of train/serve skew in the projection.

If using PCA, the projection must be fitted once and applied identically at ingestion and query time — a mismatch there is a silent quality collapse, in the same family as the prefix mismatch from Embeddings & Vector Space.

Senior: How does dimensionality influence your index and infrastructure choices?

It sets the cost floor and constrains the index family, so I would treat it as an early architectural decision rather than a model detail.

1. Dimensionality decides the index family outright. Below ~20 dimensions, exact partitioning structures work and are simpler. At embedding dimensionalities only graph (HNSW) and clustering (IVF) methods are viable, because space partitioning cannot prune. There is no judgement call here — the geometry decides it.

2. Memory is almost always the binding constraint, and the arithmetic is simple:

10M vectors:  384-dim → 15 GB    1536-dim → 61 GB    3072-dim → 123 GB
              + HNSW graph overhead, typically 30–50% on top

That decides instance size, and at the upper end it decides whether quantisation is optional or mandatory.

3. Query cost is linear in d, so 3072 dimensions is 8× the distance-computation work of 384 at the same recall. Combined with memory, choosing the largest available model has a concrete, ongoing price.

4. Higher dimensions need more index work for the same recall — a larger M in HNSW, more probes in IVF. So parameters must be re-tuned after a dimension change; carrying them over is a common cause of an unexplained recall drop.

5. The decision I would actually make: evaluate 2–3 models across dimensionalities on a real eval set, plot recall against cost, and pick the knee. In my experience a mid-size model at 768 or a truncated 1024 frequently sits within a point or two of the largest option at a third of the cost — and that margin is worth more spent on reranking, which reliably buys more quality per unit of infrastructure than extra dimensions do.

6. And I would benchmark on real embeddings, never random vectors. Random data is maximally cursed, so an index benchmarked on it looks far worse than it will perform in production — which leads to over-provisioning or to rejecting a perfectly good index.

Senior: Recall dropped after switching to a higher-dimensional model. Explain how that can happen.

Counter-intuitive, and there are several real mechanisms. I would work through them in order of likelihood rather than assuming the model is worse.

1. Index parameters were carried over unchanged. This is the commonest cause. Higher dimensions need more graph connectivity for the same recall, so an HNSW index built with M=16 at 384 dimensions may need M=32 at 1536:

384-dim,  M=16, ef=64  → recall@10 = 0.94
1536-dim, M=16, ef=64  → recall@10 = 0.88     # same params, worse recall
1536-dim, M=32, ef=128 → recall@10 = 0.96     # re-tuned

The measured "recall drop" is an index-tuning artefact, not a model property. This is easy to confirm: compare against exact brute-force search on the same vectors. If exact recall improved and indexed recall fell, it is the index.

2. Quantisation error grew. If the pipeline quantises, the relative error at higher dimensions is larger, and a configuration tuned for 384 may be too aggressive at 1536. The fix is reranking survivors on full-precision vectors.

3. Normalisation or prefix handling differs between the models. BGE normalises internally, MiniLM does not; E5 needs query/passage prefixes and OpenAI models do not. A pipeline carried over unchanged silently misuses the new model — and this shows up as a modest, uniform recall loss exactly like a worse model would.

4. The eval set was re-embedded but the corpus was not, or vice versa — a partially migrated index mixing two incompatible spaces. Worth ruling out immediately by checking the model tag distribution in the index.

5. The new model is genuinely worse on this domain. Bigger and higher-dimensional does not mean better for a specialised corpus; a general-purpose model can lose to a smaller domain-adjacent one. This is the conclusion only after the four above are eliminated.

The diagnostic that separates them is comparing exact search to indexed search on both models. Exact search isolates model quality from index behaviour, and that one comparison usually ends the investigation in a few minutes.

Solution

# A focused corpus (one domain) vs a diverse one

           ["replication","vacuum","indexing","wal","checkpoint","autovacuum",
            "partitioning","connection pooling","query planning","statistics"]] * 30

           [f"guitar {t} technique" for t in ["scales","chords","fingerpicking"]] * 30)

def intrinsic(E, label):
    C = E - E.mean(0)
    s = np.linalg.svd(C, compute_uv=False)
    var = np.cumsum(s**2) / (s**2).sum()
    d90 = int(np.searchsorted(var, 0.90)) + 1
    d95 = int(np.searchsorted(var, 0.95)) + 1
    print(f"{label:10} ambient={E.shape[1]}  90%={d90:>3}  95%={d95:>3}")

intrinsic(m.encode(diverse, normalize_embeddings=True), "diverse")

diverse    ambient=384  90%= 46  95%= 78

A focused corpus has lower intrinsic dimensionality — 31 components for 95% variance versus 78 for a diverse one. Both are far below 384.

import numpy as np
def truncate_pca(E, k):
    C = E - E.mean(0)
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(C, full_matrices=False)
    R = C @ Vt[:k].T

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • As dimensionality grows, distances between random points converge — relative contrast (max−min)/min approaches zero and "nearest neighbour" loses meaning.
  • The curse describes uniformly random data; real embeddings occupy a low-dimensional manifold and show an order of magnitude more contrast at the same width.
  • Intrinsic dimensionality — often 20–80 components for 95% of variance — is the number that matters, not the ambient 384 or 1536.
  • Almost all volume in a high-dimensional ball sits near its surface, and the inscribed ball occupies essentially none of the enclosing cube.
  • Random high-dimensional vectors are nearly orthogonal, which gives a very quiet noise floor — a real match can be 10 standard deviations above the background.
  • Space-partitioning indexes (k-d tree, R-tree, ball tree) stop pruning past ~20 dimensions and become slower than a brute-force scan.
  • At 100 dimensions a k-d tree computes 99.9% of the distances a full scan would — it prunes nothing and adds traversal overhead.
  • The surviving index families, HNSW and IVF, work precisely because they abandon the exactness proof that partitioning depends on.
  • Cost is linear in dimensions: 4d bytes per vector and O(d) per distance, so 3072 is 8× the cost of 384.
  • Higher dimensions need more index work for the same recall — re-tune HNSW M/ef after any dimension change.
  • Never benchmark an ANN index on random vectors: random data is maximally cursed and produces pessimistic, unrepresentative recall.
  • Dimensionality reduction is a cost optimisation to validate with recall@k, not a correctness fix — and Matryoshka truncation is safer than a fitted PCA projection.
  • A wide gap between the top result and the rest is the signature of healthy retrieval; a narrowing gap across your eval set is an early warning that the model does not discriminate on your corpus.
