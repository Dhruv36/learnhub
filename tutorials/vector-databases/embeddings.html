Embeddings & Vector Space Foundations

What you'll master: what an embedding model actually learns and why arithmetic on vectors means anything at all, how to choose a model using the three properties that matter (dimensions, context window, domain fit) rather than a leaderboard position, why asymmetric search needs different treatment for questions and documents, what happens to text that exceeds the context window, why switching models forces a full re-embed, and how to measure whether an embedding model is any good on your data. Prerequisites: What a Vector Database Is For.

Part 1 — What the Model Learned

An embedding model is a neural network trained so that the distance between its outputs reflects semantic relatedness. It is not doing lookup, translation, or compression in any ordinary sense — it learned a geometry.

        embedding model
text  ─────────────────►  vector ∈ ℝᵈ

"a dog"    → [ 0.21, -0.08,  0.44, ...]  ┐
"a puppy"  → [ 0.19, -0.11,  0.41, ...]  ├── close together
"a canine" → [ 0.23, -0.06,  0.47, ...]  ┘
"a lawsuit"→ [-0.32,  0.51, -0.12, ...]  ← far away

The training objective is what creates this structure. Modern text embedding models are trained contrastively: shown pairs known to be related (a question and its answer, a title and its article, two paraphrases) and pairs known to be unrelated, and adjusted so related pairs move closer and unrelated pairs move apart.

loss ≈  −log  exp(sim(anchor, positive) / τ)
              ─────────────────────────────────────────
              Σ over positive AND all negatives in the batch

Two consequences follow directly from that objective, and they explain most surprising behaviour later:

ConsequenceWhy it followsWhat it means in practice
"Similar" means whatever the training pairs meantThe model optimised for those pairs specificallyA model trained on web Q&A may not know your domain's notion of similarity
Only relative distances are meaningfulThe loss compares pairs, never fixes absolute valuesA cosine score of 0.7 means nothing on its own — only in comparison
Rare tokens are poorly placedThey appeared too rarely to shape the geometryError codes and SKUs embed near the space's centre, close to everything
Frequent phrasings dominateGradient signal follows the data distributionUnusual phrasings retrieve worse than common ones
🔑 The score has no absolute meaning — only a distribution does. Newcomers set a threshold like "0.8 is a match" from intuition and get wildly different results per model. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 routinely scores unrelated text around 0.1 and good matches around 0.5; OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small puts unrelated pairs near 0.2 and good matches near 0.6. The only defensible way to set a threshold is to score a sample of known-relevant and known-irrelevant pairs on your data and put the line between the two distributions.

Part 2 — Vector Arithmetic and Its Limits

The famous demonstration is that king − man + woman ≈ queen. It works, sometimes, and understanding why it is unreliable matters more than the party trick.

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
def e(t): return m.encode([t], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]

target = e("king") - e("man") + e("woman")
cands  = ["queen", "king", "woman", "princess", "monarch"]
V = m.encode(cands, normalize_embeddings=True)
for s, c in sorted(zip(V @ (target/np.linalg.norm(target)), cands), reverse=True):
    print(f"{s:.3f}  {c}")
EOF
0.812  king
0.744  woman
0.671  queen
0.588  monarch
0.402  princess

"king" and "woman" outrank "queen" — because the input words dominate the result. The classic demonstration excludes the input terms from the candidate list, which quietly does most of the work. Sentence embedding models are trained for whole-sentence similarity, not for compositional word algebra, so the analogy property is a weak side effect rather than a guarantee.

What is reliable is direction-based similarity between comparable texts:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
pairs = [
  ("How do I reset my password?", "What's the procedure for password recovery?"),
  ("How do I reset my password?", "How do I change my email address?"),
  ("How do I reset my password?", "The mitochondria is the powerhouse of the cell"),
]
for a, b in pairs:
    va, vb = m.encode([a, b], normalize_embeddings=True)
    print(f"{float(va @ vb):.3f}  {b[:45]}")
EOF
0.831  What's the procedure for password recovery?
0.412  How do I change my email address?
0.031  The mitochondria is the powerhouse of the c

Paraphrase scores high, same-domain-different-intent scores middling, unrelated scores near zero. That ordering is exactly what retrieval depends on, and it is what the model was actually trained to do.

Three properties decide a model's fitness, and leaderboard rank is not one of them.

Dimensions cost real money at scale, and the arithmetic is worth doing before choosing:

10M vectors:
   384-dim  ×  4 bytes  =  15 GB   (fits comfortably in RAM)
  1536-dim  ×  4 bytes  =  61 GB   (needs a large instance, or quantisation)
  3072-dim  ×  4 bytes  = 123 GB   (quantisation is mandatory)
                                    — plus HNSW graph overhead, typically +30–50%

Some newer models support Matryoshka representation learning, where the first N dimensions are themselves a usable embedding. That makes dimensionality a runtime dial rather than a model choice:

# text-embedding-3-large at reduced dimensions
resp = client.embeddings.create(model="text-embedding-3-large",
                                input=text, dimensions=1024)   # 3072 → 1024
# Quality degrades gracefully; storage drops 3x

Part 4 — Asymmetric Search and Prefixes

Retrieval is usually asymmetric: a short question must match a long passage that answers it. Those two texts do not look alike, and a model trained only on symmetric similarity handles it poorly.

Symmetric  :  "how to reset password"  ↔  "password reset instructions"     (similar shape)
Asymmetric :  "how do I reset it?"     ↔  "To reset your password, navigate to Settings,
                                            select Security, and choose Reset Password.
                                            You will receive an email within..."       (different shape)

Models such as E5 and BGE are trained with instruction prefixes that tell the model which role a text is playing. Omitting them is one of the most common and most costly mistakes in a first RAG build:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
m = SentenceTransformer('intfloat/e5-base-v2')
doc = "To reset your password, go to Settings > Security and select Reset Password."
q   = "how do I change my login credentials"

# WRONG — no prefixes
a = m.encode([q, doc], normalize_embeddings=True)
print(f"without prefixes: {float(a[0] @ a[1]):.3f}")

# RIGHT — the prefixes the model was trained with
b = m.encode([f"query: {q}", f"passage: {doc}"], normalize_embeddings=True)
print(f"with prefixes   : {float(b[0] @ b[1]):.3f}")
EOF
without prefixes: 0.792
with prefixes   : 0.871

The gap looks small in isolation and is decisive in ranking: it shifts every score, and what matters is whether the right document outranks the wrong ones. Worse, the mistake is silent — the system works, just measurably less well, and nothing errors.

❌ Embedding queries and documents identically because the API is the same function. With a prefix-trained model this discards a chunk of the model's quality, and with any model it ignores that a question and an answer have systematically different shapes. Nothing fails loudly; recall is simply worse than it should be, and the team concludes the model is mediocre.

✅ Read the model card and apply the intended prefixes on both sides. E5 wants query: and passage:; BGE wants an instruction prefix on queries only; OpenAI models want neither. Then verify with a held-out set — the prefixes must be applied consistently at ingestion and at query time, and a mismatch between the two is a silent quality regression that survives any amount of index tuning.

Part 5 — Truncation, Normalisation and Model Changes

Text longer than the context window is silently truncated. No error, no warning — the tail simply does not exist as far as the vector is concerned:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
print("max_seq_length:", m.max_seq_length)        # 256 tokens

long = "Introduction. " * 200 + "THE CRITICAL ANSWER IS 42."
short = "THE CRITICAL ANSWER IS 42."
a, b = m.encode([long, short], normalize_embeddings=True)
print(f"similarity of long vs the answer alone: {float(a @ b):.3f}")
EOF
max_seq_length: 256
similarity of long vs the answer alone: 0.104

The answer was in the text and is effectively absent from the vector. This is the single strongest argument for careful chunking — and for checking token counts at ingestion rather than assuming.

# Always know your token budget before ingesting
import tiktoken
enc = tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")
n = len(enc.encode(text))
assert n <= 8191, f"chunk is {n} tokens — will be truncated"

Normalisation scales a vector to unit length. It is what makes cosine similarity and dot product equivalent, which matters because many indexes only implement dot product:

v_normalised = v / ‖v‖          →   ‖v‖ = 1

then:  cosine(a, b) == dot(a, b)      # only when both are normalised

Normalising at ingestion is almost always right: it makes the metric unambiguous and lets you use the fastest index path. Similarity Metrics covers when it is not.

Changing embedding models invalidates every stored vector. Different models produce different, incompatible spaces — comparing across them is meaningless:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
a = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
b = SentenceTransformer('all-mpnet-base-v2')
t = "database replication"
va, vb = a.encode([t])[0], b.encode([t])[0]
print(f"dims: {len(va)} vs {len(vb)}")     # not even the same length
EOF
dims: 384 vs 768

Even at matching dimensions the axes mean different things, so a distance between them is noise. A model upgrade is therefore a full re-embed of the corpus — which is why the ingestion pipeline should be re-runnable from source documents, and why the vector index should be treated as a derived artefact rather than the system of record.

Part 6 — Evaluating a Model on Your Data

Public benchmarks (MTEB) rank models on general corpora. Your corpus is not that corpus, and the ranking frequently does not transfer. Measuring takes an afternoon and settles the question.

# The minimum viable evaluation set
[
  {"query": "how do I rotate the signing key",
   "relevant_ids": ["runbook-auth-3", "runbook-auth-4"]},
  {"query": "standby not catching up",
   "relevant_ids": ["pg-replication-2"]},
  ...   # 50–100 of these, drawn from real user queries
]

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
import numpy as np
def recall_at_k(model, docs, doc_ids, evalset, k=10):
    D = model.encode(docs, normalize_embeddings=True)
    hits = 0
    for case in evalset:
        q = model.encode([case["query"]], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
        top = [doc_ids[i] for i in np.argsort(-(D @ q))[:k]]
        hits += len(set(top) & set(case["relevant_ids"])) / len(case["relevant_ids"])
    return hits / len(evalset)
EOF
MetricAnswersUse when
Recall@kDid the relevant documents make the top k?The primary RAG metric — a reranker can fix order, not absence
MRRHow high was the first relevant result?When users see a ranked list
nDCG@kQuality of the whole ranking, graded relevanceWhen some results are more relevant than others
Latency & cost per 1MWhat it costs to runAlways — it decides ties

Recall@k is the one that matters most for RAG, because everything downstream can only reorder what retrieval returned. A document that never reaches the candidate set cannot be recovered by reranking, a better prompt, or a larger model. Evaluating Retrieval develops this into a full methodology.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is an embedding, and what does the model actually learn?

An embedding is a fixed-length list of numbers representing a piece of content, produced by a neural network trained so that the distance between outputs reflects semantic relatedness.

"a dog"     → [0.21, -0.08, 0.44, ...]  ┐
"a puppy"   → [0.19, -0.11, 0.41, ...]  ├ close
"a lawsuit" → [-0.32, 0.51, -0.12, ...] ← far

What the model learned is a geometry. Training is contrastive: it is shown pairs known to be related (a question and its answer, a title and its article) and pairs known to be unrelated, and adjusted so related pairs move closer and unrelated pairs move apart.

Two consequences follow that explain most surprising behaviour later. First, "similar" means whatever the training pairs meant — a model trained on web Q&A may not share your domain's notion of similarity. Second, only relative distances are meaningful: the loss compares pairs and never fixes absolute values, so a cosine score of 0.7 means nothing on its own.

That second point has a direct practical consequence: similarity thresholds cannot be chosen by intuition. They must come from scoring known-relevant and known-irrelevant pairs on your own data and placing the line between the two distributions.

Beginner: How do you choose the number of dimensions?

By trading retrieval quality against storage, memory and search cost — all of which scale linearly with dimensionality.

10M vectors:
   384-dim → 15 GB      1536-dim → 61 GB      3072-dim → 123 GB
                                   (plus 30–50% HNSW graph overhead)

Higher dimensions generally capture more nuance, but with diminishing returns that arrive sooner than people expect. Going from 384 to 1536 often buys a few points of recall for 4× the cost, and whether that is worth it depends entirely on the corpus.

The method is to measure rather than assume: build an evaluation set of 50–100 real queries with known-relevant documents, compute recall@10 for each candidate model, and put that next to the cost. Frequently a 384-dimension model is within a point or two of a 1536-dimension one on a narrow domain corpus, and then the choice is easy.

Matryoshka models make this a runtime dial rather than a commitment — with text-embedding-3-large you can request 1024 dimensions instead of 3072 and quality degrades gracefully, because the model was trained so the leading dimensions are themselves a usable embedding.

I would also note that dimensionality interacts with the index: at high dimensions, quantisation stops being an optimisation and becomes a requirement for the index to fit in memory at all.

Mid: What is asymmetric search and why does it need special handling?

Asymmetric search is when the query and the documents have systematically different shapes — a short question retrieving a long passage that answers it:

query:    "how do I reset it?"                    (6 words, interrogative)
passage:  "To reset your password, navigate to Settings, select
           Security, and choose Reset Password..."  (60 words, instructional)

A model trained only on symmetric similarity — paraphrase pairs, where both sides look alike — handles this poorly, because the surface forms differ in length, register and grammatical mood even when the semantic relationship is perfect.

Models like E5 and BGE are trained for it explicitly, using instruction prefixes that tell the model which role a text plays:

m.encode([f"query: {question}"])     # the asking side
m.encode([f"passage: {document}"])   # the answering side

Omitting them is a common and expensive mistake, because it is completely silent. The system works, recall is measurably worse, nothing errors, and the team concludes the model is mediocre.

Two operational points matter. The prefixes must be applied consistently at ingestion and at query time — a mismatch between the two is a quality regression that survives any amount of index tuning. And the requirement varies by model family: E5 wants both prefixes, BGE wants an instruction on queries only, OpenAI models want neither. There is no substitute for reading the model card.

Mid: What happens when text exceeds the model's context window?

It is silently truncated. There is no error, no warning, and no indication in the resulting vector that anything was dropped:

m.max_seq_length          # 256 tokens for all-MiniLM-L6-v2
text = "Introduction. " * 200 + "THE CRITICAL ANSWER IS 42."
# the answer is past token 256 and simply does not exist in the vector
similarity(embed(text), embed("THE CRITICAL ANSWER IS 42.")) → 0.104

The answer was in the document and is effectively absent from its embedding. Retrieval will never surface that document for a question about it.

The defences are layered. Count tokens at ingestion and assert on the limit, so oversized input is a loud failure rather than a silent one. Chunk documents so every chunk fits comfortably inside the window — comfortably, because token counts vary with content and a chunk sized at exactly the limit will occasionally exceed it.

But a large context window is not automatically better, which is the subtler point. A model with an 8192-token window will happily embed a whole document into one vector, and that vector becomes an average of everything in it. A specific fact gets diluted by unrelated content, so the chunk scores lower for the question it actually answers than a tighter chunk would. Retrieval granularity is a deliberate choice, not something to maximise.

This is why chunk size is one of the highest-leverage parameters in a RAG system, and why Chunking & Ingestion gets a full lesson.

Senior: How would you evaluate and select an embedding model for a production system?

I would treat it as a measurement exercise on our own data, because public benchmark rank transfers poorly to a specific domain.

1. Build an evaluation set first. 50–100 real user queries, each labelled with the document IDs that genuinely answer it. Real queries matter — synthetic ones written by the team are phrased like the documents and inflate every model's score:

{"query": "standby not catching up", "relevant_ids": ["pg-replication-2"]}

2. Measure recall@k as the primary metric. Everything downstream — reranking, prompt construction, the model itself — can only reorder what retrieval returned. A document absent from the candidate set is unrecoverable, which makes recall the metric with veto power. MRR and nDCG matter secondarily, when users see a ranked list.

3. Compare candidates on the axes that actually differ:

AxisQuestion
Recall@10 on our eval setDoes it find our documents?
DimensionsWhat does it cost to store and search at our scale?
Context windowDoes our chunk size fit comfortably?
Asymmetric supportIs our workload question→passage?
Latency and cost per millionIngestion is a bulk cost; queries are a per-request cost
Self-hosted vs APIData residency, cold-start, vendor risk

4. Weight operational factors properly. A model two points better on recall but requiring GPU hosting may be the wrong choice for a small team; conversely, an API model may be unacceptable if documents cannot leave the network. These are not tiebreakers, they are often the decision.

5. Plan for the upgrade before making the choice. Switching models invalidates every stored vector, so the ingestion pipeline must be re-runnable from source documents and the vector index must be a derived artefact rather than the system of record. Teams that store only vectors discover this at the worst moment. I would also want the model name and version recorded per vector, so a partially-migrated index is detectable rather than silently mixed.

6. Re-evaluate on a schedule. The eval set is a durable asset — it makes the next model decision an afternoon's work instead of a debate.

Senior: Your RAG system works well in English but poorly for other languages. Diagnose and fix.

Several distinct causes produce this symptom, and they need different fixes, so I would separate them before changing anything.

1. Check whether the model is multilingual at all. Most default choices — all-MiniLM-L6-v2, bge-base-en — are English-only. They will still produce vectors for French or Japanese input, which is the trap: no error, just poor geometry:

sim("dog", "chien")   → 0.31 on an English-only model    # should be ~0.9
sim("dog", "perro")   → 0.28

2. Distinguish two different requirements, because they need different models:

  • Multilingual — queries and documents in the same non-English language. Needs a model trained on that language.
  • Cross-lingual — an English query retrieving a German document. Needs a model explicitly trained to align languages in one space, such as multilingual-e5 or LaBSE.

Cross-lingual is the harder requirement and it is frequently assumed rather than specified.

3. Check tokenisation. An English-centric tokeniser fragments other scripts badly — text that is 100 tokens in English can be 400 in Japanese or Thai. That silently truncates chunks at the context window and pushes cost up, and it shows up as "retrieval is worse for these languages" without any obvious cause:

len(enc.encode("database replication lag"))        → 4 tokens
len(enc.encode("データベースレプリケーションの遅延"))  → 19 tokens

4. Check the corpus itself. Often the documents are only in English and the real problem is coverage, not embeddings — no retrieval model can find a translation that does not exist. Cross-lingual retrieval would help there; a better multilingual embedding model would not.

5. Measure per language rather than in aggregate. A single overall recall number hides this entirely — I would build a per-language eval set and report recall@10 by language, which turns "poorly for other languages" into a specific, ranked list of where the loss is.

6. Then choose the fix that matches the cause: a multilingual model plus a full re-embed if the geometry is wrong; hybrid search with per-language analysers if the failures are lexical; translation at ingestion or query time if the corpus is genuinely monolingual. And I would expect to keep language as metadata regardless, so filtering and per-language evaluation stay possible.

🏋️ Exercises

1 (easy): Explore what an embedding model considers similar.
Solution 
$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')

pairs = [
    ("cat", "kitten"),
    ("cat", "dog"),
    ("cat", "automobile"),
    ("The server is down", "The service is unavailable"),
    ("The server is down", "The server is up"),
    ("I love this product", "I hate this product"),
]
for a, b in pairs:
    va, vb = m.encode([a, b], normalize_embeddings=True)
    print(f"{float(va @ vb):.3f}   {a!r} ↔ {b!r}")
EOF
0.712   'cat' ↔ 'kitten'
0.531   'cat' ↔ 'dog'
0.098   'cat' ↔ 'automobile'
0.804   'The server is down' ↔ 'The service is unavailable'
0.831   'The server is down' ↔ 'The server is up'
0.762   'I love this product' ↔ 'I hate this product'

The last two are the interesting results. "server is down" and "server is up" score 0.831 — higher than the genuine paraphrase — and "love" and "hate" score 0.762. Embeddings capture topical similarity strongly and polarity weakly.

Why: the two sentences share almost all their tokens and their subject matter. The model was trained to place texts about the same thing near each other, and negation is a small signal against that.

The practical consequence — a query about outages will retrieve documents saying everything is fine:

$ python3 -c "
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
docs = ['The payment service is currently down',
        'The payment service is fully operational',
        'Payment service architecture overview']
import numpy as np
D = m.encode(docs, normalize_embeddings=True)
q = m.encode(['is the payment service broken'], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
for s, d in sorted(zip(D @ q, docs), reverse=True): print(f'{s:.3f}  {d}')
"
0.694  The payment service is fully operational
0.681  The payment service is currently down
0.512  Payment service architecture overview

The wrong answer ranks first. This is why status and state belong in metadata that can be filtered on, not left for the embedding to distinguish.

2 (easy): Prove that similarity scores are not comparable across models.
Solution 
$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np

pairs = [("how to reset a password", "password recovery steps"),      # relevant
         ("how to reset a password", "quarterly revenue report")]      # irrelevant

for name in ['all-MiniLM-L6-v2', 'all-mpnet-base-v2']:
    m = SentenceTransformer(name)
    print(f"\n{name}  (dim={m.get_sentence_embedding_dimension()})")
    for a, b in pairs:
        va, vb = m.encode([a, b], normalize_embeddings=True)
        print(f"  {float(va @ vb):.3f}  {b}")
EOF

all-MiniLM-L6-v2  (dim=384)
  0.681  password recovery steps
  0.042  quarterly revenue report

all-mpnet-base-v2  (dim=768)
  0.742  password recovery steps
  0.118  quarterly revenue report

Same pairs, different absolute scores. A threshold of 0.7 accepts the relevant pair on mpnet and rejects it on MiniLM — while both models rank correctly.

Derive the threshold from the distributions instead:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')

relevant = [("reset password", "password recovery guide"),
            ("server down", "service outage report"),
            ("vpn issues", "troubleshooting vpn connectivity")]
irrelevant = [("reset password", "quarterly revenue"),
              ("server down", "office lunch menu"),
              ("vpn issues", "employee handbook")]

def scores(ps):
    out = []
    for a, b in ps:
        va, vb = m.encode([a, b], normalize_embeddings=True)
        out.append(float(va @ vb))
    return np.array(out)

r, i = scores(relevant), scores(irrelevant)
print(f"relevant   : min={r.min():.3f} mean={r.mean():.3f}")
print(f"irrelevant : max={i.max():.3f} mean={i.mean():.3f}")
print(f"threshold  : {(r.min() + i.max())/2:.3f}")
EOF
relevant   : min=0.542 mean=0.631
irrelevant : max=0.121 mean=0.081
threshold  : 0.331

0.331, not the intuitive 0.8. And the gap between the distributions is itself a diagnostic — a wide separation means the model discriminates well on this corpus; overlapping distributions mean no threshold will work and the model is a poor fit.

Recompute this whenever the model or the corpus changes, and store the threshold as configuration alongside the model name rather than as a constant in the code.

3 (medium): Measure the cost of ignoring instruction prefixes.
Solution 
$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np
m = SentenceTransformer('intfloat/e5-base-v2')

docs = [
 "To reset your password, go to Settings > Security and select Reset Password. "
 "A confirmation email arrives within five minutes.",
 "Expense reports must be submitted by the fifth of each month via the finance portal.",
 "The VPN client requires a certificate renewed annually through IT self-service.",
 "Password complexity rules require 12 characters including a symbol.",
]
queries = [("how do I change my login credentials", 0),
           ("when are expenses due", 1),
           ("vpn certificate expired", 2),
           ("what are the password requirements", 3)]

def evaluate(prefix):
    D = m.encode([f"passage: {d}" if prefix else d for d in docs],
                 normalize_embeddings=True)
    correct = 0
    for q, gold in queries:
        qv = m.encode([f"query: {q}" if prefix else q], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
        ranked = np.argsort(-(D @ qv))
        correct += (ranked[0] == gold)
        print(f"  {'✓' if ranked[0]==gold else '✗'} {q[:34]:36} top={(D@qv)[ranked[0]]:.3f}")
    return correct / len(queries)

print("WITHOUT prefixes:"); a = evaluate(False)
print("WITH prefixes:");    b = evaluate(True)
print(f"\naccuracy@1: {a:.0%} → {b:.0%}")
EOF

Now show the worse failure — inconsistent prefixes:

$ python3 -c "
# documents embedded WITH prefix, query WITHOUT
D = m.encode([f'passage: {d}' for d in docs], normalize_embeddings=True)

mismatched: 0.783
consistent: 0.871

This is the dangerous case in production: ingestion and query are usually written by different code paths, often months apart. A mismatch causes a permanent, silent quality loss that no amount of index tuning recovers.

The defence is to put embedding behind a single function used by both paths, and to record the model name and prefix convention as metadata on the index so a mismatch is detectable.

Guard at ingestion:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
m = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
LIMIT = m.max_seq_length

def safe_embed(texts):
    for t in texts:
        n = len(m.tokenizer.encode(t))
        if n > LIMIT:
            raise ValueError(f"{n} tokens exceeds limit {LIMIT} — chunk this first")
    return m.encode(texts, normalize_embeddings=True)

except ValueError as e:
    print("caught:", e)

And note the other direction — a large window is not automatically better:

chunk of 40 words containing the answer   → similarity 0.641
chunk of 400 words containing the answer  → similarity 0.361

The vector is an average of everything in the text, so a specific fact gets diluted. Retrieval granularity is a deliberate choice, which is the subject of Chunking & Ingestion.

5 (hard): Build an evaluation harness and compare models on your own data.
Solution 
$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np, time

CORPUS = {
 "pg-1": "Streaming replication is asynchronous by default in PostgreSQL.",
 "pg-2": "If a standby falls behind, check for long-running queries blocking WAL replay.",
 "pg-3": "Set max_standby_streaming_delay to 30s to cancel conflicting queries.",
 "k8-1": "Pods are evicted when a node exceeds its memory allocatable threshold.",
 "k8-2": "Set requests equal to limits for the Guaranteed QoS class.",
 "sec-1": "Rotate the signing key quarterly using the key-rotation runbook.",
 "sec-2": "SSH access requires a hardware token in addition to a certificate.",
 "fin-1": "Expense reports are due by the fifth of each month.",
}
EVAL = [
 {"q": "my replica is lagging",            "gold": ["pg-2", "pg-1"]},
 {"q": "how to stop standby query cancels","gold": ["pg-3"]},
 {"q": "why did my pod get killed",        "gold": ["k8-1"]},
 {"q": "guaranteed quality of service",    "gold": ["k8-2"]},
 {"q": "when do I rotate keys",            "gold": ["sec-1"]},
 {"q": "expense deadline",                 "gold": ["fin-1"]},
]

def evaluate(model_name, k=3):
    m = SentenceTransformer(model_name)
    ids, texts = list(CORPUS), list(CORPUS.values())
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    D = m.encode(texts, normalize_embeddings=True)
    enc_ms = (time.perf_counter() - t0) * 1000

    recall, rr = 0.0, 0.0
    for case in EVAL:
        q = m.encode([case["q"]], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
        ranked = [ids[i] for i in np.argsort(-(D @ q))]
        top = ranked[:k]
        recall += len(set(top) & set(case["gold"])) / len(case["gold"])
        first = next((r+1 for r, d in enumerate(ranked) if d in case["gold"]), None)
        rr += 1/first if first else 0
    n = len(EVAL)
    return {"model": model_name, "dim": m.get_sentence_embedding_dimension(),
            "recall@3": recall/n, "mrr": rr/n, "encode_ms": enc_ms}

for name in ["all-MiniLM-L6-v2", "all-mpnet-base-v2", "BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5"]:
    r = evaluate(name)
    print(f"{r['model']:26} dim={r['dim']:<5} recall@3={r['recall@3']:.2f} "
          f"mrr={r['mrr']:.2f} encode={r['encode_ms']:.0f}ms")
EOF

all-mpnet-base-v2          dim=768   recall@3=1.00 mrr=0.94 encode=118ms
BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5     dim=384   recall@3=1.00 mrr=0.92 encode=48ms

6 (hard): Plan and execute a zero-downtime embedding model migration.
Solution

1. Confirm the problem: old and new vectors are incompatible.

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np
old = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')      # 384
new = SentenceTransformer('all-mpnet-base-v2')     # 768

vo, vn = old.encode([t])[0], new.encode([t])[0]

a = SentenceTransformer('BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5')  # also 384

vo_n = vo / np.linalg.norm(vo)

EOF
dims 384 vs 768 — cannot even compare
same dim, different model, similarity: 0.037

0.037 for the identical string. The spaces are unrelated; a mixed index returns garbage.

2. The migration design — dual-write with a version tag:

CREATE TABLE chunks (
  id          text PRIMARY KEY,
  doc_id      text NOT NULL,
  content     text NOT NULL,          -- SOURCE OF TRUTH: re-embeddable
  metadata    jsonb NOT NULL,
  model       text NOT NULL,          -- 'minilm-l6-v2' | 'bge-small-v1.5'
  embedding   vector(384) NOT NULL
);
CREATE INDEX ON chunks USING hnsw (embedding vector_cosine_ops)
  WHERE model = 'bge-small-v1.5';     -- partial index per model

Storing content is what makes this possible at all — the index is a derived artefact.

3. Backfill without downtime:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
BATCH = 500
def backfill(conn, model, new_name):
    while True:
        rows = conn.execute("""
            SELECT id, content FROM chunks
            WHERE model = 'minilm-l6-v2'
              AND NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM chunks c2
                              WHERE c2.doc_id = chunks.doc_id AND c2.model = %s)
            LIMIT %s""", (new_name, BATCH)).fetchall()
        if not rows: break
        vecs = model.encode([r.content for r in rows], normalize_embeddings=True)
        conn.executemany("""
            INSERT INTO chunks (id, doc_id, content, metadata, model, embedding)
            SELECT id || ':' || %s, doc_id, content, metadata, %s, %s
            FROM chunks WHERE id = %s""", ...)
        conn.commit()
        print(f"backfilled {len(rows)}")
EOF

Batched and resumable — a crash halfway through loses one batch, not the run.

4. Shadow-evaluate before switching any traffic:

$ python3 - <<'EOF'
def shadow_compare(evalset, search_old, search_new, k=10):
    old_r = new_r = 0.0
    regressions = []
    for case in evalset:
        o = set(search_old(case["q"], k)) & set(case["gold"])
        n = set(search_new(case["q"], k)) & set(case["gold"])
        old_r += len(o)/len(case["gold"]); new_r += len(n)/len(case["gold"])
        if len(n) < len(o): regressions.append(case["q"])
    N = len(evalset)
    print(f"recall@{k}: old={old_r/N:.3f} new={new_r/N:.3f}")
    print(f"regressed on {len(regressions)} queries: {regressions[:3]}")
EOF
recall@10: old=0.812 new=0.891
regressed on 4 queries: ['PG-4821 error', 'SKU-99120', 'ERR-3021 meaning']

Aggregate recall improved, but four queries regressed — all exact-code lookups. That is exactly the finding a single average would hide, and it argues for adding hybrid search alongside the migration rather than accepting the regression.

5. Cut over gradually with a kill switch:

EMBEDDING_MODEL = os.getenv("EMBEDDING_MODEL", "minilm-l6-v2")
ROLLOUT_PCT     = int(os.getenv("NEW_MODEL_PCT", "0"))

def pick_model(request_id):
    return "bge-small-v1.5" if hash(request_id) % 100 < ROLLOUT_PCT else EMBEDDING_MODEL
# 0% → 5% → 25% → 100%, watching recall and latency at each step

6. Only then clean up:

DELETE FROM chunks WHERE model = 'minilm-l6-v2';
DROP INDEX chunks_embedding_minilm_idx;

The properties that made this safe: source text stored so vectors are rebuildable, a model tag per row so mixed state is detectable rather than silent, partial indexes so both models coexist, shadow evaluation before any traffic moved, and a percentage rollout with an env-var revert.

The costly alternative — dropping the index and rebuilding in place — means a search outage for the duration of the backfill and no way to compare quality or roll back.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Embedding models are trained contrastively, so "similar" means whatever the training pairs meant — a general model may not share your domain's notion of similarity.
  • Only relative distances are meaningful; an absolute score like 0.7 means nothing without the distribution it came from.
  • Derive similarity thresholds by scoring known-relevant and known-irrelevant pairs on your data, and store the threshold with the model name.
  • Embeddings capture topical similarity strongly and polarity weakly — "service is up" and "service is down" score very close, so state belongs in filterable metadata.
  • Word arithmetic (king − man + woman) is an unreliable side effect, not a mechanism — sentence models are trained for sentence similarity.
  • Dimensions scale storage, memory and search cost linearly; measure the recall difference against the cost rather than defaulting to the largest.
  • Matryoshka models make dimensionality a runtime dial, degrading gracefully when truncated.
  • Retrieval is usually asymmetric — a short question matching a long passage — and prefix-trained models like E5 and BGE need their prefixes to perform.
  • Prefix mismatch between ingestion and query time is a silent, permanent quality loss no index tuning can recover.
  • Text beyond the context window is silently truncated with no error — count tokens at ingestion and assert.
  • A large context window is not automatically better: one vector for a long document averages away the specific fact you need.
  • Normalise at ingestion so cosine and dot product coincide and the fastest index path is available.
  • Different models produce incompatible spaces, so a model change requires re-embedding the whole corpus — keep source text and treat the index as derived.
  • Choose models by measuring recall@k on 50–100 real queries from your own corpus, not by leaderboard rank.
