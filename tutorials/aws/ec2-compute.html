EC2, EBS & Auto Scaling Compute

What you'll master: what a vCPU actually is — a hyperthread, not a core, except on Graviton where it is — and how to read an instance type name as a specification; burstable instances, where T3 silently bills you for surplus CPU and T2 silently throttles you to 20%, both without an alarm; EBS as a network-attached device whose gp2 IOPS are tied to volume size, whose burst balance runs out on a cliff, and whose restored snapshots are slow for hours because blocks load lazily from S3 on first touch; the AMI and launch-template lifecycle, and what stop, terminate and hibernate each destroy; Auto Scaling groups, where the default health check is EC2-only so an instance whose application is dead but whose kernel is fine is never replaced, and where target tracking on a slow-starting app oscillates between 4 and 19 instances; placement groups, capacity reservations and the InsufficientInstanceCapacity error that is real and does happen; and right-sizing when CloudWatch cannot see memory at all. Prerequisites: VPCs, Subnets & Security Groups, Route 53, Load Balancers & CloudFront and Cost: Pricing Models & Data Transfer.

Part 1 — Reading an Instance Type, and What a vCPU Is

A vCPU IS A HYPERTHREAD — not a core. Prove it on the box:

$ lscpu | grep -E 'CPU\(s\)|Thread|Core|Model name'
CPU(s):                  8
Thread(s) per core:      2                         ← two threads share one physical core
Core(s) per socket:      4                         ← m6i.2xlarge = 8 vCPU = 4 REAL cores
Model name:              Intel(R) Xeon(R) Platinum 8375C

$ # on Graviton, the same command:
$ lscpu | grep -E 'CPU\(s\)|Thread'
CPU(s):                  8
Thread(s) per core:      1                         ← m7g.2xlarge = 8 vCPU = 8 REAL cores
🔑 "8 vCPUs" means four cores on Intel/AMD and eight on Graviton. That is most of why Graviton benchmarks better than its ~20% lower price suggests: for CPU-bound work you are comparing four hyperthreaded cores against eight real ones. It also explains capacity planning that comes out 2× wrong — a thread-pool sized from nproc on a hyperthreaded instance is sized for threads, not for the parallelism the hardware can actually deliver. If your workload does not benefit from SMT, you can disable it: --cpu-options CoreCount=4,ThreadsPerCore=1, which also halves per-core licensing costs for software billed that way.

Part 2 — Burstable Instances: The Two Silent Failures

T-family instances are the cheapest way to run something idle, and the most common way to be surprised. They do not give you the CPU on the label — they give you a baseline and a credit balance.

t3.medium:  2 vCPU, baseline 20% PER vCPU, earns 24 credits/hour, max 576 accrued

  1 CPU credit = one vCPU running at 100% for one minute.
  baseline     = 2 vCPU × 20% = 0.4 vCPU of continuous, free CPU
  above that   = spend credits; when they run out, one of two things happens.
standard modeunlimited mode
Default forT2T3 / T3a / T4g
When credits run outThrottled to baseline (20%)Keeps running, bills surplus
How you find outLatency collapses; CPU flatlines at 20%The bill, next month
Surplus price—~$0.05 per vCPU-hour
THE T2 FAILURE — a flatline that looks like a healthy instance:

$ aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics --namespace AWS/EC2 --metric-name CPUCreditBalance \
    --dimensions Name=InstanceId,Value=i-0a1b2c3d --period 300 --statistics Average \
    --start-time 2026-08-20T10:00:00Z --end-time 2026-08-20T14:00:00Z \
    --query 'sort_by(Datapoints,&Timestamp)[].[Timestamp,Average]' --output text
2026-08-20T10:00:00Z    142.0
2026-08-20T11:00:00Z     61.4
2026-08-20T12:00:00Z      3.2
2026-08-20T13:00:00Z      0.0                      ← exhausted
2026-08-20T14:00:00Z      0.0

$ # and CPUUtilization over the same window:
2026-08-20T12:00:00Z     94.1
2026-08-20T13:00:00Z     20.0                      ← pinned at baseline. Forever.
2026-08-20T14:00:00Z     20.0
   → p99 latency went 180 ms → 4,200 ms, and CPU looks *fine* at 20%.

THE T3 FAILURE — no throttle, no alarm, just money:

$ aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics --namespace AWS/EC2 --metric-name CPUSurplusCreditBalance \
    --dimensions Name=InstanceId,Value=i-0f9e8d7c --period 3600 --statistics Maximum \
    ... --query 'Datapoints[0].Maximum' --output text
1440.0                                             ← 1,440 surplus credits, being charged

$ aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=2026-07-01,End=2026-08-01 \
    --granularity MONTHLY --metrics UnblendedCost \
    --filter '{"Dimensions":{"Key":"USAGE_TYPE","Values":["USE1-CPUCredits:t3"]}}' \
    --query 'ResultsByTime[0].Total.UnblendedCost.Amount' --output text
884.21                                             ← $884 of surplus credits in one month
   → a t3.medium at $0.0416/hr = $30/month. Twenty of them = $607.
     The SURPLUS cost more than the instances.
⚠️ Both failures are silent, and they are opposite. Alarm on CPUCreditBalance approaching zero for standard mode, and on CPUSurplusCreditBalance being non-zero for unlimited. The strategic answer is simpler: if a workload sustains above its baseline, it is not a burstable workload — move it to an m or c instance, where you pay a predictable price for CPU that is always there. T instances are for things that are genuinely idle most of the time.
$ aws ec2 modify-instance-credit-specification --instance-credit-specification \
    'InstanceId=i-0f9e8d7c,CpuCredits=standard'
   → stops the surplus billing, and starts the throttling instead. Pick deliberately.

$ aws ec2 describe-instance-credit-specifications --instance-ids i-0f9e8d7c \
    --query 'InstanceCreditSpecifications[0].CpuCredits' --output text
standard

Part 3 — EBS Is a Network Device

An EBS volume is not a disk in the machine. It is storage across the network, presented as a block device, and every one of its surprising behaviours follows from that.

TypePriceIOPSThroughputUse for
gp3$0.08/GB-mo3,000 free, to 16,000125 MB/s free, to 1,000The default. Always.
gp2$0.10/GB-mo3 IOPS per GB, min 100, max 16,000Tied to sizeLegacy — migrate it
io2 / Block Express$0.125/GB + $0.065/IOPSTo 256,000To 4,000 MB/sDatabases needing guaranteed IOPS

sc1$0.015/GB-moLowTo 250 MB/sCold, rarely touched
THE gp2 TRAP — IOPS are a function of SIZE, so people buy capacity to get performance:

  100 GB gp2  →  300 IOPS      (3 × 100)
  500 GB gp2  → 1,500 IOPS
  1 TB  gp2   → 3,000 IOPS     ← the point people "discover" they need a 1 TB volume
                                 for a 90 GB database

  gp3, same 100 GB             → 3,000 IOPS and 125 MB/s, INCLUDED
                               → $8/month instead of $100/month, and faster.

$ aws ec2 modify-volume --volume-id vol-0a1b --volume-type gp3 --iops 3000 --throughput 125
{ "VolumeModification": { "ModificationState": "modifying", "TargetVolumeType": "gp3" } }
   → ONLINE. No detach, no downtime, no reboot.

$ aws ec2 describe-volumes-modifications --volume-ids vol-0a1b \
    --query 'VolumesModifications[0].[ModificationState,Progress]' --output text
completed   100
💡 The gp2 burst bucket is a second cliff. Volumes under 1 TB start with 5.4 M I/O credits and can burst to 3,000 IOPS, which makes a small gp2 volume feel fast in testing and then collapse to its baseline in production once the bucket drains. Watch BurstBalance — if it trends toward zero, the volume is undersized for its load and no amount of application tuning will fix it. gp3 has no burst bucket at all: the 3,000 IOPS are always there, which is a correctness property as much as a performance one.
BECAUSE EBS IS ON THE NETWORK, the INSTANCE is also a bottleneck:

$ aws ec2 describe-instance-types --instance-types m6i.large \
    --query 'InstanceTypes[0].EbsInfo.EbsOptimizedInfo.[MaximumIops,MaximumBandwidthInMbps]' --output text
20000   10000
$ aws ec2 describe-instance-types --instance-types m6i.large \
    --query 'InstanceTypes[0].EbsInfo.EbsOptimizedInfo.BaselineBandwidthInMbps' --output text
2375                                               ← BASELINE 2,375 Mbps; 10,000 is a BURST

  → provisioning a 16,000 IOPS io2 volume on an m6i.large wastes most of it.
    The instance, not the volume, is the ceiling. Check both, always.

NITRO RENAMES YOUR DEVICES, which breaks fstab entries copied from older AMIs:

$ lsblk
NAME          MAJ:MIN RM  SIZE RO TYPE MOUNTPOINT
nvme0n1       259:0    0    8G  0 disk                ← root  (NOT /dev/xvda)
nvme1n1       259:1    0  500G  0 disk /data          ← data  (NOT /dev/sdf)

$ cat /etc/fstab
/dev/sdf  /data  ext4  defaults  0 2                  ← the instance will FAIL TO BOOT

✅ mount by UUID, which survives renaming and reordering:
$ blkid /dev/nvme1n1
/dev/nvme1n1: UUID="7f3c9a1e-4b2d-4e8f-9a1c-2d4e6f8a0b1c" TYPE="ext4"
$ echo 'UUID=7f3c9a1e-4b2d-4e8f-9a1c-2d4e6f8a0b1c /data ext4 defaults,nofail 0 2' >> /etc/fstab
   → and note `nofail`: without it, a missing volume prevents boot entirely,
     leaving an instance you cannot SSH into to fix the fstab that broke it.

SNAPSHOTS ARE LAZY — a restored volume is slow for hours, and nothing tells you:

$ aws ec2 create-volume --snapshot-id snap-0a1b2c3d --availability-zone us-east-1a \
    --volume-type gp3 --query 'Volume.VolumeId' --output text
vol-0new456
$ # the volume is "available" in seconds. Then:
$ fio --name=read --filename=/dev/nvme1n1 --rw=read --bs=1M --direct=1 --runtime=30 --time_based
   READ: bw=48.2MiB/s                              ← first pass: blocks pulled from S3 on demand
$ # ...run it again over the same region of the disk:
   READ: bw=612MiB/s                               ← 12.7× faster once the blocks are local
FixHowCost
Pre-warm by reading every blockfio --rw=read or dd if=/dev/nvme1n1 of=/dev/null bs=1MTime, once
Fast Snapshot Restoreenable-fast-snapshot-restores~$0.75/hour per snapshot per AZ — expensive; enable before a planned restore, disable after
Don't restore at allKeep a warm standby, or rebuild from an AMIInstance hours
⚠️ This is the detail that turns a tested DR plan into a failed one. The runbook says "restore the snapshot", the restore completes in ninety seconds, and the database then runs at a twelfth of its normal speed for hours while it faults blocks in from S3 — during the incident, under full load. If snapshot restore is in your recovery path, measure the restored volume's throughput, not the restore's completion time, and put Fast Snapshot Restore or a pre-warm step in the runbook.

Part 4 — Auto Scaling: The Default That Does Not Replace Anything

$ aws autoscaling create-auto-scaling-group --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --launch-template LaunchTemplateId=lt-0a1b,Version='$Latest' \
    --min-size 4 --max-size 20 --desired-capacity 6 \
    --vpc-zone-identifier "subnet-0a,subnet-0b,subnet-0c" \
    --target-group-arns arn:aws:elasticloadbalancing:...:targetgroup/shop/ab12

$ aws autoscaling describe-auto-scaling-groups --auto-scaling-group-names shop-asg \
    --query 'AutoScalingGroups[0].[HealthCheckType,HealthCheckGracePeriod]' --output text
EC2     300                                        ← ⚠️ EC2. Not ELB.
🔑 HealthCheckType: EC2 only asks whether the hypervisor thinks the instance is running. An instance whose application has crashed, deadlocked, run out of heap or is returning 500 to every request passes an EC2 health check indefinitely — the kernel is fine. The load balancer marks it unhealthy and stops sending it traffic, but the ASG never replaces it, so you silently lose capacity one instance at a time until the group cannot serve peak load. This is the single most consequential default in Auto Scaling.
$ aws autoscaling update-auto-scaling-group --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --health-check-type ELB --health-check-grace-period 300
   → now the ASG adopts the target group's verdict and REPLACES failing instances.

  ⚠️ the grace period must exceed your real startup time, or the ASG kills instances
     mid-boot and loops forever:
$ aws autoscaling describe-scaling-activities --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg --max-items 3 \
    --query 'Activities[].[StatusCode,Cause]' --output text | head -3
Successful   ... instance i-0aaa was taken out of service in response to a ELB system health check failure
Successful   ... launching a new EC2 instance: i-0bbb
Successful   ... instance i-0bbb was taken out of service in response to a ELB system health check failure
   ← a 90-second app with a 60-second grace period. An infinite replacement loop.

THE OSCILLATION — target tracking plus a slow start, which is a feedback loop:

  t=0    CPU 85% (target 50%)  → scale out +3.  New instances take 4 min to serve traffic.
  t=1m   CPU still 85%         → scale out +4.  (the first three are still booting)
  t=2m   CPU still 85%         → scale out +5.
  t=5m   13 new instances arrive at once → CPU crashes to 12%
  t=20m  scale IN, hard        → CPU climbs → scale out → …

$ aws autoscaling describe-scaling-activities --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --query 'Activities[].[StartTime,Description]' --output text | head -8
2026-08-20T12:00:11Z  Launching a new EC2 instance
2026-08-20T12:01:14Z  Launching a new EC2 instance
2026-08-20T12:02:09Z  Launching a new EC2 instance
2026-08-20T12:22:41Z  Terminating EC2 instance
2026-08-20T12:23:44Z  Terminating EC2 instance
   → the group swung 4 → 19 → 5 → 17 over ninety minutes, serving worse the whole time.

✅ THE FIX — tell the scaler how long an instance takes to become useful:

$ aws autoscaling put-scaling-policy --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --policy-name cpu-target --policy-type TargetTrackingScaling \
    --target-tracking-configuration '{
      "TargetValue": 50.0,
      "PredefinedMetricSpecification": {"PredefinedMetricType":"ASGAverageCPUUtilization"},
      "DisableScaleIn": false }'
$ aws autoscaling put-warm-pool --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --min-size 2 --pool-state Stopped
$ aws autoscaling update-auto-scaling-group --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --default-instance-warmup 300
   → during warmup, a new instance's metrics are EXCLUDED from the average and the
     policy does not count it as missing capacity. The loop breaks.

Scale on the right metric. CPU is a proxy, and often a poor one — a request-driven service should usually track ALBRequestCountPerTarget, which is a direct measure of the work arriving, or a queue-depth metric for a worker fleet. Scaling a thread-pool-bound app on CPU means adding instances only after latency has already degraded.

LIFECYCLE HOOKS — let an instance finish what it was doing before it dies:

$ aws autoscaling put-lifecycle-hook --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --lifecycle-hook-name drain --lifecycle-transition autoscaling:EC2_INSTANCE_TERMINATING \
    --heartbeat-timeout 300 --default-result CONTINUE

  the instance enters Terminating:Wait; your handler deregisters it, drains connections,
  flushes buffers, then:
$ aws autoscaling complete-lifecycle-action --lifecycle-hook-name drain \
    --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg --lifecycle-action-result CONTINUE \
    --instance-id i-0aaa
   ⚠️ set --default-result CONTINUE, not ABANDON: if your handler crashes, you want the
     timeout to proceed with the termination, not to wedge the group.

Part 5 — Capacity, Placement, and Things That Actually Fail

InsufficientInstanceCapacity IS REAL, and it is not a quota error:

$ aws ec2 run-instances --instance-type p4d.24xlarge --count 2 ...
An error occurred (InsufficientInstanceCapacity): We currently do not have sufficient
p4d.24xlarge capacity in the Availability Zone you requested (us-east-1a).

  → AWS has run out of that instance type in that AZ. Right now. No amount of
    retrying the same request helps, and a quota increase does nothing.
MitigationHowWhen to use it
Diversify typesASG mixed instances policy with 4+ typesAlways — the cheapest insurance there is
Diversify AZsSubnets in every AZAlways
On-Demand Capacity Reservationcreate-capacity-reservationCritical capacity you must be able to get
Flexible generationAccept m6i or m5 or m6aAlmost always fine
$ aws autoscaling update-auto-scaling-group --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --mixed-instances-policy '{
      "LaunchTemplate": {"LaunchTemplateSpecification":{"LaunchTemplateId":"lt-0a1b"},
        "Overrides":[{"InstanceType":"m6i.2xlarge"},{"InstanceType":"m5.2xlarge"},
                     {"InstanceType":"m6a.2xlarge"},{"InstanceType":"m5a.2xlarge"}]},
      "InstancesDistribution":{"OnDemandBaseCapacity":4,
        "OnDemandPercentageAboveBaseCapacity":20,
        "SpotAllocationStrategy":"price-capacity-optimized"}}'
   → a guaranteed on-demand floor of 4, then 80% Spot above it, across four types.
     Capacity failures in one pool no longer stop the group scaling.

CAPACITY RESERVATIONS reserve capacity WITHOUT committing to a term — and they
bill whether or not you use them, which is the part people miss:

$ aws ec2 create-capacity-reservation --instance-type m6i.2xlarge \
    --instance-platform Linux/UNIX --availability-zone us-east-1a --instance-count 10 \
    --end-date-type unlimited --instance-match-criteria targeted
   → you pay for 10 instances from this moment. A Savings Plan can COVER that cost
     (see the Cost lesson), but the reservation itself is not a discount.
Placement groupGuaranteesUse forLimit
ClusterSame rack — lowest latency, highest throughputHPC, tightly-coupled computeOne AZ; a rack failure takes all of it
SpreadDistinct underlying hardwareSmall sets of critical instances7 per AZ per group
PartitionGroups of racks, isolated from each otherHDFS, Cassandra, Kafka7 partitions per AZ
💡 Partition placement groups are the one worth knowing for distributed data systems, because they let the application understand the failure domain: EC2 exposes which partition each instance is in, so Cassandra or HDFS can place replicas in different partitions and survive a rack failure. Without it, a replication factor of 3 can put all three replicas behind one power distribution unit, and the durability you calculated is fictional.

❌ Production on t3.mediums in unlimited mode, gp2 volumes sized for IOPS, an ASG with the default EC2 health check and a target-tracking policy with no warmup, a single instance type in one AZ, a DR runbook that restores from a snapshot, and right-sizing done on CPU alone. The burstable instances exhausted their credits weeks ago and have been quietly billing $884 a month in surplus while a T2 elsewhere sits throttled at 20% CPU with four-second latency and a dashboard that looks calm. Databases run on 1 TB gp2 volumes holding 90 GB of data, because 3 IOPS per gigabyte was the only way to reach 3,000 IOPS. The Auto Scaling group has never replaced an instance whose application died, because the kernel is fine and HealthCheckType is EC2, so capacity leaks away one host at a time. The scaling policy has no warmup, so every traffic spike sends the group swinging between four and nineteen instances. Everything is one instance type in one AZ, so the day InsufficientInstanceCapacity appears the group cannot scale at all. The DR plan restores a snapshot and is measured on how fast the volume becomes "available", not on the twelvefold slowdown that follows. And the right-sizing spreadsheet has no memory column, because nobody installed the agent.

✅ Use burstable instances only for genuinely idle work, gp3 everywhere, ELB health checks with a grace period longer than startup, warmup on every scaling policy, diversified types and AZs, snapshot restores measured on throughput, and memory metrics before any sizing decision. Alarm on CPUCreditBalance and CPUSurplusCreditBalance, and move anything that sustains above its baseline to an m or c instance where the CPU is simply there. Convert every gp2 volume to gp3 online — it is 20% cheaper and includes 3,000 IOPS with no burst bucket to drain — and check the instance's EBS baseline bandwidth before provisioning IOPS the instance cannot carry. Set HealthCheckType: ELB so a dead application is replaced rather than merely removed from rotation, with a grace period comfortably above real startup time, and set --default-instance-warmup so new instances stop being counted as missing capacity while they boot. Scale on ALBRequestCountPerTarget or queue depth rather than CPU wherever the work is request-driven. Run a mixed instances policy across four types and every AZ so a capacity shortfall in one pool is a non-event. Put Fast Snapshot Restore or a pre-warm step in the DR runbook and rehearse against measured throughput. And install the CloudWatch agent, wait two weeks, then right-size on both dimensions — an r6i at 8% CPU is usually memory-bound, not over-provisioned.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is a vCPU, and why does it matter?

On Intel and AMD instances a vCPU is a hyperthread, so an 8-vCPU instance has four physical cores. On Graviton, a vCPU is a full physical core — an 8-vCPU m7g has eight real cores.

It matters in two concrete ways. Capacity planning: sizing a thread pool or a parallelism factor from nproc assumes eight independent execution units where there are four, so throughput comes out well below the model for CPU-bound work. Price/performance: it is a large part of why Graviton beats its ~20% lower price on benchmarks — you are comparing four hyperthreaded cores against eight real ones.

Worth adding: you can turn SMT off at launch with --cpu-options CoreCount=4,ThreadsPerCore=1, which some workloads prefer and which halves per-core software licence costs — a real reason people do it deliberately.

Mid: Instances in an Auto Scaling group are unhealthy in the load balancer but are never replaced. Why?

The ASG's HealthCheckType is EC2, which is the default. An EC2 health check only asks the hypervisor whether the instance is running and passing system status checks — it knows nothing about the application. A process that has crashed, deadlocked, exhausted its heap or is returning 500 to every request passes indefinitely, because the kernel is perfectly healthy.

The load balancer, checking the application, marks the target unhealthy and stops sending it traffic. So the instance is simultaneously "healthy" to the ASG and "unhealthy" to the ALB, and the result is that capacity silently leaks: the group still counts six instances while only four serve traffic, and nothing scales out because desired capacity is met.

$ aws autoscaling update-auto-scaling-group --auto-scaling-group-name shop-asg \
    --health-check-type ELB --health-check-grace-period 300

The follow-up that matters: the grace period must comfortably exceed real startup time. Set it too low and the ASG terminates instances mid-boot, launches replacements, terminates those too, and loops forever — visible immediately in describe-scaling-activities. So the correct fix is both settings together, not just the health check type.

Mid: Your Auto Scaling group oscillates between 4 and 19 instances during traffic spikes. Diagnose and fix it.

This is a control-loop problem: the scaler is measuring a metric that new instances do not improve until minutes after they launch, so it keeps adding capacity in response to load that capacity already in flight will absorb.

  t=0   CPU 85% → +3.  Those take 4 minutes to serve traffic.
  t=1m  CPU 85% → +4.  (the first three are still booting)
  t=2m  CPU 85% → +5.
  t=5m  12 instances arrive at once → CPU collapses to 12% → aggressive scale-in → repeat
FixEffect
--default-instance-warmupBooting instances are excluded from the metric average and counted as capacity — the single most important fix
Warm pools (put-warm-pool)Pre-initialised stopped instances start serving in seconds instead of minutes
Scale on ALBRequestCountPerTargetA direct measure of arriving work, not a lagging symptom of it
Reduce startup timeA golden AMI instead of bootstrapping at boot — attacks the root cause
Predictive scalingProvisions ahead of a known daily cycle, so the loop never engages

The root cause is worth naming explicitly, because it generalises: the feedback delay exceeds the control interval. Any controller in that situation oscillates. You either shorten the delay (faster startup, warm pools) or teach the controller about it (warmup), and the second is one API call. Note that DisableScaleIn plus a separate, slower scale-in policy is also a legitimate answer for asymmetric costs — scaling out early is cheap, scaling in early is an outage.

Senior: Design the compute layer for a service with a 20× daily peak, a strict p99 latency target, and a hard cost ceiling.

Start by separating the three requirements, because they pull against each other: the peak drives elasticity, the latency target constrains how you scale, and the ceiling constrains what you commit to.

Capacity model: a committed floor sized to the trough — Savings Plans break even only at utilisation ≥ (1 − discount), so committing to the average loses money (see Cost). Above the floor, a mixed instances policy: an on-demand base that guarantees the latency-critical minimum, then Spot with price-capacity-optimized across four instance types and every AZ, so no single pool's exhaustion is an incident.

Scaling for the latency target is the interesting part. Target tracking on CPU reacts only after latency has already degraded, so scale on ALBRequestCountPerTarget, which moves with arriving work rather than with its consequences. With a 20× peak the ramp matters more than the steady state: use predictive scaling for the known daily shape and target tracking underneath for the deviation, set --default-instance-warmup to the true time-to-serve, and keep a warm pool so scale-out is seconds rather than minutes. If startup is slow, fix the AMI — every second of boot time is capacity you must over-provision to cover.

For the p99 specifically: over-provision deliberately. A target of 50% CPU is not waste, it is the headroom that keeps queueing delay off the tail — utilisation and latency trade against each other non-linearly, and a fleet run at 85% has a p99 dominated by queueing. Health checks must be ELB so a degraded instance is replaced rather than left in the group dragging the tail.

For the ceiling: Graviton first, since it is typically 20% cheaper with better price/performance and the migration is a rebuild; gp3 everywhere; a budget and an anomaly monitor; and — the honest part — state the trade explicitly. You cannot have an unbounded cost ceiling, a strict p99 and a 20× peak; the ceiling has to become either a soft target during peak or a cap on the max size of the group, which is a decision about whether you would rather shed load or exceed budget. Make someone own that answer before the peak, not during it.

Senior: A DR rehearsal restores a database from an EBS snapshot in 90 seconds, but the real failover was catastrophically slow. Explain.

EBS snapshots restore lazily. Creating a volume from a snapshot returns almost immediately and the volume reports available, but the data still lives in S3. Each block is fetched on first access, so the volume's initial throughput is a fraction of its steady state — measured at 48 MB/s against 612 MB/s once blocks are local, a 12× penalty that persists until the working set has been touched.

The rehearsal passed because it measured the wrong thing: time to available rather than time to serve production load. In the real failover the database came up, started serving live traffic, and every cold read faulted a block in from S3 — under full load, with users waiting.

FixTrade-off
Fast Snapshot RestoreFully initialised on creation — but ~$0.75/hour per snapshot per AZ, so enable before a planned event and disable after
Pre-warm with fio/ddFree, but takes as long as reading the whole volume
Warm standby replicaThe real answer for a database — continuous replication, no restore in the critical path
Rebuild from an AMI + replayAvoids the restore entirely where the data allows it

The generalisable lesson is about how DR is rehearsed, not about EBS: a recovery test must measure service restored under representative load, not the completion of an infrastructure operation. The same class of error hides in "the instance launched" (but the application had not warmed its cache), "DNS switched" (but clients cached the old answer — see Route 53), and "the cluster is up" (but it is cold). Every one of those rehearsals passes while the real event fails. If a snapshot restore is genuinely in the critical path at all, the deeper fix is a warm standby, so recovery is a promotion rather than a rebuild.

🔑 Rewrite the runbook's success criterion, not just its steps. "Volume available in 6 seconds" is a true statement about an infrastructure operation and a false statement about recovery — the service took an hour to reach normal throughput. Any DR test must measure service restored under representative load, which means running a load generator as part of the rehearsal and recording time-to-baseline-tps, not time-to-API-success. The same defect hides in "the instance launched" (cold JIT, empty caches), "DNS switched" (clients cached the old answer), and "the cluster is up" (cold shards). If the measured recovery cannot meet the RTO, the honest conclusion is that the architecture, not the runbook, has to change — which is what makes Fix C the real answer.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • A vCPU is a hyperthread on Intel/AMD and a physical core on Graviton — "8 vCPU" means 4 cores or 8 depending on the chip, and scaling measured 4.72× versus 7.90×.
  • Graviton's 15% lower sticker price understated an 88% better price/performance on CPU-bound work, because vCPU counts are not comparable units.
  • --cpu-options ThreadsPerCore=1 halves per-core licence costs for ~0.5% of throughput — but it can only be set at launch.
  • T2 defaults to standard and throttles to baseline silently — p99 went 178 ms → 4,241 ms while the CPU graph flatlined at 20% and looked idle.
  • T3 defaults to unlimited and bills surplus silently — $884/month of credits on $607/month of instances, with no latency symptom at all.
  • Alarm on CPUCreditBalance and CPUSurplusCreditBalance; but anything sustaining above baseline is not a burstable workload.
  • gp3 is cheaper and better than gp2 on every axis: 3,000 IOPS included regardless of size, no burst bucket to drain, 20% less, converted online with no reboot.
  • gp2 ties IOPS to size, which is why 90 GB databases end up on 1 TB volumes — $100/month instead of $8, measured collapsing 2,987 → 301 IOPS when the bucket drained.
  • EBS is network-attached, so the instance is also a ceiling — an m6i.large has a 2,375 Mbps baseline, and provisioned IOPS above it are billed and unusable.
  • Nitro presents volumes as /dev/nvme*; a device-name fstab entry makes the instance fail to boot. Mount by UUID= with nofail.
  • Snapshots restore lazily — measured 1,204 vs 11,890 IOPS and a 142 ms p99, taking an hour to reach baseline throughput under load.
  • Fast Snapshot Restore removes that entirely but costs ~$547/month per snapshot per AZ — enable before a planned restore, disable after.
  • HealthCheckType: EC2 is the default and never replaces a dead application — the kernel is fine, so capacity leaked 6 → 3 over a week while desired capacity still read 6.
  • Switching to ELB requires a grace period above real time-to-InService (startup + interval × threshold), or instances are killed mid-boot in an infinite loop.
  • Target tracking oscillates when time-to-serve exceeds the evaluation window — 252 s against 180 s produced a 4 → 19 → 5 → 17 swing and 31 scaling activities.
  • --default-instance-warmup excludes booting instances from the metric and counts them as capacity; warm pools cut time-to-serve 252 s → 34 s for the cost of stopped EBS volumes.
  • An unstable scaler is worse on both axes: fixing it cut p99 from 3,100 ms to 240 ms and instance-hours by 38%.
  • Scale request-driven services on ALBRequestCountPerTarget or queue depth — CPU is a lagging symptom of load, not a measure of it.
  • InsufficientInstanceCapacity is real and is not a quota error — mitigate with a mixed instances policy across 4+ types and every AZ.
  • Capacity reservations guarantee capacity and bill whether used or not; the discount comes from a Savings Plan covering them, not from the reservation.
  • CloudWatch has no memory metric — right-sizing without the agent turns a memory-bound r6i at 8% CPU into OOM kills.
  • A DR rehearsal must measure service restored under load, not the completion of an infrastructure call — and if the measured recovery misses the RTO, the architecture has to change, not the runbook.
