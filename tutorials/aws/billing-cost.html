Cost: Pricing Models & Data Transfer Foundations

What you'll master: the four dimensions AWS actually bills — compute-time, storage-months, requests and movement — and why the fourth is the one nobody models until it appears; the compute pricing ladder (On-Demand, Spot, Reserved, Savings Plans) and the single arithmetic rule that tells you whether a commitment is worth making — you must use (1 − discount) of what you committed just to break even; data transfer priced per direction, where two chatty services in different Availability Zones quietly cost $400 a month with no line item naming them, and a NAT Gateway charges $180 a month to reach S3 for free; storage classes whose minimum durations, minimum billable object sizes and per-object transition fees mean a "cheaper" tier can cost more — with a lifecycle rule measured at a 104-month payback; Lambda memory tuning where more memory is both faster and cheaper; the request-priced services that are trivial until they are your largest line item; and the attribution machinery — cost allocation tags that are not retroactive, budgets, anomaly detection, and the three Cost Explorer queries that find any regression. Prerequisites: Shared Responsibility, Accounts & Regions and IAM: Identities, Policies & Evaluation.

Part 1 — What You Are Actually Billed For

Almost every AWS charge is one of four shapes. Learning to classify a service by shape tells you where its bill will come from before you have run it for a month.

ShapeUnitExamplesWhat makes it explode
TimePer second or hour, whether idle or notEC2, RDS, NAT Gateway, ALB, Interface EndpointsThings left running; things duplicated per AZ
Capacity-timePer GB-month, prorated hourlyS3, EBS, snapshots, log retentionNothing ever deleted; snapshots nobody owns
RequestsPer million calls or per 1,000 objectsLambda, API Gateway, S3 PUT/GET, DynamoDB, KMSA retry loop; a chatty client; small objects
MovementPer GB, per directionInternet egress, cross-AZ, cross-Region, NAT processingTopology — and it is invisible in your code
The same 1 TB, four different bills (us-east-1, list price, illustrative):

  stored in S3 Standard for a month     1,000 GB × $0.023        =  $23.00
  stored on EBS gp3 for a month         1,000 GB × $0.08         =  $80.00
  sent to the internet                  1,000 GB × $0.09         =  $90.00
  sent across an Availability Zone      1,000 GB × $0.01 × 2     =  $20.00
  pulled through a NAT Gateway          1,000 GB × $0.045        =  $45.00  (plus the transfer)
🔑 Moving a terabyte once costs more than storing it for a month. That single comparison reorders most cost intuitions. Engineers instinctively optimise storage — it is the number they can see in the console — while transfer, which is a property of architecture and appears nowhere in the code, is usually the larger and faster-growing line.

Three units trip people up constantly:

TrapReality
GB vs GiBStorage and transfer are billed in GB (10⁹); instance memory is quoted in GiB (2³⁰). A "1,024 GB" bill is not 1 TiB.
"per GB-month"Prorated hourly. 100 GB for 10 days is billed as ≈32.9 GB-months' worth, not 100.
"per hour"EC2 Linux and RDS bill per second (60-second minimum); Windows and some marketplace AMIs bill per hour, rounded up.
Where the bill actually lives — usage types, not services:

$ aws ce get-cost-and-usage \
    --time-period Start=2026-07-01,End=2026-08-01 \
    --granularity MONTHLY --metrics UnblendedCost \
    --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=USAGE_TYPE \
    --query 'ResultsByTime[0].Groups[?Metrics.UnblendedCost.Amount > `500`]' \
    --output table
|  USE1-NatGateway-Bytes        |   4821.55  |   ← NAT processing, not "EC2"
|  USE1-DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes |  3980.12 |  ← cross-AZ chatter
|  USE1-BoxUsage:m6i.2xlarge    |   3102.44  |
|  USE1-TimedStorage-ByteHrs    |   1877.03  |
|  DataTransfer-Out-Bytes       |   1204.90  |   ← internet egress

Grouping by service tells you "EC2 is expensive", which you already knew. Grouping by usage type tells you which behaviour is expensive, and that is actionable. The USE1- prefix is the Region; charges without a Region prefix are global or egress.

💡 The ce get-cost-and-usage API itself costs $0.01 per paginated request. That is nothing for investigation, and genuinely surprising if you put it in a dashboard that refreshes every minute for forty teams.

Part 2 — The Compute Pricing Ladder

ModelTypical discountCommitmentRisk you accept
On-Demand—NoneNone; you pay the sticker price
Spotup to ~90%NoneTwo minutes' notice, then reclaimed
Reserved Instances~40–60%1 or 3 years, specific family/RegionLocked to a shape you may outgrow
Savings Plans~27% (1 yr) to ~50%+ (3 yr)1 or 3 years of $/hour spendYou pay the commitment whether you use it or not
Graviton (ARM)~20% cheaper, better price/perfNone — it is just a different instanceRebuild for arm64

The distinction that matters most, and that interviews probe: a Savings Plan is not a discount on instances, it is a commitment to spend. You promise "$10 per hour of compute for a year"; AWS applies the discounted rate to the first $10/hour of eligible usage, every hour. Usage above the commitment is billed On-Demand. Usage below it is still billed — the commitment is charged in full, every hour, used or not.

THE ONLY ARITHMETIC YOU NEED:

  discount d, commitment C ($/hr), actual eligible usage U ($/hr at on-demand rates)

  cost with a plan     = C                (if U ≥ C, plus (U − C) on-demand)
  cost without a plan  = U

  break-even:  C = U × (1 − d)
               → you must actually use (1 − d) of what you commit.

  d = 27%  →  use ≥ 73% of the commitment, or you have lost money.
  d = 50%  →  use ≥ 50%.

Worked, on a fleet whose on-demand compute averages $14.00/hr but troughs at $6.00/hr:

  ❌ commit to the average, $14.00/hr at 27% off:
       paid            = $14.00/hr                       = $10,220 / month
       on-demand cost of what ran (avg 9.8 $/hr eligible) = $ 7,154 / month
       → the plan COST $3,066/month more than doing nothing.

  ✅ commit to the trough, $6.00/hr at 27% off:
       plan            $6.00/hr                          = $ 4,380 / month
       remainder on-demand (9.8 − 6.0/0.73 = 1.58 $/hr)  = $ 1,153 / month
                                                    total  $ 5,533 / month
       → 23% cheaper than pure on-demand, with zero waste.
🔑 Commit to your floor, never your average. A Savings Plan is a put option you have already exercised: the downside is unbounded if usage falls, and the upside is capped at the discount. Size it against the lowest hour of a typical week, then add a second, smaller plan later if the floor rises. Two stacked plans are fine; an oversized one is unwindable only by waiting out the term — Savings Plans cannot be sold, and only Standard RIs have a resale marketplace.
QuestionReserved InstanceSavings Plan
What is committed?A specific instance shapeA dollar amount per hour
Covers Fargate / Lambda?❌✅ (Compute SP)
Survives a family change?Convertible RIs only✅ (Compute SP, any family/Region/OS)
Resellable?Standard RIs, on the marketplace❌ never
Best forRDS, ElastiCache, Redshift (no SP exists)EC2, Fargate, Lambda

Spot is the opposite trade: no commitment, the deepest discount, and the instance can be reclaimed with a two-minute warning. It is correct for anything that can be interrupted and retried — batch, CI runners, stateless web tiers behind a load balancer, Kubernetes nodes in a separate node group (see Kubernetes disruptions, where the same reclaim event is what podFailurePolicy is for). It is wrong for anything holding state that cannot be rebuilt in two minutes.

Diversify across instance types and AZs — a single-type Spot request is a single point of eviction:

$ aws ec2 create-fleet --cli-input-json file://fleet.json
  "TargetCapacitySpecification": { "TotalTargetCapacity": 40,
      "OnDemandTargetCapacity": 8,          ← a floor that cannot be reclaimed
      "DefaultTargetCapacityType": "spot" },
  "SpotOptions": { "AllocationStrategy": "price-capacity-optimized" },
  "LaunchTemplateConfigs": [{ "Overrides": [
      {"InstanceType":"m6i.2xlarge"}, {"InstanceType":"m5.2xlarge"},
      {"InstanceType":"m6a.2xlarge"}, {"InstanceType":"m5a.2xlarge"} ]}]

$ aws ec2 describe-spot-price-history --instance-types m6i.2xlarge \
    --product-descriptions "Linux/UNIX" --max-items 1 \
    --query 'SpotPriceHistory[0].[SpotPrice,AvailabilityZone]' --output text
0.1523   us-east-1c            ← vs $0.3840 on-demand: 60% off, today
⚠️ price-capacity-optimized beats lowest-price in practice: the absolute cheapest pool is cheapest because nobody wants it, which usually means it is small and about to be reclaimed. Paying 3% more for a deep pool trades a rounding error for a large drop in interruption rate.

Part 3 — Data Transfer: The Line Item Nobody Models

This is the section that separates engineers who have owned an AWS bill from those who have not. Transfer pricing is directional, and the direction that costs money is rarely the one people assume.

THE CLASSIC #1 — a NAT Gateway charging you to reach a free destination.

Three private subnets, one NAT Gateway each, services pulling 4 TB/month from S3:

  NAT hourly       3 × 730 h × $0.045              =  $  98.55
  NAT processing   4,000 GB × $0.045               =  $ 180.00
  S3 transfer      in-Region, S3 to EC2            =  $   0.00
                                              total  $ 278.55 / month

$ aws ec2 create-vpc-endpoint --vpc-id vpc-0ab1 --service-name com.amazonaws.us-east-1.s3 \
    --vpc-endpoint-type Gateway --route-table-ids rtb-01 rtb-02 rtb-03
{ "VpcEndpoint": { "VpcEndpointId": "vpce-0f3c9a1e", "State": "available" } }

The next month:
  NAT processing   112 GB × $0.045                 =  $   5.04   ← only non-S3 traffic
  Gateway endpoint                                 =  $   0.00
                                        saved         $ 175 / month, for one route-table entry.
🔑 A Gateway Endpoint for S3 and DynamoDB is free and should exist in every VPC from the day it is created. There is no cost argument against it and no operational downside; it is a prefix-list route, not a device. If your account has private subnets and no S3 gateway endpoint, you are paying NAT processing for traffic that never needed to leave the Region.
THE CLASSIC #2 — cross-AZ chatter, which no line item will name for you.

Two services, 20 TB/month between them, spread across 3 AZs for availability:

  ~2/3 of requests land in a different AZ from the caller
  cross-AZ:  20,000 GB × $0.01 (out) + $0.01 (in)  =  $ 400.00 / month

The bill says only:   USE1-DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes    $400.00
It does NOT say:      "checkout-service ⇄ inventory-service"

Finding the pair — VPC Flow Logs in Athena, joined against the AZ of each ENI:

SELECT src.az AS from_az, dst.az AS to_az,
       ROUND(SUM(f.bytes) / 1e9, 1) AS gb
FROM   flow_logs f
JOIN   eni_az src ON f.srcaddr = src.private_ip
JOIN   eni_az dst ON f.dstaddr = dst.private_ip
WHERE  f.day >= '2026/07/01' AND src.az <> dst.az
GROUP  BY 1, 2 ORDER BY gb DESC LIMIT 5;

 from_az     to_az       gb
 us-east-1a  us-east-1b  6412.8
 us-east-1b  us-east-1a  6390.2
 us-east-1a  us-east-1c  3401.5
 ...                                  ← 20 TB, now with names attached

The fixes, in order of how much architecture they cost you:

FixEffectWhat you give up
Cache the callee's responsesRemoves the request entirelyStaleness
Topology-aware routing (trafficDistribution: PreferSameZone on a Kubernetes Service, or an AZ-local ALB target group)Keeps traffic in-AZ when a healthy local endpoint existsUneven load; needs replicas in every AZ
Co-locate the two services in one AZTransfer → $0Availability. Usually the wrong trade.
Compress the payloadLinear reductionCPU
Merge the two servicesTransfer → $0Independent deployment
⚠️ Do not solve a $400/month transfer bill by collapsing into one AZ. An AZ is the failure boundary you bought multi-AZ to survive; $400 is roughly two hours of an engineer's time, and a zonal outage is not. Cache first, route topologically second, and treat co-location as a last resort for genuinely non-critical, high-volume paths only.

Glacier Deep Archive$0.00099$0.02/GB, 12 h wait180 days40 KB + 32 KB overhead

Four separate mechanisms can make a colder tier more expensive, and a lifecycle rule written from the storage column alone will hit all four:

1. MINIMUM DURATION — delete a Standard-IA object on day 3 and you are billed 30 days.
2. MINIMUM BILLABLE SIZE — a 40 KB object in Standard-IA is billed as 128 KB.
3. PER-OBJECT TRANSITION FEE — $0.01 per 1,000 objects to IA / Glacier IR,
                               $0.05 per 1,000 to Glacier Flexible / Deep Archive.
4. RETRIEVAL FEE — reading it back costs money that Standard does not charge.

MEASURED: 50 million log objects averaging 40 KB. Total 2,000 GB.

  Today, S3 Standard:            2,000 GB × $0.023            = $ 46.00 / month

  ❌ "Move to Standard-IA, it's half the price":
       billed size  50,000,000 × 128 KB = 6,400 GB
       storage      6,400 GB × $0.0125                        = $ 80.00 / month
       transition   50,000 thousands × $0.01                  = $500.00 once
       → 74% MORE per month, plus $500 to get there.

  ❌ "Then Glacier Flexible, it's 6x cheaper":
       data         2,000 GB × $0.0036                        = $  7.20
       32 KB overhead per object at Glacier rate  1,600 GB    = $  5.76
       8  KB overhead per object at Standard rate   400 GB    = $  9.20
                                                       total    $ 22.16 / month
       transition   50,000 thousands × $0.05                  = $2,500.00 once
       saving $23.84/month  →  PAYBACK: 104 MONTHS.
🔑 Below roughly 128 KB, object count dominates object size, and no storage class will help you. The fix is not a lifecycle rule; it is to stop creating 50 million tiny objects — aggregate the day's logs into hourly Parquet files, which collapses 50 M objects into ~700, cuts storage to ~$4/month after compression, and makes them queryable by Athena as a side effect. Run the arithmetic per object count before writing any lifecycle rule.
Get the object count and size distribution BEFORE deciding — S3 Storage Lens or a one-liner:

Part 5 — Request-Priced Services, and the Lambda Memory Paradox

Serverless and managed services are priced per request, which feels free at prototype scale and occasionally becomes the largest line on the bill. Lambda in particular has a pricing shape that inverts the usual intuition.

Lambda is billed in GB-SECONDS:   memory (GB) × duration (s) × $0.0000166667
                                  + $0.20 per 1M invocations

CPU is allocated PROPORTIONALLY to memory. You do not buy memory; you buy a machine.
A function at 1,769 MB gets one full vCPU; at 128 MB it gets ~7% of one.

MEASURED — the same CPU-bound function, 1,000,000 invocations/month:

  memory     duration    GB-seconds     compute      + requests    TOTAL
  128 MB     11,800 ms   1.475          $24.58        $0.20        $24.78
  256 MB      5,600 ms   1.400          $23.33        $0.20        $23.53
  512 MB      2,600 ms   1.300          $21.67        $0.20        $21.87
  1024 MB     1,200 ms   1.200          $20.00        $0.20        $20.20   ← cheapest
  1769 MB       950 ms   1.641          $27.35        $0.20        $27.55
  3008 MB       920 ms   2.703          $45.04        $0.20        $45.24

  1024 MB is BOTH the cheapest option AND ~10× faster than 128 MB.
🔑 Reducing Lambda memory to save money usually costs money. Below the point where the function stops being CPU-starved, duration grows faster than memory shrinks, so GB-seconds go up. Above the point where extra CPU no longer helps, duration flattens while memory keeps being billed, so cost climbs again. The curve is U-shaped; sweep it once with Power Tuning or a loop, and pin the minimum. Re-measure after any dependency upgrade that changes the CPU profile.

    because the charge appears under "CloudWatch", not under the service.

$ aws logs put-retention-policy --log-group-name /aws/lambda/checkout --retention-in-days 14
   → retention caps STORAGE. It does not reduce INGESTION, which is the larger number.
     Ingestion is only fixed by logging less: raise the level, sample, or drop
     health-check lines at the agent.

DynamoDB on-demand vs provisioned — the break-even is a utilisation percentage:

  1 WCU sustained for a month = 3,600 × 730 = 2,628,000 writes
  provisioned:  $0.00065 / WCU-hr × 730                = $0.4745
  on-demand:    2.628 M × $1.25 / M                    = $3.2850
                                                  ratio  6.92×

  → on-demand is cheaper below ≈ 14% sustained utilisation, and 7× worse at 100%.

  Spiky, unpredictable, or new         → on-demand.
  Steady baseline you can predict      → provisioned + auto scaling.
  Both                                 → provisioned floor, and let auto scaling ride the peaks.

Part 6 — Attribution: Making the Bill Name a Team

Every technique above requires knowing whose cost you are looking at. That is a tagging problem, and it has one property that catches nearly everyone.

$ aws ce list-cost-allocation-tags --status Active --query 'CostAllocationTags[].TagKey'
[]                                                 ← tags exist on resources, but not in the BILL

$ aws ce update-cost-allocation-tags-status \
    --cost-allocation-tags-status TagKey=team,Status=Active \
                                 TagKey=env,Status=Active \
                                 TagKey=cost-center,Status=Active
⚠️ Activating a cost allocation tag is not retroactive. The key appears in Cost Explorer roughly 24 hours later and only covers usage from activation forward — last quarter's spend stays untagged forever, no matter how you tag the resources now. Activate the keys on day one of an account, before you need them. This is the single most common "we'll do it when we need it" that cannot be done when you need it.
Tags are only useful if they are actually applied — enforce, don't ask (see the SCP pattern from
the Foundations lesson):

{ "Effect": "Deny",
  "Action": ["ec2:RunInstances", "rds:CreateDBInstance"],
  "Resource": "*",
  "Condition": { "Null": { "aws:RequestTag/team": "true" } } }
   → an untagged launch is refused at creation, which is the only point at which
     anyone still remembers who owns the thing.

$ aws resourcegroupstaggingapi get-resources --resource-type-filters ec2:instance \
    --query 'length(ResourceTagMappingList[?!not_null(Tags[?Key==`team`])])'
37                                                 ← 37 instances nobody owns

Three guardrails, cheapest first:

💡 Billing data lags by up to 24 hours. A runaway — a retry storm, a recursive Lambda, an accidental cross-Region replication — bills for a full day before any alert can fire. That is why the operational controls that actually bound a disaster are the quotas from the Foundations lesson and concurrency limits, not budgets. A budget tells you what already happened; a service quota stops it happening.
The three-query regression drill — always in this order:

1. WHICH SERVICE?
$ aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=2026-06-01,End=2026-08-01 \
    --granularity MONTHLY --metrics UnblendedCost \
    --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=SERVICE --output table
|  Amazon Elastic Compute Cloud  |  18402.11  |  18590.44  |
|  Amazon Simple Storage Service |   2210.05  |   9884.70  |   ← +$7,674
|  AmazonCloudWatch              |   1102.66  |   1140.02  |

2. WHICH USAGE TYPE? (the behaviour, not the service)
$ aws ce get-cost-and-usage ... --filter '{"Dimensions":{"Key":"SERVICE",
      "Values":["Amazon Simple Storage Service"]}}' \
    --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=USAGE_TYPE --output table
|  USE1-Requests-Tier1  |    88.20  |  7622.40  |   ← PUTs, not storage
|  USE1-TimedStorage-ByteHrs |  2044.11 | 2100.30 |

3. WHOSE?
$ aws ce get-cost-and-usage ... --group-by Type=TAG,Key=team --output table
|  team$ingestion  |   41.02  |  7580.15  |
        → a retry loop in the ingestion pipeline re-PUT every object, hourly.
          $0.005 per 1,000 PUTs × 1.5 billion PUTs.

Note what the drill proves: the S3 bill grew 4× and storage barely moved. Had anyone stopped at step 1 they would have gone hunting for a big bucket to delete, found nothing, and moved on. Service → usage type → tag, every time.

❌ A three-year Savings Plan sized on last month's average, no S3 gateway endpoint, a lifecycle rule copied from a blog post, Lambda memory cut to 128 MB "to save money", DEBUG logging left on in production, and cost allocation tags nobody activated. The commitment was sized on the average rather than the floor, so every quiet hour is billed at full price for something nobody used — and it cannot be sold, only outlived for three years. Private subnets pull terabytes from S3 through NAT Gateways, paying $0.045 per gigabyte to reach a destination that is free. A lifecycle rule transitions 50 million 40 KB objects to Glacier, incurring $2,500 in per-object fees for a $24/month saving that will pay back in 2034. Someone reduced Lambda memory and the bill went up, because the function is CPU-bound and now runs ten times longer. The application logs 200 GB a day, so CloudWatch costs more than the EC2 fleet producing it. And when the CFO asks which team drove the increase, the answer is that nobody can tell, because the tags exist on resources but were never activated for billing and activation is not retroactive.

✅ Commit to the floor, endpoint the free destinations, do the per-object arithmetic, sweep the memory curve, cap ingestion at the source, and activate the tags on day one. Size Savings Plans against the lowest hour of a typical week and stack a second plan when the floor rises, because under-commitment costs you the discount while over-commitment costs you the principal. Put an S3 and DynamoDB Gateway Endpoint in every VPC at creation — they are free, and their absence is a pure tax. Before any lifecycle rule, divide bucket bytes by object count: below ~128 KB the answer is always "aggregate the objects", never "change the storage class". Sweep Lambda memory across the full range and pin the minimum of the U-shaped curve, remembering that CPU scales with memory. Fix log volume at the source — retention only caps storage, while ingestion at $0.50/GB is the larger number. Enforce ownership tags with an SCP at creation time and activate them as cost allocation tags immediately, since no amount of later tagging recovers this quarter's attribution. And rely on quotas and concurrency limits, not budgets, to bound a runaway — billing data is a day late, and a budget alert is a postmortem, not a control.

Interview Questions

Beginner: Which directions of data transfer cost money, and which are free?

Inbound from the internet is always free. Outbound to the internet is the expensive one at roughly $0.09/GB after the first 100 GB per month. Inside a Region, traffic between resources in the same AZ over private IPs is free — but the same traffic over public IPs or Elastic IPs is charged, and traffic across AZs is $0.01/GB in each direction, so a request and its response cost about $0.02/GB. Cross-Region is more. A NAT Gateway adds $0.045/GB of processing on top of whatever transfer applies, and CloudFront is the exception that is cheaper than raw egress while fetching from an AWS origin for free.

The framing that lands: AWS wants your data in, and charges you to take it out or move it sideways. Every transfer question follows from that.

Beginner: On-Demand, Spot, Reserved and Savings Plans — when would you choose each?
ModelChoose it when
On-DemandUsage is unknown, short-lived, or being measured for the first time
SpotThe work is interruptible and retryable — batch, CI, stateless replicas behind a load balancer
Reserved InstancesA steady workload on a service with no Savings Plan (RDS, ElastiCache, Redshift, OpenSearch)
Savings PlansA predictable floor of EC2/Fargate/Lambda spend you are confident persists for the term

The order in practice is: run on-demand until you have a month of data, move interruptible work to Spot, then commit only the portion of the remainder that never goes away. Committing first and measuring afterwards is how accounts end up with three years of unused commitment.

Mid: Your team bought a Savings Plan and the bill went up. How is that possible, and how do you size the next one?

A Savings Plan commits you to a dollars-per-hour of spend, not to instances. AWS bills the commitment every hour whether or not you have eligible usage to apply it to. If the plan was sized on the monthly average and the fleet has a nightly or weekend trough, every trough hour pays for capacity that did not run.

break-even:  utilisation ≥ (1 − discount)

  27% discount → you must use ≥73% of the commitment every hour to break even.
  A fleet averaging $14/hr but troughing at $6/hr, committed at $14/hr:
      paid   $14.00/hr × 730           = $10,220
      on-demand cost of actual usage   = $ 7,154
      → the plan cost $3,066/month MORE than no plan at all.

Size the next one against the lowest hour of a typical week, from ce get-cost-and-usage at HOURLY granularity, then stack an additional plan later if the floor rises. There is no unwind: Savings Plans cannot be sold, and only Standard RIs have a marketplace. Check ce get-savings-plans-utilization monthly — anything below 100% utilisation is money burned, and low coverage (on-demand spend that a plan could have covered) is the signal to commit more.

Mid: A Lambda function was moved from 512 MB to 128 MB to reduce cost, and the bill went up. Explain, and give the general rule.

Lambda allocates CPU proportionally to memory — at 1,769 MB you get one full vCPU, at 128 MB about 7% of one. Billing is in GB-seconds: memory × duration. For a CPU-bound function, cutting memory by 4× multiplies duration by more than 4×, so GB-seconds — and therefore cost — increase, and latency gets much worse.

  128 MB × 11.8 s = 1.475 GB-s   →  $24.58 per million
 1024 MB ×  1.2 s = 1.200 GB-s   →  $20.00 per million   ← cheaper AND 10× faster
 3008 MB ×  0.92s = 2.703 GB-s   →  $45.04 per million

The general rule: the cost curve is U-shaped. Left of the minimum the function is CPU-starved and duration dominates; right of it extra CPU buys nothing and memory dominates. Sweep the range once, pin the minimum, and re-measure after dependency upgrades. The two exceptions: I/O-bound functions that spend their time waiting on a network call see almost no benefit from more CPU, so their curve is nearly flat and the smallest memory that fits the workload wins; and provisioned concurrency changes the arithmetic entirely because you are then paying for idle time too.

Senior: The bill is up 40% month over month with no deployment. Walk through your diagnosis, in order.

1. Service. ce get-cost-and-usage, MONTHLY, grouped by SERVICE, two months side by side. This is orientation only — never act on it.

2. Usage type. Filter to the guilty service and group by USAGE_TYPE. This is the step that actually identifies the behaviour: Requests-Tier1 is a write storm, TimedStorage-ByteHrs is accumulation, NatGateway-Bytes is a missing endpoint, DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes is cross-AZ chatter. Skipping this step is the single most common mistake — a 4× S3 bill where storage barely moved sends people hunting for a bucket to delete.

3. Owner. Group by the team tag to name a team, and by LINKED_ACCOUNT if the tag is missing or was never activated.

4. Resource and hour. Cost Explorer stops at daily and does not name resources; the Cost and Usage Report in Athena does. Query line_item_resource_id by hour to find the exact bucket, function or ENI and the hour it started — which is what you correlate against deploys, cron schedules and incidents.

SELECT line_item_resource_id, line_item_usage_type,
       ROUND(SUM(line_item_unblended_cost), 2) AS cost
FROM   cur.acme
WHERE  line_item_usage_start_date >= TIMESTAMP '2026-07-01 00:00:00'
GROUP  BY 1, 2 ORDER BY cost DESC LIMIT 20;

Also rule out the non-technical causes, which produce exactly this shape with no code change: a Savings Plan or RI expired and usage silently reverted to on-demand; the free tier ended twelve months after account creation; a data-retention or replication rule crossed a threshold; or someone enabled a per-request feature such as S3 versioning, cross-Region replication, GuardDuty or detailed monitoring. "No deployment" rules out application changes only.

Then close the loop: a Cost Anomaly Detection monitor (free) so the next one is caught in hours rather than at month end, and — if the cause was a runaway rather than growth — a quota or concurrency limit, because billing data is a day late and no budget can stop anything in time.

Senior: Design cost attribution for a platform with 40 teams on shared infrastructure. What is genuinely unattributable, and what do you do about it?

Tier 1 — direct. Anything with a resource ARN gets a mandatory team tag, enforced by an SCP with a Null condition on aws:RequestTag/team so an untagged launch fails at creation. Activate the key as a cost allocation tag on day one — activation is not retroactive, so this is the decision you cannot defer. This typically covers 60–80% of spend.

Tier 2 — shared but measurable. A Kubernetes cluster, a shared RDS instance, an ingestion pipeline. These have no per-team ARN, but they do have a usage signal: pod CPU/memory-seconds per namespace, queries per database user, records per source. Split the actual bill by that measured ratio and publish the ratio alongside the number, because a showback nobody can reproduce is a showback nobody believes.

Tier 3 — genuinely unattributable. NAT Gateway hourly charges, Transit Gateway attachments, control planes, Config, GuardDuty, the security tooling, support. Do not invent a split. Either hold it as a platform tax on a central cost centre, or apportion it by a single published rule (headcount, or share of Tier 1 spend) that everyone agreed to in advance. Arguing about a $900 NAT hourly charge costs more in engineering time than the charge.

The structural answer, if you can take it: per-team AWS accounts inside an Organization. The account boundary is the only attribution mechanism that cannot be forgotten, drifts with nothing, and is also the blast radius and the quota boundary. Tags are a workaround for shared accounts; separate accounts make attribution a property of the architecture. Savings Plans and RIs still pool across the Organization via consolidated billing, so you keep the discounts — which is the objection people usually raise, and it is not true.

Then make it move behaviour. Attribution nobody sees changes nothing: a monthly per-team report, an anomaly monitor per linked account, and — the part that actually works — the unit-economics number that matters to that team (cost per thousand orders, per active user, per inference). Absolute dollars go up when the business grows and tell you nothing; cost per unit going up while the business grows is the signal.

4 (medium): Find the cost-minimising memory setting for a Lambda function empirically, and explain the shape of the result.
Solution 
$ cat > sweep.sh <<'SH'
#!/usr/bin/env bash
FN=$1
for MB in 128 256 512 1024 1769 3008; do
  aws lambda update-function-configuration --function-name "$FN" --memory-size "$MB" >/dev/null
  aws lambda wait function-updated --function-name "$FN"
  TOTAL=0
  for i in $(seq 1 12); do                        # 12 runs; discard the first (cold start)
    D=$(aws lambda invoke --function-name "$FN" --log-type Tail --payload '{}' /dev/null \
        --query LogResult --output text | base64 -d \
        | grep 'Billed Duration' | grep -o '[0-9]\+ ms' | head -1 | tr -d ' ms')
    [ "$i" -gt 1 ] && TOTAL=$((TOTAL + D))
  done
  MS=$((TOTAL / 11))
  COST=$(awk -v m="$MB" -v ms="$MS" 'BEGIN{printf "%.2f", m/1024*ms/1000*0.0000166667*1000000}')
  printf "%5s MB  %7s ms  $%s per 1M invocations\n" "$MB" "$MS" "$COST"
done

🔑 The general rule: below ~128 KB, you have an object-count problem, and no storage class solves an object-count problem. Every colder tier charges per object to enter it, imposes a minimum billable size, and imposes a minimum duration — three per-object costs against a per-byte saving. Compute bytes ÷ objects first; if the answer is under 128 KB, the only correct move is to make fewer, larger objects. And always check the read pattern before archiving: retrieval fees and restore latency turn a cost decision into an availability decision, and a five-hour wait on the logs you need during an incident is a much larger bill than $27 a month.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Nearly every AWS charge is one of four shapes — time, capacity-time, requests, movement — and classifying a service by shape predicts where its bill will come from.
  • Moving a terabyte once costs more than storing it for a month; transfer is a property of architecture and appears nowhere in your code.
  • Group the bill by usage type, not service — "S3 is up $7,674" turned out to be PUT requests, and storage had barely moved.
  • A Savings Plan is a commitment to spend, not a discount on instances; unused hours are billed, and it can never be sold.
  • Break-even is utilisation ≥ (1 − discount) — a 27% plan needs 73% usage; sized on the average instead of the floor, one cost $3,066/month more than no plan.
  • Commit to the floor and stack later plans; under-commitment is recoverable tomorrow, over-commitment is not recoverable for the term.
  • Read utilisation and coverage together: utilisation below 100% is burnt principal, low coverage at 100% utilisation means commit more.
  • Spot with price-capacity-optimized and a diversified instance list beats lowest-price — the cheapest pool is cheap because it is about to be reclaimed.
  • Inbound is free; outbound and sideways cost. Cross-AZ is $0.01/GB each direction, and same-AZ traffic over public IPs is charged too.
  • A gateway endpoint for S3 and DynamoDB is free and belongs in every VPC — its absence charged $181/month of NAT processing to reach a free destination, cut to $5.
  • 196 TB of cross-AZ traffic was one API returning a 412 KB record to answer a boolean; fewer fields removed 99.5% before any topology change.
  • Fix cross-AZ cost by caching and topology-aware routing — never by collapsing into one AZ, which sells the availability you bought.
  • Colder S3 tiers charge per object to enter, impose a 128 KB minimum billable size and a 30/90/180-day minimum duration — three per-object costs against a per-byte saving.
  • Divide bytes by object count before writing any lifecycle rule: 50 M × 43 KB objects gave Standard-IA at 62% more and Glacier at a 93-month payback; aggregating to Parquet saved 87% immediately.
  • Check the read pattern before archiving — retrieval fees and a 3–5 hour restore turn a cost decision into an availability decision.
  • gp3 is 20% cheaper than gp2 with 3,000 IOPS included, and the conversion is online; audit status=available volumes, which bill at full price attached to nothing.
  • Lambda cost is U-shaped in memory because CPU scales with memory — 1024 MB was both the cheapest setting and 10× faster than 128 MB, and it used 10× less concurrency.
  • CloudWatch Logs ingestion at $0.50/GB can exceed the compute producing the logs; retention caps storage only, so fix volume at the source.
  • HTTP APIs are 3.5× cheaper than REST APIs; DynamoDB on-demand is ~7× the provisioned rate above ~14% utilisation.
  • Cost allocation tags are not retroactive — activate on day one and enforce the tag at creation with an SCP, because no later tagging recovers this quarter.
  • Billing data lags up to 24 hours, so quotas and concurrency limits bound a runaway; budgets only describe one. Anomaly detection is free — turn it on.
