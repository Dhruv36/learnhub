Shared Responsibility, Accounts & Regions Foundations

What you'll master: where the shared responsibility line actually sits, and how it moves between EC2, RDS, Lambda and S3 — because nearly every publicised "AWS breach" was on the customer's side of it; what a Region and an Availability Zone really are, why your AZ us-east-1a is not the same physical place as someone else's, and which services are global with a hidden us-east-1 dependency; why the account — not the VPC, not the tag — is the real blast radius, and how Organizations, OUs and SCPs express that; the SCP semantics people get backwards (they can only take permissions away, never grant them); service quotas, which are per-account per-Region and are the reason something that worked in dev fails in production; and the API model underneath everything — signed requests, throttling, retries with jitter, and eventual consistency that makes a just-created resource temporarily invisible.

Part 1 — The Line Moves Per Service

"AWS is responsible for security of the cloud; you are responsible for security in the cloud" is accurate and useless until you know where the line falls for the service in front of you.

LayerEC2RDSLambdaS3
Physical, power, network fabricAWSAWSAWSAWS
HypervisorAWSAWSAWSAWS
Guest OS, patchingYOUAWSAWSAWS
Runtime / engine versionYOUYOU choose, AWS patchesAWSAWS
Application codeYOUYOUYOUn/a
Network exposureYOUYOUYOUYOU
IAM and access policyYOUYOUYOUYOU
Encryption choiceYOUYOUYOUYOU
Your dataYOUYOUYOUYOU
🔑 Everything in the bottom four rows is yours in every service. That is why the recurring headline is never "AWS was hacked" — it is a public S3 bucket, an over-permissive IAM role, a security group open to 0.0.0.0/0, or a leaked access key. AWS's side of the line has an extremely good record; the interesting failures all live on yours.

The practical reading of the table: as you move right, you give up control and gain less to operate. An EC2 instance means you own kernel patching and CVE response; RDS means AWS patches the engine on a maintenance window you choose; Lambda means you own nothing below your handler. That is a trade about operational burden, not about safety — a misconfigured Lambda role is exactly as dangerous as a misconfigured EC2 role.

Part 2 — Regions, AZs and the Global Services

  REGION            an independent geographic deployment with its OWN control plane
    └── AZ          one or more discrete data centres, isolated power/cooling/network
          └── DC    the physical buildings

  · AZs within a Region are < 100 km apart, single-digit-millisecond RTT
  · Regions are fully independent: an outage in one does NOT propagate
  · Most AWS APIs are REGIONAL — resources do not exist outside their Region

$ aws ec2 describe-availability-zones --region eu-west-1 \
    --query 'AvailabilityZones[].[ZoneName,ZoneId]' --output text
eu-west-1a    euw1-az2
eu-west-1b    euw1-az1
eu-west-1c    euw1-az3
        ↑ ZoneNAME is per-account; ZoneID is the real physical zone
⚠️ us-east-1a in your account is not us-east-1a in mine. AWS randomises the name-to-zone mapping per account so customers do not all pile into "a". Two accounts coordinating placement — a shared VPC, a cross-account peering, a partner integration — must compare Zone IDs (use1-az4), not zone names, or they will believe they are co-located when they are not.
ServiceScopeThe catch
IAMGlobalControl plane lives in us-east-1; changes propagate eventually
Route 53GlobalAPI is us-east-1-only
CloudFrontGlobalACM certificates must be issued in us-east-1

This is why a us-east-1 incident makes headlines out of proportion to its footprint: even companies running entirely in Frankfurt may be unable to change IAM, create CloudFront distributions, or update Route 53 during one. Their running workloads keep serving; their control plane does not.

Part 3 — The Account Is the Blast Radius

BoundaryIsolatesVerdict

AccountIAM, quotas, billing, most APIsThe real boundary
  Organization
   └── Root
        ├── OU: Security      → log-archive, audit accounts
        ├── OU: Infrastructure→ networking, shared services
        ├── OU: Workloads
        │     ├── OU: Prod    → shop-prod, payments-prod      ← SCP: strict
        │     └── OU: NonProd → shop-dev, shop-staging        ← SCP: relaxed
        └── OU: Sandbox       → per-engineer accounts         ← SCP: spend guardrails

An account per environment sounds heavyweight and is the standard because it makes several problems disappear at once: a runaway process cannot exhaust production's quotas, a compromised dev credential cannot reach production data, and the bill is already broken down without a tagging discipline nobody maintains.

SCPs subtract; they never add

  effective permissions = SCP allows  ∩  identity policy allows  ∩  resource policy allows
                          ────────────
                          a FILTER, not a grant

$ aws s3 ls --profile prod
An error occurred (AccessDenied) when calling the ListBuckets operation:
User: arn:aws:sts::111122223333:assumed-role/Admin/alice is not authorized to perform:
s3:ListAllMyBuckets with an explicit deny in a service control policy
                                                    ↑ even for an account administrator
🔑 An SCP that "allows" something grants nobody anything. It only defines the ceiling within which IAM policies may operate. Attaching a permissive SCP does not give users permissions, and attaching a restrictive one can lock out administrators — including, if you are careless with the management account, yourself. Test SCPs on a sandbox OU before promoting them.

Two more account-level facts worth internalising early. The root user is not an IAM identity, cannot be restricted by IAM policy, and can perform a handful of things nothing else can (closing the account, changing the support plan, some S3/SQS policy recoveries) — so it gets hardware MFA and is then never used. And a few actions cannot be denied by SCPs at all in the management account, which is the strongest argument for keeping that account empty of workloads.

⚠️ Quotas are the single most common cause of "it worked in dev". They are per account and per Region, so a limit increase granted in eu-west-1 does nothing in eu-central-1, and one granted in the dev account does nothing in production. Raise them ahead of a launch, per Region, per account — the request is asynchronous and can take days.

$ aws iam create-role --role-name app --assume-role-policy-document file://trust.json
$ aws lambda create-function --role arn:aws:iam::111122223333:role/app ...

        ↑ the role EXISTS; IAM has not finished propagating. Retry in a few seconds —
          this is why Terraform and CDK have explicit dependency and retry handling.

Two habits follow. Always use the SDK's built-in retry configuration rather than writing your own loop — it implements jittered exponential backoff and knows which error codes are retryable. And prefer waiters (aws ec2 wait instance-running, get_waiter() in boto3) over polling by hand, because they encode the correct terminal states.

Part 6 — The Day-One Baseline

ControlWhy, specifically
Hardware MFA on the root user, credentials locked awayRoot cannot be restricted by IAM; its compromise is total
No IAM users with long-lived access keysLeaked keys are the most common breach vector; use IAM Identity Center + SSO
CloudTrail enabled in all Regions, to a locked S3 bucket in a separate accountWithout it there is no answer to "what happened"
S3 Block Public Access at the account levelMakes the classic public-bucket mistake impossible
A budget with an alarmThe first sign of a compromised key is usually a crypto-mining bill
Default EBS and S3 encryption onFree, and removes a class of audit findings
Separate accounts for prod / non-prod / securityThe only boundary that actually contains a mistake
Regions you do not use, disabledShrinks the attack surface for key abuse

❌ One account for everything, IAM users with access keys committed to a repository, the root user used for daily work, no CloudTrail, and quotas discovered during a launch. Dev, staging and production share an account, so a load test exhausts the Lambda concurrency limit and takes production down with it, and a developer's credential is one policy mistake away from the production database. An access key ends up in a public repository, and because there is no budget alarm the first anyone hears of it is a five-figure bill for mining instances in five Regions nobody uses. The root user has no MFA and is shared through a password manager, so nothing in CloudTrail — which was never enabled — could attribute the actions anyway. An SCP is written to "give the prod OU S3 access", which grants nothing and confuses everyone. And on launch day the production account cannot start more than five vCPUs of EC2, because the quota increase was requested in the dev account, in a different Region.

✅ Separate accounts, no long-lived keys, audit from day one, and quotas raised before you need them. Give every environment its own account under Organizations, with SCPs as guardrails that express what may never happen — remembering they only subtract, so the identity policies still have to grant. Put hardware MFA on the root user and then never use it; issue human access through IAM Identity Center with short-lived credentials, and give workloads roles rather than keys. Turn on CloudTrail in all Regions, delivering to a bucket in a separate security account that the workload accounts cannot write to, and enable account-level S3 Block Public Access so the classic mistake is structurally impossible. Set a budget alarm on day one, because an unexpected bill is the fastest detector of a leaked credential. Compare Zone IDs rather than zone names whenever two accounts must be co-located. And raise service quotas per account, per Region, weeks ahead of a launch — the request is asynchronous, and the default of five vCPUs will not run a single large instance.

Interview Questions

Beginner: Explain the shared responsibility model.

AWS secures the cloud; you secure what you put in it — and the exact boundary depends on the service.

LayerEC2RDSLambda
Hardware, hypervisorAWSAWSAWS
Guest OS patchingYouAWSAWS
Application codeYouYouYou
IAM, network exposure, dataYouYouYou

The part worth emphasising is that the last row never moves. Access policy, network exposure and your data are yours in every service — which is why publicised incidents are consistently public S3 buckets, over-broad IAM roles and leaked keys rather than failures of AWS infrastructure.

Moving right along the table reduces what you operate, not what you are responsible for. A misconfigured Lambda execution role is exactly as dangerous as a misconfigured EC2 instance profile.

Beginner: What is the difference between a Region and an Availability Zone?

A Region is an independent geographic deployment with its own control plane; an AZ is one or more discrete data centres within it.

Availability ZoneRegion
IsolationPower, cooling, networkEverything, including the control plane
Latency betweenSingle-digit millisecondsTens to hundreds
Protects againstA data-centre failureA regional outage
Cost of spanningCross-AZ data transfer chargesMuch higher; full duplication

The detail that catches people: AZ names are randomised per account, so your us-east-1a is not mine. Cross-account placement must compare Zone IDs like use1-az4.

And the asymmetry to mention: multi-AZ is cheap and mostly automatic; multi-Region is expensive and hard, because data replication, failover and testing all become your problem. Most systems should be multi-AZ by default and multi-Region only with a specific requirement.

Mid: Why use multiple AWS accounts, and how do SCPs fit in?

The account is the only boundary that reliably contains a mistake. Tags isolate nothing, VPCs isolate only network reachability, and IAM policies isolate only as well as they are written.

Isolated by an account boundaryNot isolated within one account
IAM blast radiusA misconfigured role reaching prod data
Service quotasA load test exhausting Lambda concurrency
Billing attributionCost allocation that depends on tagging discipline
Most API surfacesAccidental cross-environment API calls

SCPs are guardrails at the OU or account level, and they only subtract:

effective = SCP allows ∩ identity policy allows ∩ resource policy allows

An SCP that "allows" S3 grants nobody anything — it merely leaves S3 inside the ceiling. That is the misconception to correct in an interview. Typical real uses are denying Region use outside an approved list, denying the deletion of CloudTrail or security tooling, and denying root-user actions.

Two operational cautions: an SCP can lock out administrators, so test on a sandbox OU first; and workloads should not run in the management account, where some actions cannot be restricted at all.

Mid: A deployment works in dev and fails in production with a quota error. Why does that happen so often?

Because quotas are per account and per Region, and most defaults are far lower than people assume.

$ aws service-quotas get-service-quota --service-code ec2 --quota-code L-1216C47A \
    --query 'Quota.[QuotaName,Value]' --output text
Running On-Demand Standard instances    5.0        ← five vCPUs, not five instances
QuotaDefaultBites when
Lambda concurrency1,000/RegionOne function starves every other
VPCs5/RegionPer-team VPCs
Elastic IPs5NAT gateways consume them
SG rules60 inboundA growing IP allowlist

A limit increase in one account and Region does nothing anywhere else, and the request is asynchronous — sometimes days. So capacity planning has to include a quota audit per target Region, weeks before a launch.

The related trap is Lambda concurrency being a shared, account-wide pool: without reserved concurrency on the critical functions, a runaway batch job throttles your API. That is a quota acting as a shared fate boundary, which is another argument for separate accounts.

Senior: Design the account structure for a company running several products across multiple environments.

Accounts as the isolation primitive, OUs as the policy grouping, and a small number of shared accounts for things that genuinely must be central.

Root
 ├── Security OU
 │     ├── log-archive        (CloudTrail, Config — write-once, nobody can delete)
 │     └── audit              (read-only access into every account)
 ├── Infrastructure OU
 │     ├── network            (Transit Gateway, shared VPCs, DNS)
 │     └── shared-services    (CI runners, artefact registries)
 ├── Workloads OU
 │     ├── Prod OU            → one account per product
 │     └── NonProd OU         → dev and staging per product
 └── Sandbox OU               → per-engineer, tight budget SCPs, auto-nuked
DecisionReason
Account per product per environmentQuotas and IAM blast radius are per account
Empty management accountSome actions cannot be restricted by SCPs there
Log archive in its own accountAn attacker in a workload account cannot erase the evidence
SCPs on the Prod OUDeny Region sprawl, deny disabling security tooling, deny root actions
Identity Center for humansShort-lived credentials; one place to offboard
Centralised networkingTransit Gateway and IP planning are genuinely global concerns

What I would automate from the start: account vending — a new account arrives with CloudTrail, Config, GuardDuty, budget alarms, Block Public Access and the baseline SCPs already applied. Retrofitting a baseline onto forty accounts is a project; applying it at creation is a template.

The cost people worry about and should not: accounts are free. The real cost is operational — cross-account access, shared networking and consolidated observability all need design. That is exactly the work a landing zone (Control Tower, or a Terraform equivalent) exists to do, and doing it early is far cheaper than doing it after an incident forces the issue.

Senior: How would you reason about regional dependencies in a disaster-recovery plan?

Separate what keeps serving from what lets you change anything — they fail differently and most plans only consider the first.

DependencyLives inIf it is unavailable
IAM control planeus-east-1Existing credentials work; you cannot create or change roles
Route 53 APIus-east-1DNS resolves; you cannot fail over by changing records
CloudFront distributions and ACM certsus-east-1Existing distributions serve; no changes
Organizations / SCPsus-east-1Existing policy applies; no changes
Your workload RegionWherever you deployedServing stops

The Route 53 row is the one that ruins DR plans. If your failover mechanism is "update a DNS record", and the API to do that is unavailable, you have a plan that fails precisely when it is needed. The mitigations are health-check-based failover configured in advance (which continues to work without API access), and weighted records already in place so failover is a data-plane behaviour rather than a control-plane action.

The general principle is static stability: the recovery path should not require creating anything. Pre-provision the standby, pre-create the roles, pre-configure the health checks — and accept the standing cost, because a recovery that depends on a control plane you do not control is a hope, not a plan.

And test it by disabling the control plane, not just the workload. Most DR exercises simulate "the Region is slow" and quietly assume every API still answers.

3 (medium): Audit service quotas before a launch.
Solution 
$ cat > tools/quota-audit.sh <<'SH'
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# Compare current usage against quotas for the services a launch depends on.
set -uo pipefail
REGION=${1:?region}; PROFILE=${2:-default}
A="--region $REGION --profile $PROFILE"

check() { # service-code quota-code label current
  local val
  val=$(aws service-quotas get-service-quota --service-code "$1" --quota-code "$2" \
        $A --query 'Quota.Value' --output text 2>/dev/null) || val="?"
  printf '  %-34s quota=%-10s in use=%s\n' "$3" "$val" "$4"
}

check ec2 L-1216C47A "EC2 On-Demand standard vCPUs" \
  "$(aws ec2 describe-instances $A --filters Name=instance-state-name,Values=running \
     --query 'Reservations[].Instances[].CpuOptions.CoreCount' --output text | wc -w)"
check vpc L-F678F1CE "VPCs per Region" \
  "$(aws ec2 describe-vpcs $A --query 'length(Vpcs)' --output text)"
check ec2 L-0263D0A3 "Elastic IPs" \
  "$(aws ec2 describe-addresses $A --query 'length(Addresses)' --output text)"
check lambda L-B99A9384 "Lambda concurrent executions" \
  "$(aws lambda get-account-settings $A --query 'AccountUsage.FunctionCount' --output text) functions"
check rds L-7B6409FD "RDS instances" \
  "$(aws rds describe-db-instances $A --query 'length(DBInstances)' --output text)"
SH

$ cat > tools/account-baseline.sh <<'SH'
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# Read-only check of the controls every account should have from day one.
set -uo pipefail
P=${1:-default}; FAIL=0
ok()   { printf '  ✅ %s\n' "$1"; }
bad()  { printf '  ❌ %s\n' "$1"; FAIL=1; }

echo "== root user"
S=$(aws iam get-account-summary --profile "$P" --query 'SummaryMap' --output json)
[ "$(jq -r '.AccountMFAEnabled' <<<"$S")" = "1" ] && ok "root MFA enabled" || bad "root MFA MISSING"
[ "$(jq -r '.AccountAccessKeysPresent' <<<"$S")" = "0" ] && ok "no root access keys" || bad "ROOT ACCESS KEYS EXIST"

echo; echo "== IAM users with long-lived keys"
n=$(aws iam list-users --profile "$P" --query 'Users[].UserName' --output text | tr '\t' '\n' | while read -r u; do
      [ -n "$u" ] && aws iam list-access-keys --user-name "$u" --profile "$P" \
        --query 'AccessKeyMetadata[?Status==`Active`].AccessKeyId' --output text
    done | wc -w)
[ "$n" -eq 0 ] && ok "no active IAM user keys" || bad "$n active IAM user access key(s)"

echo; echo "== CloudTrail"
t=$(aws cloudtrail describe-trails --profile "$P" \
    --query 'trailList[?IsMultiRegionTrail==`true`].Name' --output text)
[ -n "$t" ] && ok "multi-region trail: $t" || bad "no multi-region CloudTrail"

echo; echo "== S3 account-level Block Public Access"
acct=$(aws sts get-caller-identity --profile "$P" --query Account --output text)
b=$(aws s3control get-public-access-block --account-id "$acct" --profile "$P" \
    --query 'PublicAccessBlockConfiguration.[BlockPublicAcls,IgnorePublicAcls,BlockPublicPolicy,RestrictPublicBuckets]' \
    --output text 2>/dev/null)
[ "$b" = "True	True	True	True" ] && ok "block public access: all four ON" || bad "block public access incomplete: ${b:-NOT SET}"

echo; echo "== budgets"
n=$(aws budgets describe-budgets --account-id "$acct" --profile "$P" \
    --query 'length(Budgets)' --output text 2>/dev/null || echo 0)
[ "$n" -gt 0 ] && ok "$n budget(s) configured" || bad "NO budget alarm"

echo; echo "== default EBS encryption (per region)"
for r in eu-west-1 us-east-1; do
  e=$(aws ec2 get-ebs-encryption-by-default --region "$r" --profile "$P" \
      --query 'EbsEncryptionByDefault' --output text)
  [ "$e" = "True" ] && ok "$r: default EBS encryption on" || bad "$r: default EBS encryption OFF"
done

echo; echo "== enabled regions"
aws account list-regions --profile "$P" \
  --query 'Regions[?RegionOptStatus==`ENABLED`].RegionName' --output text 2>/dev/null \
  | tr '\t' '\n' | wc -l | xargs printf '  %s regions enabled\n'
exit $FAIL
SH

The principle: a recovery path that requires creating anything depends on a control plane you do not control. Static stability means the standby already exists and failover is a data-plane behaviour — which is also the only version you can test cheaply.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • The shared responsibility line moves per service, but IAM, network exposure and your data are yours in every one — which is why publicised incidents are always on the customer side.
  • Moving from EC2 to RDS to Lambda reduces what you operate, not what you are responsible for.
  • A Region is an independent deployment with its own control plane; an AZ is one or more discrete data centres, single-digit milliseconds away.
  • AZ names are randomised per account — us-east-1a in one account maps to a different physical zone in another. Cross-account placement must use Zone IDs.
  • IAM, Route 53, CloudFront, Organizations and billing are global with a us-east-1 control plane; ACM certificates for CloudFront must be issued there.
  • A us-east-1 incident means workloads keep serving while you cannot change IAM, DNS or CloudFront — which is why DNS-based failover must be configured in advance.
  • The account is the real blast radius: tags isolate nothing, VPCs isolate only network reachability, and IAM isolates only as well as it is written.
  • One account per environment contains quota exhaustion, IAM mistakes and billing attribution in a single move.
  • SCPs only subtract — an SCP that "allows" S3 grants nobody anything; effective permissions are the intersection of the SCP, the identity policy and any resource policy.
  • An SCP denying Regions blocked even an account administrator — and must exempt global services, or IAM, Route 53 and CloudFront break.
  • Keep the management account empty: some actions cannot be restricted by SCPs there.
  • The root user is not an IAM identity and cannot be constrained by IAM policy — hardware MFA, then never used.
  • Quotas are per account AND per Region, and an increase in one applies to neither the other Region nor the other account.
  • A new account's default of five On-Demand vCPUs will not launch a single m5.2xlarge; Lambda's 1,000 concurrent executions are a shared pool one function can exhaust.
  • Quota increases are asynchronous — hours to days — so they belong weeks before a launch, not on the day.
  • Every action is a signed API call: AccessDenied names the principal and action, and throttling requires exponential backoff with jitter, which the SDK already implements.
  • Control planes are eventually consistent — a just-created IAM role can be rejected as unassumable for several seconds. Retry, and use waiters rather than sleeps.
  • Day-one baseline: root MFA and no root keys, no IAM user access keys, multi-Region CloudTrail to a separate account, account-level S3 Block Public Access, a budget alarm, default encryption, and unused Regions disabled.
  • A budget alarm is the fastest practical detector of a leaked credential.
  • Static stability: a recovery path that requires creating anything depends on a control plane you do not control.
