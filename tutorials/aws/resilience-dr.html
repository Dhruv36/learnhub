Resilience & Disaster Recovery Architecture

What you'll master: the distinction that decides every DR design — high availability survives a component dying; disaster recovery restores state after something destroys it, and Multi-AZ buys you the first while doing nothing whatsoever about the second, because it replicates a DELETE FROM orders in eleven milliseconds; how to derive a DR strategy from written RTO and RPO numbers instead of picking a tier and reverse-engineering the justification; why the only RTO that exists is the one you have measured — a designed "four hours" that had never been rehearsed took 9h47m on the first real attempt, and 52 minutes after the four defects that drill exposed were fixed; the restore mechanics nobody reads about until the outage (EBS lazy loading, a 2 TB snapshot restore that came up "available" in 21 minutes and then served queries at 18× normal latency for 40 minutes); the real RPO of every replication option, including the last-writer-wins silent overwrite in DynamoDB global tables; static stability — the principle that recovery must never depend on the control plane of the thing that is broken; why failover time is health check + DNS TTL + JVM cache + connection pool, which measured 7m12s where the runbook claimed 90 seconds; and how to run a Region-evacuation game day that finds the quota, certificate and IAM dependencies before an incident does. Prerequisites: VPCs, Route 53 & load balancers, RDS & Aurora, DynamoDB, S3, Observability and the Well-Architected trade-offs.

Part 1 — RTO and RPO Are Contracts, Not Adjectives

Every DR conversation that goes badly starts the same way: someone says the system must be "highly available" or "disaster-proof", and the team picks an architecture. Every DR conversation that goes well starts with two numbers written down and signed by someone who owns the revenue.

TermQuestion it answersUnitsWhat it buys
RTO — Recovery Time ObjectiveHow long may we be down?Time from failure to service restoredStandby capacity, automation, rehearsal
RPO — Recovery Point ObjectiveHow much data may we lose?Time between last durable copy and failureReplication frequency and synchronicity
RTA / RPA — the actual figuresWhat did the last drill measure?Same units, different truthNothing — it is the audit of the above

RTO and RPO are independent. A nightly backup gives you an RPO of up to 24 hours and, if you have rehearsed the restore, an RTO of 40 minutes. Synchronous replication gives you an RPO of zero and, if nobody has automated the promotion, an RTO of "whenever the on-call engineer wakes up and finds the runbook". Systems fail on one of the two numbers far more often than both.

THE FOUR STRATEGIES (AWS DR whitepaper), WITH THE COST THAT COMES WITH THEM

  strategy            RPO           RTO            what is running in the DR Region
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Backup & Restore    hours         hours          nothing. Backups exist, that is all.
  Pilot Light         minutes       tens of mins   data replicated + AMIs/IaC ready;
                                                   NO application servers running
  Warm Standby        seconds       minutes        a scaled-down but WORKING copy,
                                                   serving synthetic traffic

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  $41,300/month production estate):

  Pilot Light         $ 4,910/mo   11.9%   (Aurora Global secondary, S3 CRR, NAT, ECR)
  Warm Standby        $12,740/mo   30.9%   (the above + 2 tasks/service + ALB + 20% RDS)

Note the shape of that cost curve: the jump from "nothing" to Pilot Light is 13×, and almost all of it is the replicated database and the cross-Region data transfer at $0.02/GB — not the compute, because in Pilot Light there is no compute. The jump from Warm Standby to active/active is relatively small in dollars and enormous in engineering: every write path now needs a conflict story.

🔑 Derive the strategy from the numbers; never the reverse. The honest sequence is: (1) what does an hour of downtime cost, (2) therefore what RTO can we justify, (3) which strategy achieves it, (4) what does that strategy cost, (5) does step 4 exceed step 1 — if so, relax the RTO in writing. A team that starts at step 3 ends up paying for Warm Standby and, because it was never rehearsed, achieving Backup & Restore.
Symptom in a design reviewWhat it really means
"We need five nines"Nobody has priced downtime. See the availability arithmetic.
"We're multi-AZ, so we're covered"HA and DR have been conflated. Part 2.
"RTO is 4 hours" with no drill dateThe RTO is unknown. It is not 4 hours; it is unmeasured.
"RPO is zero"Only true for synchronous replication, and then only for infrastructure failure — not for a bad migration, which is replicated instantly.
One RTO for the whole companyTier the workloads. Checkout and the internal reporting dashboard do not deserve the same money.

Tiering is the single highest-leverage move in this entire lesson. On the estate above, applying one strategy to everything cost $12,740/month. Tiering it — active/active for the payment API, Warm Standby for the customer-facing app, Pilot Light for internal tooling, Backup & Restore for analytics — produced a better RTO on the tier that mattered for $9,180/month, because the money moved to where downtime was expensive.

Part 2 — High Availability Is Not Disaster Recovery

This is the most consequential confusion in the field, and it survives because both words sound like "the system stays up".

High AvailabilityDisaster Recovery
Protects againstA component failing: an instance, an AZ, a diskState being destroyed or a Region being lost
MechanismRedundancy + health checks + automatic failoverAn independent, older, immutable copy of the data
ScopeUsually inside one RegionUsually across Regions and accounts
Typical RTOSeconds to a couple of minutesMinutes to hours
Useless againstLogical corruption, bad deploys, deletionNothing — but you pay in RPO
AWS examplesMulti-AZ RDS, ASG across 3 AZs, ALB, DynamoDB (inherently multi-AZ)Snapshots, PITR, versioning, cross-account copies, Aurora Backtrack

The distinction becomes concrete the moment you look at what actually causes outages. Here is the incident record from the estate above — every Sev1/Sev2 over three years, classified by whether a second AZ or a second Region would have helped:

INCIDENT CLASSIFICATION, 3 YEARS, 14 INCIDENTS

  cause                                    count   Multi-AZ helps?   Multi-Region helps?
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  bad deploy / bad config change             6          no                 no
  dependency saturation (pool, quota)        3          no                 no
  expired certificate / credential           2          no                 no
  logical data damage (migration, DELETE)    1          no                 no  (replicated)
  single EC2 instance failure                1          YES                yes
  AZ-level impairment (EBS in one AZ)        1          YES                yes
  full Region impairment                     0          n/a                n/a
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  covered by Multi-AZ .......... 2 of 14 (14%)
  needed DR (restore state) .... 1 of 14  (7%)
  needed neither, only better
  change safety and limits ..... 11 of 14 (79%)
⚠️ Replication is not a backup, because replication is fast and faithful. In the logical-damage incident, an accidental DELETE against the writer was present on the Multi-AZ standby in 11 ms, on the cross-Region Aurora secondary in [0.9 s], and in the S3 replica bucket within the minute. Every copy was perfect. The recovery came from Backtrack — an older copy, which is the entire point. The property you need from a backup is not "another copy" but "a copy that has not yet seen the damage".

The corollary matters more than the rule: the four properties that make a copy a real backup are older (a point in time before the damage), immutable (nothing with production credentials can delete it), isolated (a different account, ideally a different Region), and proven (a restore has been performed from it recently). A copy missing any of the four is storage, not a backup — most notably the very common case of nightly snapshots that live in the same account as the thing they protect, which a compromised or mistaken admin credential removes along with everything else.

Part 3 — Backups That Actually Restore

Everyone has backups. The interesting question is what happens in the 40 minutes after you start the restore, and AWS storage has two behaviours that surprise people at exactly that moment.

3.1 EBS and RDS restores are lazy

An EBS snapshot lives in S3. When you restore a volume from it, the volume is available immediately — but blocks are fetched from S3 on first access. The volume reports "available", the database reports "available", and then the first queries touch cold blocks and pay an S3 round trip each.

MEASURED: RESTORE OF A 2 TB RDS (gp3, PostgreSQL) FROM SNAPSHOT

  t+0m00s   restore-db-instance-from-db-snapshot issued
  t+21m14s  status = available          <-- the runbook declared victory here
  t+21m30s  first application query      p50 = 412 ms   (normal p50 = 23 ms)
  t+30m00s  sustained traffic            p50 = 288 ms   p99 = 3,900 ms
  t+61m00s  most working set touched     p50 =  31 ms   p99 =   210 ms
  t+95m00s  fully warm                   p50 =  23 ms   p99 =   180 ms

  "available" to "actually usable"  = 40 minutes of 18x latency
  the RTO the drill claimed          = 21 minutes
  the RTO the customer experienced   = 61 minutes
🔑 Define RTO as "meets the latency SLO", not as "status = available". Options for the gap: Fast Snapshot Restore (FSR) pre-hydrates a snapshot in a chosen AZ so restored volumes get full performance immediately — it is billed per hour per snapshot per AZ (about $0.75/hour, roughly $540/month per snapshot per AZ, so enable it on the DR snapshot only when a drill is scheduled or the RTO genuinely demands it); or restore early and run a warming pass (pg_prewarm, a full table scan) before shifting traffic; or accept the warm-up and write it into the RTO.

3.2 Restore does not restore everything

Restores produce a new resource, and new resources do not inherit the operational furniture of the old one. This is where drills lose their first twenty minutes.

Restore typeWhat you getWhat is NOT restored — you must reapply
RDS snapshot restoreA new instance with a new endpointParameter group choices if not specified, security groups (defaults applied), Multi-AZ setting, tags, CloudWatch alarms, IAM auth config, the endpoint name every client is configured with
DynamoDB PITR / backup restoreA new tableAuto scaling policies, IAM resource policies, CloudWatch alarms, tags, TTL settings, stream settings — and the table name
EBS snapshotA new volume in one AZAttachment, mount, fstab entries, and any encryption key grant if the KMS key differs
S3 versioningPrevious object versions in placeNothing to restore — but deletes leave a delete marker; recovery means removing markers, at scale, in order

The endpoint problem is the one that bites hardest: your application connects to orders.cluster-abc123.eu-west-1.rds.amazonaws.com, and the restore produced orders-restore-2026-08-21.cluster-xyz789.... If the connection string is baked into a task definition, recovery now includes a deploy. The fix is a layer of indirection created before the incident: a Route 53 CNAME (db.internal.example.com) or a Secrets Manager / Parameter Store lookup at connection time, so recovery is a one-record change rather than a release.

3.3 Immutability and isolation

THE BACKUP TOPOLOGY THAT SURVIVES A COMPROMISED ADMIN CREDENTIAL

  ACCOUNT: prod (blast radius)          ACCOUNT: backup-vault (isolated)
  ----------------------------          ---------------------------------
  RDS / Aurora ---- AWS Backup ------->  Vault with VAULT LOCK (compliance)
  DynamoDB     ----   plan       ----->  min retention 35d, max 400d
  EBS          ----             ----->  cross-Region copy to us-west-2
  S3 bucket    ---- replication ----->  bucket with OBJECT LOCK + versioning

  properties enforced by the platform, not by policy:
   - Vault Lock in COMPLIANCE mode cannot be disabled or shortened by ANYONE,
     including the account root, once the 3-day cooling-off window elapses
   - S3 Object Lock in COMPLIANCE mode: no version can be deleted or
     overwritten until its retain-until date, root included
   - the prod account has NO permission to delete in the vault account;
     only to create
⚠️ The 3-day cooling-off window on Vault Lock compliance mode is the setting people skip. Until it elapses, the lock is reversible — which is a feature (you can fix a mistake) and a trap (a team that enabled it on Friday and was breached on Saturday had a reversible lock). Governance mode, by contrast, is reversible forever by anyone holding backup:DisableVaultLock — useful for testing, worth nothing against a compromised admin.

Part 4 — Replication: The RPO You Get, Not the One on the Slide

Every replication option is asynchronous unless it explicitly is not, and asynchronous means your RPO is the replication lag at the moment of failure — which is the worst moment, because failures correlate with load, and lag correlates with load.

MechanismSync?Typical lagRealistic RPOThe catch
RDS Multi-AZ (single standby)Synchronous—Zero (infrastructure failure only)Standby is not readable; failover typically 60–120 s
RDS Multi-AZ DB cluster (2 readable standbys)Semi-sync to onemsNear zeroFailover typically under 35 s; more expensive

Aurora Global DatabaseAsync, storage-levelTypically under 1 sSeconds (unplanned); zero for managed planned failoverReplication is done by the storage layer, so it barely loads the writer — but unplanned promotion still loses the in-flight tail

4.1 Measuring your own lag instead of quoting the docs

MEASURED: AURORA GLOBAL DATABASE, eu-west-1 -> us-east-1, ONE WEEK

  metric: AuroraGlobalDBReplicationLag  (milliseconds)

  quiet period (03:00-06:00)      p50 =   142 ms   p99 =    380 ms
  business hours                  p50 =   310 ms   p99 =  1,240 ms
  nightly batch write (23:00)     p50 = 4,900 ms   p99 = 21,700 ms   <-- !!
  during the Black Friday peak    p50 = 2,100 ms   p99 =  8,400 ms

  the RPO on the design document:  "under 1 second"
  the RPO if we lose the Region at 23:14: up to 21.7 SECONDS of writes

Twenty-one seconds of writes at that hour is roughly 4,000 orders. The design document was not lying — sub-second is the typical figure — it was quoting a median as if it were a guarantee. Alarm on the p99 of replication lag, not the average, and state RPO as "under N seconds at p99, measured weekly", with the batch-window exception written down.

4.2 The last-writer-wins trap in global tables

DynamoDB global tables are multi-active: every replica accepts writes. Conflicts are resolved by last writer wins, using the timestamp assigned by the receiving Region. There is no application-level conflict callback, no vector clock, and no error.

TWO REGIONS, ONE ITEM, 300 ms APART

  t+0ms    eu-west-1: UpdateItem cart#914  ADD items :socks     -> {shoes, socks}
  t+300ms  us-east-1: UpdateItem cart#914  ADD items :hat       -> {shoes, hat}
                      (us-east-1 has not yet seen the socks write)
  t+1.1s   replication settles

  RESULT: {shoes, hat}.  The socks write is gone. No error was returned,
          no metric incremented, no log line written. The customer sees a
          cart that "lost" an item and blames the app.
🔑 Multi-active is a routing decision, not a database feature you switch on. Either pin each entity to a home Region (route by tenant, user or cart ID so a given item is only written in one place — an active/active fleet with single-writer data), or design the item so conflicting writes cannot collide: per-write records instead of mutated sets, distinct attributes per Region, or CRDT-shaped structures you fold at read time. "Just turn on global tables and go active/active" is how you get silent, unattributable data loss.

4.3 S3 replication has no ordering guarantee

CRR replicates objects independently. If your process writes data.parquet and then _SUCCESS, the destination bucket can receive _SUCCESS first — and a consumer in the DR Region will read a manifest that promises data which has not arrived. Replication Time Control gives you a 15-minute SLA and per-object metrics, but not ordering. Design the DR consumer to verify, not to trust the marker: compare object counts, sizes or a checksum manifest before acting.

Part 5 — Failover Mechanics: Static Stability and the Control Plane

Two ideas do most of the work here. The first is that every AWS service has a control plane (create, modify, delete — the APIs) and a data plane (the thing actually serving: running instances, an ALB forwarding packets, S3 GETs). Data planes are engineered for higher availability than control planes and stay up in situations where control planes are impaired.

The second idea follows from the first, and it is the single most useful principle in DR design:

🔑 Static stability: a system should keep working, and recovery should complete, without making any control-plane call to the impaired thing. If your Region failover requires RunInstances in the failover Region, an ACM certificate to be issued, a quota increase to be approved, or an IAM role to be created, then your DR plan has a dependency on exactly the kind of API that is least likely to work under a large correlated event — and on a support queue that will be very busy. Pre-provision, and let the recovery action be a data-plane action: shift weight, flip a routing control, scale an already-warm ASG.
THE SAME FAILOVER, TWO DESIGNS

  DYNAMIC (what most runbooks say)          STATIC (what survives)
  --------------------------------          ------------------------------------
  1. terraform apply in us-west-2           1. capacity already running at 30%
  2. wait for ASG to launch 40 instances    2. ALB + target groups already exist
  3. request quota increase (vCPU limit)    3. quotas already raised, verified
  4. create/validate ACM cert               4. cert issued and attached months ago
  5. update Route 53                        5. flip the ARC routing control
  6. hope RunInstances succeeds in a        6. ASG desired count 30% -> 100%
     Region every other customer is            (a data-plane-ish scaling action

5.1 Failover time is a sum, and DNS is only the first term

"Route 53 fails over in 90 seconds" is true and irrelevant, because clients do not obey it. The real client-observed recovery is the sum of every cache between the health check and the socket.

MEASURED: CLIENT-OBSERVED FAILOVER, BEFORE AND AFTER

  term                                          before        after
  --------------------------------------------------------------------
  health check detection                        90 s          30 s
    (3 failures x 30 s interval)                              (fast: 3 x 10 s)
  Route 53 record TTL                           300 s         60 s
  resolver / OS cache beyond TTL                ~20 s         ~20 s
  JVM DNS cache (networkaddress.cache.ttl)      infinite!     30 s
    - a SecurityManager was installed, so the
      JVM cached the successful lookup FOREVER
  connection pool holding sockets to dead IPs   until app     15 s
    - pools do not re-resolve on their own;       restart     (maxLifetime
      an open TCP connection never checks DNS                  + validation)
  --------------------------------------------------------------------
  TOTAL client-observed recovery                7m12s*        1m35s
  runbook claim                                 90 s          90 s
  * and only because someone restarted the fleet at 7m12s
⚠️ Every cache in that list is a client-side setting, so DR work is application work. Set networkaddress.cache.ttl explicitly (do not inherit it), bound connection lifetime in every pool (maxLifetime in HikariCP, maxIdleTime in most others), enable connection validation, and use a short TTL on the failover record — 60 s costs a rounding error in Route 53 queries and removes 240 s from your RTO. Then measure it from a real client, because the one term you did not think of is the one that dominates.

5.2 Health checks that check the right thing

Health check targetFails over when…Problem
/ or TCP port openThe process is deadMisses a healthy process serving 100% errors because its database is unreachable
Deep check that queries the DBAny dependency is degradedA shared dependency blip fails over every Region at once, or flaps — the classic self-inflicted outage
Composite: shallow liveness + a separate readiness signalThis Region specifically cannot serveThe right answer, and the most work

The rule from probes transfers exactly: a check may only test what its remedy can fix. Regional failover is the remedy, so the check must test something regional. If the payment provider is down globally, failing over to us-east-1 achieves nothing except moving a broken service and adding cross-Region latency to the incident.

5.3 Route 53 ARC, and when it is worth $2,190 a month

Route 53 Application Recovery Controller gives you routing controls — on/off switches for traffic to a cell or Region — whose data plane is distributed across five Regions, so you can flip them during an event in which the Route 53 control plane (us-east-1) is impaired. It also provides readiness checks (is the standby actually configured to take traffic: capacity, quotas, replicas) and safety rules (never let an operator turn off the last healthy cell, or turn on a cell that is not ready).

An ARC cluster is billed at about $3.00/hour — roughly $2,190/month, which makes it the most expensive line item in many DR budgets and one that is entirely correct for a payment system and entirely wrong for an internal tool. The cheap approximation for smaller workloads: Route 53 failover records with health checks pointed at an S3 object you can toggle, giving a manual "big red switch" whose control path is S3, not your application.

Part 6 — Proving It: Game Days, Blast Radius, and Failing Back

6.1 The drill is the design review

A DR plan that has never been executed is a hypothesis. The first drill on this estate was scheduled for two hours and took nine hours and forty-seven minutes; none of the four blocking defects were on anyone's risk list, and none of them were exotic.

DRILL 1 — "EVACUATE eu-west-1", FINDINGS IN ORDER OF DISCOVERY

  t+00:12  FAIL  ACM certificate for the failover ALB did not exist in us-east-1
                 (certs are REGIONAL; CloudFront additionally requires us-east-1)
                 -> issued + DNS validation: 34 minutes
  t+01:05  FAIL  vCPU quota in us-east-1 was the account default; the ASG
                 launched 9 of 40 instances and then InsufficientInstanceCapacity
                 -> quota increase via support: 3h20m (this dominated the drill)
  t+04:40  FAIL  Secrets Manager secrets were not replicated; the app started,
                 could not read DB credentials, crash-looped
                 -> enabled multi-Region secret replication: 18 minutes
  t+05:20  FAIL  ECR images were only in eu-west-1; ECS could not pull
                 -> cross-Region replication rule + re-push: 41 minutes
  t+06:50  OK    application serving; Aurora secondary promoted (RPO 1.4 s)
  t+09:47  OK    all synthetic checks green, drill declared complete

  DRILL 2 (same scenario, 6 weeks later, after fixes):  52 minutes
  DRILL 3 (unannounced, on-call engineer only):         71 minutes
🔑 Every one of those four defects is a static-stability failure — each required a control-plane action (issue, request, replicate, push) at the worst possible time. And note what drill 3 measured that drill 2 could not: the difference between the team that built it running the plan, and one on-call engineer at 03:00 following it. That 19-minute delta is your real RTO.

6.2 Fault injection, safely

Two rules make this safe: every experiment declares a stop condition (a CloudWatch alarm that aborts it automatically — FIS supports this natively), and the first run of anything happens in a pre-production account. The value is not in breaking things; it is in the moment somebody says "that shouldn't have affected checkout" and is wrong.

6.3 Blast radius: cells and shuffle sharding

DR reduces the cost of a disaster. Cell-based architecture reduces the probability that any one customer sees it, by making failures partial instead of total.

SHUFFLE SHARDING, WHY THE ARITHMETIC IS SO GOOD

  8 cells, each tenant assigned to 2 of them  ->  C(8,2) = 28 distinct pairs

  one poisoned tenant takes down BOTH of its cells:
    - tenants sharing BOTH cells (fully down):  1 in 28  = 3.6%
    - tenants sharing ONE cell (degraded, other cell absorbs): 12 in 28 = 43%
    - tenants sharing NEITHER (unaffected):     15 in 28  = 54%

  compare: one shared fleet -> 100% of tenants down
           8 plain (non-shuffled) shards -> 12.5% of tenants fully down

  measured during a real poison-pill incident (a tenant's 400 MB
  request body exhausting worker threads):
    before cells: 100% of tenants saw errors for 22 minutes
    after cells:  3.6% of tenants saw errors for 22 minutes,
                  43% saw elevated latency for 90 seconds

6.4 Failing back is harder than failing over

Failover is a rehearsed emergency. Failback is an unrehearsed reversal in which the two sides have diverged, and it is where drills usually stop and real incidents do not.

Failback questionWhy it is hardDesign answer
Where is the authoritative data now?The DR Region took writes; the original has an older, partially-replicated copyDeclare the DR Region primary and reverse replication — do not "sync back"
Did the original Region lose writes?Unpromoted replicas may hold writes the new primary never sawSnapshot the old primary before anything reconnects; reconcile offline
When do we go back?Pressure to return "to normal" while the Region is still recoveringNever during the incident. Schedule failback as a planned change, with its own runbook
Do we need to go back at all?Symmetric design means "primary" is just a labelBuild symmetric, then failback is the next scheduled failover — the best possible answer
💡 The strongest DR posture is one where failover is boring because it happens on purpose. Teams that fail over between Regions on a schedule — monthly, in business hours, announced — never discover a certificate, quota or credential problem during an actual outage, because the plan is exercised more often than it is needed. It also removes the worst failure mode of all: hesitation, because nobody is sure the failover will work.

❌ A DR plan consisting of Multi-AZ everywhere, nightly snapshots in the production account, a Route 53 record with a 300-second TTL, an untested four-hour RTO on a wiki page last edited eighteen months ago, and cross-Region replication described as a backup. Multi-AZ covers 14% of the incidents this system has actually had, and none of the top three causes. The snapshots share an account with the workload, so the credential that deletes production deletes them too. The 300-second TTL is the smallest term in a failover budget dominated by a JVM that caches DNS forever and a connection pool that never re-resolves, so the true recovery is seven minutes and only after a fleet restart. The four-hour RTO is a wish: the first real attempt will spend three hours and twenty minutes in a support queue for a vCPU quota nobody raised in the failover Region, and will then fail again on an ACM certificate that does not exist there, on secrets that were never replicated, and on container images that live in one Region's registry. And the replica is not a backup — when a migration deletes 40,000 rows, the replica deletes them too, perfectly, in under a second.

✅ Written RTO/RPO per workload tier, signed by someone who owns the revenue; backups that are older, immutable, isolated and proven; static stability everywhere in the recovery path; failover time measured from a real client; and a drill on the calendar. Tier the estate so the payment API gets active/active and analytics gets Backup & Restore — the same money buys a better RTO where it matters. Push backups into a separate account behind Vault Lock in compliance mode, past its three-day cooling-off window, with S3 Object Lock on the object copies. Pre-provision the failover Region completely: quotas raised and verified by a readiness check, certificates issued, secrets replicated, images replicated, capacity already running at 30% — so recovery is "scale up and flip a routing control", not "create things during an event". Set the DNS TTL to 60 s and pin networkaddress.cache.ttl, bound pool connection lifetime, and measure the total from a client rather than from Route 53. Alarm on the p99 of replication lag, not the mean, and state the RPO as a measured number with its batch-window exception. Then drill it: announced first, unannounced second, with the on-call engineer driving — and treat the delta between those two numbers as the RTO you actually have.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is the difference between RTO and RPO, and can you give an example where one is small and the other is large?

RTO is how long the service may be unavailable — measured from the failure to service restored. RPO is how much data you may lose — measured from the last durable copy back to the failure. They are independent axes and are bought with different things: RTO with standby capacity, automation and rehearsal; RPO with replication frequency and synchronicity.

Small RTO, large RPO: a stateless marketing site restored from a nightly S3 sync — up in 3 minutes, but any content published since last night is gone (RPO 24 h). Large RTO, small RPO: an Aurora Global Database with no automation — the secondary is within a second of the writer (RPO ~1 s), but promotion is manual and the runbook has never been used, so the RTO is however long it takes to page someone and have them work it out.

The senior addition: quote both as measured figures with a drill date. An RTO that has never been executed is not a number, it is an intention — the first drill on our estate turned a claimed 4 hours into a measured 9h47m.

Beginner: Name the four DR strategies and what is running in the DR Region for each.
StrategyRunning in DRRPO / RTOGood fit

The one thing to add unprompted: Warm Standby's real advantage over Pilot Light is not the compute, it is that a warm copy is continuously proven. Something that serves synthetic traffic every minute cannot silently rot, whereas a pilot light discovers its missing certificate during the incident.

Mid: Your production database is Multi-AZ with cross-Region read replicas and hourly snapshots. An engineer runs a migration that drops a column with three years of data. Walk through the recovery.

First, state the thing the question is testing: none of the three mechanisms listed protects against this. Multi-AZ applied the DDL synchronously. The cross-Region replica applied it within seconds. Both copies are perfect and both are wrong. Only the snapshot — an older copy — has the data.

The recovery, in order:

  1. Stop the bleeding: block writes to the affected tables or take the service into a degraded read-only mode. Every minute of new writes is a minute you must reconcile later.
  2. Fix the time. Get the exact timestamp of the migration from the audit trail (CloudTrail, the database log, the deploy record). This is the restore target.
  3. Choose the mechanism by cost of downtime. Aurora MySQL with Backtrack enabled: rewind the cluster to seconds before the migration, in minutes, without a restore — but it rewinds everything, so you lose legitimate writes made since. RDS/Aurora PITR: restore to a new cluster at T-1s; the production cluster keeps running, and you then extract and merge the missing column. Snapshot restore: same shape but coarser (up to an hour of data missing from the copy).
  4. Prefer restore-beside over restore-in-place when the damage is scoped to a table or column. Restore to a parallel cluster, extract the lost data, backfill into production. Downtime stays near zero and no legitimate write is lost. Restore-in-place is for damage so broad that reconciliation is not feasible.
  5. Reconcile and verify: row counts, checksums against the restored copy, and a business-level check (yesterday's revenue total) before resuming writes.

Then the prevention, which is what the interviewer actually wants: migrations that drop data should be two-phase (stop using the column, deploy, wait a retention period, then drop), destructive DDL should require a snapshot immediately prior as part of the pipeline, and production credentials for schema changes should be gated. And the general rule: replication faithfully replicates mistakes; only an older copy is a backup.

Mid: The runbook says failover takes 90 seconds. In a drill, customers saw errors for over seven minutes. Where did the time go, and how do you fix it?

Ninety seconds is the Route 53 term — health check detection plus the record change. The client-observed recovery is a sum of every cache between that record and the socket:

TermTypical defaultFix
Health check detection3 failures × 30 s = 90 sFast health checks (3 × 10 s = 30 s), accepting more sensitivity
Record TTL300 s60 s on failover records; the extra query cost is negligible
Downstream resolver cachesHonours TTL, roughlyNothing you control — budget ~20 s
JVM DNS cache30 s normally; forever when a SecurityManager is installedSet networkaddress.cache.ttl explicitly; never inherit it
Connection poolsNever re-resolve; an established TCP connection has no DNS to checkBound maxLifetime (e.g. 15 s during an incident, 30 min normally), enable validation, and make the app react to connection errors by rebuilding the pool

In our measurement the JVM cache and the pool were the entire problem — the fleet only recovered when someone restarted it at 7m12s. After pinning the DNS TTL, bounding pool lifetime and switching to fast health checks, the same drill measured 1m35s.

The generalisation worth saying out loud: DNS failover is an application-configuration problem wearing an infrastructure costume. And you cannot know your failover time from the AWS side — it must be measured from a client that behaves like your clients.

Senior: Design DR for a payments platform: 12,000 transactions/minute at peak, $180k/hour of downtime, a regulator requiring seven-year immutable retention. Justify every choice.

Start with the numbers, not the architecture. $180k/hour means one hour of downtime pays for roughly two years of a full second Region. So the usual "active/active is too expensive" argument is inverted here — but only for the payment path. Tier ruthlessly: the authorisation path gets active/active, the merchant dashboard gets Warm Standby, reporting and analytics get Backup & Restore. Uniform protection would waste money on the dashboard and under-protect the path that earns.

Targets: authorisation path RTO under 60 s, RPO zero for committed transactions — and note that in payments, RPO zero is not negotiable in the way RTO is: a lost authorisation is a reconciliation dispute, a regulatory issue and a customer charged twice.

Data, with the conflict story stated: RPO zero rules out async-only replication for the authoritative ledger. Two workable shapes:

  • Regionally partitioned writes (preferred): route each merchant to a home Region by merchant ID. Each Region is authoritative for its own merchants, replicated to the other for read/standby. There is no cross-Region write conflict by construction, and evacuation means moving a merchant set, which is a routing change. Cost: an evacuated merchant's in-flight tail is bounded by replication lag, so pair it with (b).
  • Durable intake ahead of the ledger: accept authorisations into a Region-local, replicated, append-only log before they touch the ledger, with an idempotency key per transaction. A Region loss then loses at most what has not been acknowledged, and replaying is safe because every write is idempotent. This is what actually delivers "RPO zero" in practice — not a database setting, but an idempotent write path plus a durable intake.

Failover mechanism: Route 53 ARC routing controls (the $2,190/month is 44 seconds of downtime — trivially justified here), with readiness checks proving the standby's quotas, capacity and replica health continuously, and safety rules preventing an operator from disabling the last healthy cell. Both Regions run real traffic, so failover is a weight change against a proven path, not an activation.

Static stability: each Region runs enough capacity to take 100% of peak alone. That means ~50% average utilisation, which the cost review will attack — the answer is the queueing argument from Well-Architected plus the $180k/hour. No recovery step may issue a certificate, request a quota or create a role.

Regulatory retention: seven years, immutable — S3 with Object Lock in compliance mode and a seven-year retain-until date, in a separate account, replicated to a second Region, with Glacier Deep Archive transitions for cost. Compliance mode specifically, because governance mode is reversible by anyone with the permission, which is not what a regulator means by immutable. Note the Vault Lock cooling-off period for AWS Backup vaults and confirm it has elapsed.

Blast radius: cells with shuffle sharding by merchant, so a poison-pill merchant takes out 1 in 28 rather than everyone. This buys more real availability than the second Region does, because poison pills happen and Region losses mostly do not.

Proof: scheduled monthly failover in business hours (which makes failover boring), quarterly unannounced drills driven by on-call, continuous restore testing of the immutable copies — a backup nobody has restored is not evidence for a regulator either.

What I would deliberately not do: synchronous cross-Region database replication. The inter-Region round trip is added to every commit, and it makes the remote Region a hard dependency of the local write path — you have converted two independent failure domains into one, which is the opposite of the goal.

Senior: You inherit a system whose DR plan has never been tested and whose owner insists it works. You have one week. What do you do, and in what order?

Do not argue about the plan; measure it. An untested plan is a hypothesis, and the cheapest way to end the disagreement is evidence. The order matters, because each step is cheaper than the next and any of them can end the exercise.

DayActionWhy this order
1Inventory the state. Every stateful store, its backup mechanism, retention, account and Region. One page.Costs hours, needs no permission, and typically finds one store with no backup at all — which ends the debate immediately
1Restore one thing. Take the most important database, restore it into a sandbox, run a real query, record the wall-clock time.The single highest-information action available. It is non-disruptive and produces the first true number
2Readiness audit of the failover Region: quotas, certificates, secrets, images, IAM roles, capacity. Compare against what the plan assumes exists.These are the four defects that dominated our first drill; all are checkable from a laptop with read-only access
3Measure replication lag p99 over a full day including the batch window, and compare with the stated RPO.Observational; usually shows the stated RPO is a median quoted as a guarantee
4Trace one failover path end to end on paper: health check → TTL → client DNS cache → connection pool. Note every default.Finds the JVM/pool terms that dominate real recovery, without touching production
5Tabletop with on-call, not with the authors. Read the runbook aloud and stop at every step that names a person, a console click or a resource that must be created.Surfaces the difference between the plan working and the plan being executable at 03:00
End of weekOne page: measured RTO/RPO versus stated, ranked by gap ÷ effort, with a date for the first real drill.Turns "it works" / "it doesn't" into a prioritised list — and the ranking almost always puts one-hour fixes at the top

Two things about how to run this politically: never present it as an audit of the owner — present the restore timing and the readiness gaps as facts, and let them draw the conclusion. And expect the top-ranked fixes to be tiny: raise a quota, replicate a secret, pin a DNS TTL, create a CNAME. On our estate the difference between a failed 4h11m drill and a clean 52-minute one was about three engineer-days of work, none of it architectural.

6 (hard): Recover from logical corruption that every replica faithfully copied: a migration deleted 40,000 rows across two tables at a known time, and it is now 90 minutes later with production still taking writes. Produce the recovery, the timeline, and the prevention.
Solution

This is the incident where Multi-AZ, cross-Region replicas and global tables all contribute nothing — they replicated the damage in milliseconds. Only an older copy helps, and the design question is which older copy costs the least to use.

STEP 0 — STOP THE BLEEDING (first 4 minutes, before any restore)

  - put the affected service into read-only mode (feature flag), so the
    reconciliation set stops growing
  - DO NOT roll back the deploy blindly: the migration is applied; a rollback
    that re-runs DDL can make the damage worse
  - capture the exact time from CloudTrail / the DB log / the deploy record:
      2026-08-21T09:41:07Z   ALTER TABLE ... / DELETE FROM ...
  - snapshot the CURRENT state before touching anything. You are about to
    change things; you want a way back to "damaged but known".

🔑 Restore beside, backfill idempotently, verify at the business level. The instinct under pressure is to restore in place and swap tables, which loses every write since the damage and turns a data-loss incident into two. ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING (or its equivalent — conditional writes in DynamoDB, INSERT IGNORE in MySQL) makes the repair safe to re-run and impossible to make worse, which is exactly what you want at 12:20 with a director watching.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • HA survives a component dying; DR restores state after something destroys it. They are separate budgets — Multi-AZ covered 2 of 14 real incidents (14%), and 79% needed neither AZ nor Region redundancy, only better change safety and limits.
  • Replication is not backup, because it is fast and faithful — an accidental DELETE reached the standby in 11 ms and the cross-Region secondary in 0.9 s. A backup must be older, immutable, isolated and proven; a copy missing any of the four is storage.
  • Derive the strategy from written RTO/RPO and the cost of downtime, never the reverse — and tier the estate: the tiered design recovered the important tier faster for less money than one uniform strategy.
  • The only RTO that exists is the measured one. A designed "4 hours" took 9h47m on the first attempt and 52 minutes after roughly three engineer-days of fixes — none of them architectural.
  • Define RTO as "meets the latency SLO", not "status = available". A 2 TB restore reported available at 21m14s and then served 18× latency for 40 minutes while EBS hydrated blocks lazily from S3; Fast Snapshot Restore (~$0.75/hour per snapshot per AZ) or a warming pass buys that back.
  • Restores do not restore the furniture — Multi-AZ, retention, alarms, auto scaling policies, TTL and stream settings, and above all the endpoint. Add indirection (internal CNAME or a secret resolved at connect time) before the incident, or recovery includes a deploy.
  • Backups belong in a separate account behind Vault Lock in compliance mode (past its three-day cooling-off window) with S3 Object Lock — governance mode is reversible by anyone holding the permission, which is worth nothing against a compromised admin.
  • Your RPO is replication lag at the moment of failure, and failures correlate with load. Aurora Global measured p50 310 ms in business hours but 21.7 s during the nightly batch — roughly 4,000 orders. State RPO as a p99 with named exceptions; alarm on p99, not the mean.
  • DynamoDB global tables resolve conflicts by last writer wins, silently — no error, no metric, no log. Multi-active is a routing decision: pin entities to a home Region, or model data so writes cannot collide.
  • S3 CRR has no ordering guarantee, so a _SUCCESS marker can arrive before its data. Verify counts or checksums in the destination; RTC gives a 15-minute SLA, not ordering; replication metrics are off by default.
  • Static stability: recovery must not call the control plane of the broken thing. Every one of the four drill-blocking defects — missing certificate, unraised quota, unreplicated secrets, single-Region images — was a control-plane action attempted at the worst moment; one of them cost 3h20m in a support queue.
  • Failover time is a sum: health check + TTL + resolver + client DNS cache + connection pool. The measured 7m12s against a 90-second claim was entirely the last two terms; fixing networkaddress.cache.ttl and pool maxLifetime brought it to 1m35s with no infrastructure change.
  • A health check may only test what its remedy can fix: regional failover needs a regional signal, or one shared-dependency blip fails over every Region at once.
  • Route 53 ARC gives routing controls with a five-Region data plane, readiness checks and safety rules for ~$2,190/month — obviously right for payments, obviously wrong for internal tooling; the cheap approximation is failover records driven by a togglable health check.
  • Cells with shuffle sharding cut blast radius superlinearly: 8 cells, 2 per tenant, gives 28 pairs — a poison-pill tenant fully affected 3.6% of tenants instead of 100%.
  • Drill announced, then unannounced with on-call driving. The 52 → 71 minute delta was documentation and permission-to-act, not engineering — the cheapest RTO reduction available, and the number worth publishing.
  • Failback is the unrehearsed half. Reverse replication rather than "syncing back", snapshot the old primary before anything reconnects, and never fail back during the incident. Symmetric designs make failback just the next scheduled failover.
  • For logical corruption: restore beside, backfill idempotently, verify at the business level. Restoring in place would have lost the 6,204 legitimate writes made after the damage; ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING made the repair safe to re-run and impossible to worsen — zero data lost, 89 minutes read-only.
  • Rehearse logical damage, not only Region loss — it is far more common, and it is the drill almost nobody runs.
