Connecting VPCs, PrivateLink & On-Prem Networking

What you'll master: the question that picks the technology — do you need network reachability or service reachability? — and why answering "service" makes overlapping CIDRs, transitive routing and half the mesh disappear; VPC peering and the non-transitivity that surprises everyone, plus the n(n−1)/2 arithmetic that makes a mesh collapse under its own weight; Transit Gateway as a hub whose route tables are the isolation proof an auditor can read, and whose $0.05/hour per attachment is not free; PrivateLink, which puts an ENI from your CIDR in front of someone else's service so two VPCs with identical address ranges can talk — along with the private-DNS override that silently breaks the public endpoint for an entire VPC; Site-to-Site VPN versus Direct Connect, where DX carries no encryption at all and a single connection has no SLA; and the failure that dominates real hybrid networks — the packets get through and the name does not resolve. Prerequisites: VPCs, Subnets & Security Groups and Cost: Pricing Models & Data Transfer.

Part 1 — Ask Which Kind of Reachability You Need

Every connectivity option below answers one of two completely different questions, and picking the wrong category is what produces the designs people regret. Ask first, then choose.

Part 2 — Peering, and the Word "Transitive"

A peering connection is a route between two VPCs. It is free of hourly charges, has no bandwidth bottleneck, and is the cheapest way to connect two VPCs that exchange a lot of data. It also has one property that catches every engineer exactly once.

$ aws ec2 create-vpc-peering-connection --vpc-id vpc-0app --peer-vpc-id vpc-0data \
    --query 'VpcPeeringConnection.VpcPeeringConnectionId' --output text
pcx-0a1b2c3d
$ aws ec2 accept-vpc-peering-connection --vpc-peering-connection-id pcx-0a1b2c3d

  ...and it does nothing yet. A peering connection is a POSSIBILITY, not a route.
  You must add routes on BOTH sides, and security groups on both sides:

$ aws ec2 create-route --route-table-id rtb-app  --destination-cidr-block 10.40.0.0/16 \
    --vpc-peering-connection-id pcx-0a1b2c3d
$ aws ec2 create-route --route-table-id rtb-data --destination-cidr-block 10.0.0.0/16 \
    --vpc-peering-connection-id pcx-0a1b2c3d

NON-TRANSITIVITY, demonstrated:

    vpc-A (10.0.0.0/16)  ←pcx-1→  vpc-B (10.20.0.0/16)  ←pcx-2→  vpc-C (10.40.0.0/16)

$ # from an instance in A, reaching B:
$ ping -c1 -W2 10.20.1.14
64 bytes from 10.20.1.14: icmp_seq=1 ttl=254 time=0.71 ms        ✅

$ # from the same instance, reaching C:
$ ping -c1 -W2 10.40.1.9
--- 10.40.1.9 ping statistics ---
1 packets transmitted, 0 received, 100% packet loss                ❌

$ aws ec2 create-route --route-table-id rtb-A --destination-cidr-block 10.40.0.0/16 \
    --vpc-peering-connection-id pcx-1
An error occurred (InvalidParameterValue): route table contains an unsupported route
   → you cannot even ASK for it. B will not forward between two of its peerings.
⚠️ The same restriction has three other faces people meet separately and never connect: you cannot route through a peering to reach the peer's Internet Gateway (no "shared NAT" across a peering), you cannot reach the peer's NAT Gateway, and you cannot use the peer's gateway endpoints. All four are the same rule: a peering carries traffic between the two VPCs, and nothing else. It is a wire, not a router.
THE MESH ARITHMETIC — why peering stops scaling:

    connections = n(n−1)/2, and each needs routes on BOTH sides

     4 VPCs →   6 connections →   12 route entries
    10 VPCs →  45 connections →   90 route entries
    20 VPCs → 190 connections →  380 route entries      ← and every new VPC adds 19 more
    40 VPCs → 780 connections → 1,560 route entries

  quota: 125 active peering connections per VPC.

The numbers are not the real problem — the change cost is. Adding the 21st VPC to a 20-VPC mesh means twenty new peering connections and forty route entries, in twenty accounts, each needing an accept and each a chance to be forgotten. That is what a Transit Gateway removes.

Part 3 — Transit Gateway: A Hub With Readable Isolation

$ TGW=$(aws ec2 create-transit-gateway --description "core hub" \
    --options DefaultRouteTableAssociation=disable,DefaultRouteTablePropagation=disable \
    --query 'TransitGateway.TransitGatewayId' --output text)
tgw-0a1b2c3d

  ⚠️ Disabling the two defaults is the single most important choice here.
     Leave them enabled and EVERY attachment can reach EVERY other attachment
     the moment it is created — which is the opposite of why you bought a TGW.

SEGMENTATION — separate route tables are the whole point:

$ aws ec2 create-transit-gateway-route-table --transit-gateway-id $TGW  # ×3: prod, nonprod, shared

  associate each attachment with ONE route table (where its traffic is looked up)
  propagate  each attachment into  route tables (where its CIDR becomes reachable)

  prod-rt        ← prod VPCs associated;  prod + shared propagated
  nonprod-rt     ← dev/staging associated; nonprod + shared propagated
  shared-rt      ← shared services + on-prem; everything propagated

$ aws ec2 associate-transit-gateway-route-table \
    --transit-gateway-route-table-id tgw-rtb-prod --transit-gateway-attachment-id tgw-attach-0prod
$ aws ec2 enable-transit-gateway-route-table-propagation \
    --transit-gateway-route-table-id tgw-rtb-prod --transit-gateway-attachment-id tgw-attach-0shared
🔑 "Prod's route table has no route to dev" is an isolation proof you can read in one screen, and an auditor can verify without understanding your application. That is worth more than the convenience: it converts a claim about security groups scattered across forty accounts into a single routing fact. Compare search-transit-gateway-routes output against the intended matrix in CI and you have continuous evidence.
$ aws ec2 search-transit-gateway-routes --transit-gateway-route-table-id tgw-rtb-prod \
    --filters Name=type,Values=static,propagated --query 'Routes[].[DestinationCidrBlock,State]' --output text
10.0.0.0/16     active                             ← prod
10.1.0.0/16     active                             ← prod
10.90.0.0/16    active                             ← shared services
                                                   ← NO 10.20.x (nonprod). Provable.

THE COST NOBODY BUDGETS — a TGW is not free, and the attachment charge is per attachment:

  30 VPC attachments × $0.05/hr × 730 h               = $ 1,095 / month
  data processing     60 TB × $0.02/GB                = $ 1,200 / month
                                                        ─────────
                                                        $ 2,295 / month, before transfer

  vs peering for the same 60 TB between two heavy VPCs:
  cross-AZ transfer only                              = $   600 / month

Which gives the practical hybrid that large accounts converge on: a Transit Gateway for everything, plus a direct peering for the one or two pairs that move serious volume. The TGW provides reachability, segmentation and the on-prem attachment; the peering shortcut carries the elephant flow at half the per-GB rate and no attachment fee. Longest-prefix match makes the peering win automatically for that pair, with no application change.

💡 Appliance mode is the detail that decides whether a firewall inspection design works. Without it, a TGW picks the AZ path per flow direction independently, so a request can enter via the appliance in AZ-a and its reply return via AZ-b — a stateful firewall sees half a conversation and drops it, producing intermittent failures that look like packet loss. Enable it on the appliance's VPC attachment (ApplianceModeSupport=enable) and the TGW keeps both directions on the same AZ. Symptoms without it are maddening: mostly working, failing under load, never reproducible.

Part 4 — PrivateLink: Service Reachability, Overlap Welcome

PrivateLink inverts the model. Instead of joining two networks, it places an ENI in your subnet, with an address from your CIDR, that forwards to a Network Load Balancer in someone else's VPC. Your VPC never learns their addresses; they never learn yours.

PROVIDER — expose a service behind an NLB:
$ aws ec2 create-vpc-endpoint-service-configuration \
    --network-load-balancer-arns arn:aws:elasticloadbalancing:us-east-1:1111:loadbalancer/net/orders/ab \
    --acceptance-required \
    --query 'ServiceConfiguration.ServiceName' --output text
com.amazonaws.vpce.us-east-1.vpce-svc-0f3c9a1e5b2d

$ aws ec2 modify-vpc-endpoint-service-permissions \
    --service-id vpce-svc-0f3c9a1e5b2d --add-allowed-principals arn:aws:iam::222233334444:root

CONSUMER — create an interface endpoint, in a VPC with an IDENTICAL CIDR:
$ aws ec2 describe-vpcs --vpc-ids vpc-0consumer --query 'Vpcs[0].CidrBlock' --output text
10.0.0.0/16                                        ← the provider VPC is ALSO 10.0.0.0/16

$ aws ec2 create-vpc-endpoint --vpc-id vpc-0consumer --vpc-endpoint-type Interface \
    --service-name com.amazonaws.vpce.us-east-1.vpce-svc-0f3c9a1e5b2d \
    --subnet-ids subnet-0a subnet-0b --security-group-ids sg-0endpoint \
    --query 'VpcEndpoint.[VpcEndpointId,State]' --output text
vpce-0d4e8f1a   pendingAcceptance

$ aws ec2 describe-vpc-endpoints --vpc-endpoint-ids vpce-0d4e8f1a \
    --query 'VpcEndpoints[0].DnsEntries[0].DnsName' --output text
vpce-0d4e8f1a-x7f2.vpce-svc-0f3c9a1e5b2d.us-east-1.vpce.amazonaws.com

$ dig +short vpce-0d4e8f1a-x7f2....vpce.amazonaws.com
10.0.2.171                                         ← an address in the CONSUMER's CIDR
10.0.3.88
$ curl -s https://vpce-0d4e8f1a-x7f2....vpce.amazonaws.com/orders/42 | jq -r .status
shipped                                            ✅ two VPCs, one CIDR, working
🔑 Overlapping CIDRs are not a problem PrivateLink tolerates — they are a problem it does not have. There is no route between the VPCs, so there is nothing to conflict. This is the answer to "we acquired a company and everything is 10.0.0.0/16", to "the vendor will not renumber", and to every case where two teams need one API call and neither wants a shared address plan. It is also one-way: the provider cannot initiate a connection back to you, which is exactly what you want from a counterparty you do not fully trust.

THE PRIVATE-DNS TRAP — one checkbox, VPC-wide blast radius:

$ aws ec2 modify-vpc-endpoint --vpc-endpoint-id vpce-0kms --private-dns-enabled
   → this overrides `kms.us-east-1.amazonaws.com` for the ENTIRE VPC.

Now, an instance in a subnet with NO route to that endpoint's subnet:
$ dig +short kms.us-east-1.amazonaws.com
10.0.2.44                                          ← the endpoint's private IP
$ aws kms list-keys
Connect timeout on endpoint URL: "https://kms.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/"
   → DNS resolves VPC-wide, but only some subnets can REACH the endpoint ENIs.
     The public endpoint is no longer available to fall back to. Everything
     in the VPC that used KMS just broke, including things you did not change.

✅ the fixes, in order of preference:
  1. put endpoint ENIs in a subnet every workload can reach, in EVERY AZ you use
  2. verify the SG on the endpoint allows 443 from the whole VPC CIDR (a default-deny
     SG on an endpoint is the second-most-common cause of this exact timeout)
  3. if you cannot, leave private DNS OFF and use the endpoint-specific DNS name

$ aws ec2 describe-vpc-endpoints --vpc-endpoint-ids vpce-0kms \
    --query 'VpcEndpoints[0].[PrivateDnsEnabled,SubnetIds,Groups[].GroupId]' --output json
[ true, [ "subnet-0a" ], [ "sg-0endpoint" ] ]      ← ONE subnet, one AZ. Fix that first.

⚠️ Direct Connect is not encrypted. It is a private circuit, not a secure one — the traffic is in the clear on someone else's fibre and through someone else's cross-connect facility. Auditors and regulators routinely treat "it's a dedicated line" as insufficient, and they are right. Either run MACsec (available on dedicated 10/100 Gbps ports at supported locations) or run an IPsec VPN over the DX, which is the common answer and costs you some throughput to the tunnel's ceiling. This is one of the most reliable senior interview questions in the whole hybrid-networking area, and "DX is private so it's secure" is the wrong answer.
THE STANDARD RESILIENT PATTERN — DX primary, VPN backup, BGP chooses:

    on-prem router ──DX (primary, low latency, cheap egress)──┐
                   └─VPN over internet (backup)───────────────┴── TGW ── all VPCs

$ aws ec2 create-vpn-connection --type ipsec.1 --customer-gateway-id cgw-0a1b \
    --transit-gateway-id tgw-0a1b2c3d --options EnableAcceleration=false

  BGP does the failover. Influence the choice explicitly rather than hoping:
    - AS-PATH prepending on the VPN advertisement makes it less preferred
    - a more specific prefix on DX wins outright (longest prefix match beats everything)
    - AWS prefers, in order: longest prefix → static/DX over VPN → shorter AS_PATH

$ aws directconnect describe-connections \
    --query 'connections[].[connectionId,location,bandwidth,connectionState]' --output text
dxcon-fg1a2b3c   EqDC2    10Gbps   available
   → ONE connection at ONE location. No SLA applies.
     AWS's 99.99% DX SLA requires two connections at TWO different locations,
     and the 99.9% tier requires two at one location. A single circuit is a
     maintenance window away from an outage.

The cost case for DX is usually egress, not bandwidth. At ~$0.02/GB versus ~$0.09/GB over the internet, a workload pushing 100 TB a month to on-prem saves roughly $7,000 a month — which pays for redundant ports comfortably. Below a few TB, a VPN is the right answer and provisions in minutes.

Part 6 — The Failure That Actually Happens: DNS

Once routing works, hybrid connectivity breaks in one place far more often than any other: names. The packets have a path; the resolver does not know the name; and the error looks nothing like a network problem.

$ ping db.corp.internal
ping: db.corp.internal: Name or service not known         ← NOT a timeout. A resolution failure.
$ ping 10.200.4.19                                        # the same host, by address
64 bytes from 10.200.4.19: icmp_seq=1 ttl=61 time=2.14 ms ✅ the NETWORK is fine
DirectionWhat you needWhy
AWS → on-prem namesOutbound Resolver endpoint + forwarding ruleThe VPC resolver has never heard of corp.internal
On-prem → AWS private namesInbound Resolver endpointOn-prem DNS needs an address in your VPC to forward to
VPC → another VPC's private zoneAssociate the private hosted zone with that VPCPHZs are per-VPC, and routing does not change that
Many VPCs → one shared zoneResolver rules shared via RAMAssociating every zone with every VPC does not scale
$ OUT=$(aws route53resolver create-resolver-endpoint --direction OUTBOUND \
    --name to-corp --security-group-ids sg-0resolver \
    --ip-addresses SubnetId=subnet-0a SubnetId=subnet-0b \
    --query 'ResolverEndpoint.Id' --output text)

$ RULE=$(aws route53resolver create-resolver-rule --rule-type FORWARD \
    --domain-name corp.internal --name corp-forward --resolver-endpoint-id $OUT \
    --target-ips Ip=10.200.0.10,Port=53 Ip=10.200.0.11,Port=53 \
    --query 'ResolverRule.Id' --output text)

$ aws route53resolver associate-resolver-rule --resolver-rule-id $RULE --vpc-id vpc-0app

$ dig +short db.corp.internal
10.200.4.19                                        ✅

Share the rule org-wide instead of repeating it per VPC:
$ aws ram create-resource-share --name resolver-rules \
    --resource-arns arn:aws:route53resolver:us-east-1:1111:resolver-rule/$RULE \
    --principals arn:aws:organizations::1111:organization/o-abc123
💡 Two Resolver details worth carrying. Cost: endpoints bill per IP address, per hour (~$0.125), and each endpoint needs at least two for availability — about $180/month per endpoint pair, so an inbound and an outbound pair is ~$360/month before queries. People are startled by that. Order: resolver rules match on the most specific domain, and a rule for corp.internal is overridden by one for db.corp.internal — which is how you carve exceptions, and how someone accidentally black-holes one hostname while the rest of the zone works fine.

❌ A peering mesh grown to twenty VPCs, a Transit Gateway with default association and propagation left on, peering used where one API call was needed, private DNS enabled on an endpoint that exists in one AZ, a single Direct Connect circuit treated as both redundant and encrypted, and hybrid DNS that was never configured in either direction. Adding the twenty-first VPC means twenty new peering connections and forty route entries across twenty accounts, and somebody will forget one. The Transit Gateway that was supposed to segment prod from dev was created with the defaults, so every attachment reaches every other one and the isolation exists only in a diagram. A team that needed to call one vendor API got a peering instead, permanently coupling two address plans and granting network-wide reachability in both directions. Someone enabled private DNS on a KMS endpoint that lives in a single subnet, so the name now resolves VPC-wide to an address most subnets cannot reach and every workload using KMS broke at once, including ones nobody touched. The single DX circuit carries no encryption, has no applicable SLA, and its next maintenance window is an outage. And when the network finally works, nothing can resolve corp.internal, which gets escalated to the network team as "the VPN is down".

✅ Hub-and-spoke with explicit route tables, PrivateLink for service access, endpoints in every AZ, DX plus VPN with encryption, and DNS designed as a first-class part of connectivity. Move to a Transit Gateway before the mesh does, and create it with DefaultRouteTableAssociation=disable,DefaultRouteTablePropagation=disable so isolation is the default rather than an afterthought — then diff search-transit-gateway-routes against the intended matrix in CI so the proof stays true. Keep one direct peering for any pair moving serious volume, because longest-prefix match will use it automatically at half the per-GB rate and no attachment fee. When the requirement is "call this API", use PrivateLink: one ENI, one direction, no address-space coupling, and overlapping CIDRs stop being a conversation. Put interface endpoints in every AZ you use, allow 443 from the VPC CIDR on their security group, and understand that enabling private DNS overrides the name for the whole VPC. Run MACsec or IPsec over Direct Connect — private is not encrypted — and buy two circuits at two locations if the 99.99% SLA is meant to apply. Finally, build inbound and outbound Resolver endpoints with forwarding rules shared through RAM at the same time as the routing, because "the network is up and the name does not resolve" is the single most common hybrid failure and it is entirely preventable.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What does it mean that VPC peering is "not transitive"?

If VPC A is peered with B, and B is peered with C, then A cannot reach C. Traffic only ever flows between the two VPCs a peering directly connects; B will not forward between two of its own peerings. AWS does not merely drop such traffic — it refuses to let you create the route at all, returning InvalidParameterValue: route table contains an unsupported route.

The same rule explains three other things people hit separately: you cannot use the peer's Internet Gateway, you cannot use the peer's NAT Gateway, and you cannot use the peer's gateway endpoints. A peering is a wire between two VPCs, not a router. When you need transitivity — or on-prem connectivity, which is inherently transitive — the answer is a Transit Gateway.

Beginner: When would you use PrivateLink instead of peering?

When you need service reachability rather than network reachability — which is most of the time that someone asks for peering.

Use PrivateLink whenUse peering/TGW when
One app needs to call one endpointYou own both sides and want them to be one network
The counterparty is a vendor or another teamBroad access across many services
The CIDRs overlapAddress plans are already coordinated
The connection must be one-wayBoth sides initiate

The property that decides it most often is overlap: PrivateLink puts an ENI with an address from your CIDR in front of the service, so there is no route between the VPCs and therefore nothing to conflict. Two VPCs both on 10.0.0.0/16 can talk. It is also unidirectional — the provider cannot call back into your VPC — which is the right default for anything you do not control.

Mid: You enabled private DNS on a VPC endpoint and unrelated workloads started failing. Explain.

Private DNS overrides the service's public hostname for the entire VPC. Once enabled on, say, a KMS endpoint, kms.us-east-1.amazonaws.com resolves to the endpoint's private addresses for every instance in the VPC — and the public endpoint is no longer available as a fallback.

That is fine if every subnet can reach the endpoint ENIs. It is an outage if the endpoint exists in one subnet, one AZ, or behind a security group that does not allow 443 from the VPC CIDR. Workloads in other subnets resolve the name successfully and then time out connecting — and crucially, this breaks things nobody deployed, because the change is VPC-wide while the deployment was scoped to one service.

CheckExpected
describe-vpc-endpoints --query 'VpcEndpoints[0].SubnetIds'One subnet per AZ in use, not one subnet
The endpoint's security groupAllows 443 from the whole VPC CIDR
Route from each workload subnet to the endpoint subnetsPresent (same VPC, so normally the local route)
dig from a failing instanceReturns the private IP — proving DNS is not the failure, reachability is

If you cannot place endpoints in every AZ, leave private DNS off and address the endpoint by its generated vpce-*.vpce.amazonaws.com name, which is explicit and has no VPC-wide effect. Roll out private DNS the way you would any VPC-wide change: after the ENIs exist everywhere, not alongside creating them.

Mid: Twelve VPCs on a peering mesh, and the team wants to add ten more. What do you recommend, and what does it cost?

Migrate to a Transit Gateway. The mesh is at 12 VPCs — 66 connections and 132 route entries. Twenty-two VPCs would be 231 connections and 462 route entries, and each new VPC after that adds 22 more. The failure mode is not capacity, it is change: every addition touches every existing account and one will be missed.

  peering mesh, 22 VPCs    231 connections, 462 route entries, $0/hr
  transit gateway, 22 VPCs  22 attachments,   1 route table per segment
                            22 × $0.05/hr × 730          = $  803 / month
                            + $0.02/GB processing

What you get for that money is transitivity, one attachment per new VPC, a single on-prem attachment that all VPCs can use, and route-table segmentation that is readable as an isolation proof. What you give up is $0.02/GB, which matters if any pair moves tens of TB.

The migration is incremental and safe because longest-prefix match decides: attach the VPCs, add TGW routes for the CIDRs, verify, then remove peering routes one pair at a time — the peering route is equally specific, so remove it to shift traffic, and re-add it to roll back. Keep the one or two highest-volume peerings permanently as a cost optimisation; a hybrid of TGW plus targeted peering is a deliberate design, not a half-finished migration.

Senior: You acquire a company. Their entire estate is 10.0.0.0/16, and so is yours. They need to call three of your APIs and you need to call two of theirs, starting next month. What do you do?

Do not renumber, and do not try to route. Renumbering an acquired estate is a multi-quarter project with an outage budget, and the requirement here is five API calls — a service requirement, not a network one.

PrivateLink in both directions. Each side puts an NLB in front of the services it exposes and publishes an endpoint service; the other side creates interface endpoints, which appear as ENIs with addresses from the consumer's own CIDR. There is no route between the two networks, so the identical 10.0.0.0/16 ranges never meet and never conflict. Five endpoint services, five sets of endpoints, and the address collision is simply not part of the problem.

What I would explicitly reject: NAT-ing one side's range onto another (fragile, opaque, and breaks anything that embeds addresses); a VPN with overlapping CIDRs and dual NAT (the same, with more moving parts); and an emergency renumbering to hit a next-month date, which is how you turn an integration into an outage.

The longer game: if the two estates must genuinely merge later, renumber one side onto an allocation from a joint IPAM plan, VPC by VPC, with PrivateLink carrying the integrations throughout so there is never a flag day. The five endpoints keep working the entire time — that is the real argument for starting here rather than treating it as a stopgap.

Senior: Design connectivity for a regulated bank: 40 accounts, three environments, on-prem core systems, and an auditor who wants proof that production cannot reach development.

Transit Gateway hub, segmented by route table, with the isolation expressed as routing rather than as filtering. Three TGW route tables — prod, nonprod, shared. Prod VPC attachments associate with prod-rt, into which only prod and shared CIDRs propagate. Dev and staging associate with nonprod-rt. The proof the auditor wants is one command: search-transit-gateway-routes --transit-gateway-route-table-id tgw-rtb-prod returns no nonprod CIDR. That is a routing fact, not a claim about forty accounts' security groups, and it can be diffed against an intended matrix in CI so the evidence is continuous rather than a point-in-time screenshot.

On-prem attaches once — Direct Connect primary with a VPN backup, both to the TGW, with a DX gateway associating to the TGW. Encrypt it: MACsec on the DX ports, or IPsec over DX, because a private circuit is not an encrypted one and a bank's auditor will ask. Two DX connections at two locations, or the 99.99% SLA does not apply.

Egress and shared services centralised in an inspection VPC off the shared route table: NAT and a firewall appliance with ApplianceModeSupport=enable so flow symmetry holds and the stateful inspection actually works. Gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB in every VPC because they are free; interface endpoints in the shared VPC for services many accounts need, exposed to the others over the TGW.

Third parties never get network access. Vendor integrations are PrivateLink endpoint services — unidirectional, principal-scoped, revocable, and immune to the vendor's address plan.

DNS is part of the design, not a follow-up: Route 53 Resolver inbound and outbound endpoints in the shared VPC, forwarding rules for the on-prem zones shared org-wide via RAM, private hosted zones associated centrally. Otherwise the routing will be perfect and nothing will resolve.

The guardrails that make it hold: SCPs preventing member accounts from creating Internet Gateways, accepting peering connections, or deleting flow logs — because an isolation guarantee a team can undo is not a guarantee. Central IPAM so no two accounts can collide. Flow logs to a central account for the audit trail. The distinction that matters to a regulator is between controls teams could change and controls they cannot; put every isolation property in the second category, at the Organization level, and the architecture answers the audit by construction.

🔑 Two endpoints, because the two directions are entirely separate systems. Teams routinely build the outbound one, confirm that AWS can now resolve on-prem names, and declare hybrid DNS finished — then discover months later that nothing on-prem can resolve anything in AWS. Test each direction from a host on that side, with dig @<endpoint-ip> to bypass the local resolver and isolate which component is failing. And open both UDP and TCP on port 53: DNS falls back to TCP for responses over 512 bytes, so a UDP-only rule produces the maddening "most names work, some do not".

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Ask first: do you need network reachability or service reachability? Most "connect these VPCs" requests are a single API call wearing a network costume.
  • Peering is not transitive — A→B and B→C gives nothing between A and C, and AWS refuses the route at the API rather than silently dropping traffic.
  • The same rule blocks using a peer's IGW, NAT Gateway or gateway endpoints: a peering is a wire, not a router.
  • A peering connection is a possibility, not a route — nothing works until routes exist on both sides, plus security groups on both sides.
  • Mesh growth is n(n−1)/2: the 21st VPC costs 20 new connections and 40 route entries across 20 accounts, and one will be forgotten.
  • Transit Gateway is transitive, reaches on-prem, and scales at one attachment per VPC — but $0.05/hr per attachment plus $0.02/GB is not free ($2,295/month for 30 attachments and 60 TB).
  • Create a TGW with default association and propagation DISABLED, or every attachment reaches every other one and the segmentation exists only in the diagram.
  • Association decides where an attachment's traffic is looked up; propagation decides which tables learn its CIDR. Those are different questions.
  • "Prod's route table contains no nonprod CIDR" is an isolation proof readable in one screen — diff it in CI, and test that the check actually fails.
  • A CI check detects drift; an SCP prevents it. Auditors distinguish controls a team could undo from controls they cannot.
  • TGW + one direct peering for the elephant pair is a deliberate hybrid — 43% cheaper in the worked example, and longest-prefix match uses it automatically.
  • Appliance mode keeps both directions of a flow on one AZ; without it a stateful firewall sees half a conversation and fails intermittently under load.
  • PrivateLink puts an ENI from YOUR CIDR in front of someone else's service — so two VPCs both on 10.0.0.0/16 can talk, because no route between them exists at all.
  • PrivateLink is unidirectional and principal-scoped: the provider cannot initiate back, and access is revocable in one API call.
  • Gateway endpoints (S3, DynamoDB) are free; interface endpoints cost ~$0.01/hr per AZ plus $0.01/GB. Never pay for the former.
  • --private-dns-enabled is a VPC-wide DNS change — it removes the public endpoint as a fallback and breaks workloads nobody deployed.
  • Build endpoint ENIs in every AZ and allow 443 from the VPC CIDR before enabling private DNS; the rollback is one call and takes seconds.
  • Direct Connect carries no encryption — use MACsec or IPsec over it — and a single circuit has no applicable SLA.
  • DX's real win is usually egress at ~$0.02/GB versus ~$0.09/GB, which pays for redundant ports at scale; below a few TB, a VPN provisions in minutes.
  • The commonest hybrid failure is DNS, not routing: "name or service not known" while the IP pings fine. Outbound endpoint + FORWARD rule for AWS→on-prem, inbound endpoint for on-prem→AWS — they are two separate systems.
  • Open both UDP and TCP on port 53, share resolver rules through RAM rather than per-VPC, and budget ~$180/month per endpoint pair.
