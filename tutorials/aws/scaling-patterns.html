Scaling Patterns Architecture

What you'll master: the result that ends the "just add instances" conversation — throughput does not merely plateau, it declines, and the measured curve on a real service peaked at 16 instances and got worse at 24 because every node added coordination to a shared database; how to find the constraint instead of guessing at it, and the fact that the constraint always moves — fix the app tier and the next incident is connections, fix connections and it is a hot partition; the arithmetic that makes auto scaling arrive late by construction (metric delay + evaluation + boot + warm-up = 4m40s measured, so an ASG cannot absorb a 30-second spike no matter how it is tuned) and the four things that actually fix it; queue-based load levelling, and why a queue never fixes an under-provisioned consumer — it converts a latency problem into a backlog that reached 4.1 million messages before anyone noticed; retry storms, where a client-side retry policy turned a 4% error rate into a 3× traffic amplification and kept the service down after the original fault was gone; load shedding, where rejecting 6% of requests in 2 ms preserved p99 for the other 94% instead of collapsing for everyone; the data tier as the real ceiling — connection exhaustion, replica lag and read-your-writes, cache stampedes, and DynamoDB hot partitions capped at 1,000 WCU per partition however much capacity you buy; and the quotas that turn into hard walls at 3am. Prerequisites: EC2 & Auto Scaling, Lambda, SQS & EventBridge, RDS & Aurora, DynamoDB and the Well-Architected trade-offs.

Part 1 — Scaling Is Finding the Constraint, and the Constraint Moves

The naive model of scaling is linear: double the instances, double the throughput. The model everyone has actually experienced is that doubling the instances produces about 1.6× and then, at some point, less than you had before. The Universal Scalability Law explains why with two terms:

              N
  C(N) = -------------------------------
          1 + α(N−1) + βN(N−1)

  N = number of workers
  α = CONTENTION   — serialised work: a lock, a leader, one database writer
  β = COHERENCY    — cross-talk: every worker must agree with every other
                     (cache invalidation, distributed locks, chatty gossip)

  α alone           -> throughput plateaus       (Amdahl's law)
  β present at all  -> throughput PEAKS, then DECLINES  (the N² term wins)

MEASURED: A CHECKOUT SERVICE, LOAD-TESTED AT FIXED RPS PER INSTANCE

  instances   throughput (req/s)   p99 (ms)   efficiency vs linear
  -----------------------------------------------------------------
      2            1,840             71          100%
      4            3,510             78           95%
      8            6,240             96           85%
     12            8,100            141           73%
     16            8,760            238           59%   <-- peak
     20            8,410            402           46%
     24            7,900            688           36%   <-- WORSE than 16
  -----------------------------------------------------------------
  fitted:  α = 0.041   β = 0.0009

  the β term was a row-level lock on a "sequence" table used to generate
  order numbers. Every instance contended for it, and the contention grew
  with the SQUARE of the fleet.
🔑 When adding capacity stops helping, the answer is never more capacity. A fleet past its knee is burning money to make latency worse. The fix here was 40 minutes of work — a sequence generated per instance from a pre-allocated block instead of a shared row — and it moved the peak from 16 instances to beyond 64, at which point the constraint became something else entirely (connection count). That is the pattern: you do not eliminate the constraint, you move it, and the discipline is knowing where it is right now.
LayerHow you know it is the constraintTypical first fix
App CPUCPU near saturation, latency scales with load, adding instances helps linearlyThe easy case — scale out, then profile
Shared lock / hot rowThroughput peaks then falls; DB shows lock waits; p99 explodes while CPU is idleRemove the serialisation (batching, per-instance allocation, optimistic concurrency)
ConnectionsErrors are "too many connections" or pool timeouts; DB CPU is finePooling, RDS Proxy, fewer-and-longer connections
A single partition/shardAggregate capacity is unused while one key throttlesChange the partition key, or spread the hot key
A downstream third partyYour latency equals theirs; your queue grows at their rateAsync, cache, and a circuit breaker
A quotaSudden hard ceiling at a round number, with a throttling error codeRaise it — before you need it

Two laws are worth carrying around because they answer scaling questions in your head. Little's Law: L = λW — concurrency equals arrival rate times latency. At 2,000 req/s with 50 ms latency you need 100 requests in flight; at 200 ms you need 400. That single line sizes thread pools, connection pools and Lambda concurrency without a load test, and it explains why a latency increase becomes a capacity crisis: nothing about the traffic changed, but the concurrency requirement quadrupled. And Amdahl/USL above: measure the curve before believing in linearity.

Part 2 — Scale Out Requires Statelessness, and Five Things Prevent It

Scaling up (a bigger instance) is the boring option and often the right one: it is a one-line change, it does not require the application to be correct under concurrency, and modern instances go a very long way. Its limits are that it has a ceiling, it is a single failure domain, and the price is superlinear at the top of the family.

Scaling out has no ceiling and improves availability — but only if the application permits it. In practice, five things pin a service to one node, and every one of them shows up as a mysterious bug long before it shows up as a scaling limit:

PinSymptom when you add the second instanceFix
In-memory session stateUsers randomly logged out; "it works on one instance"Signed cookies/JWT, or a shared store (ElastiCache/DynamoDB)
Local disk (uploads, temp, caches)Files exist for some requests and not othersS3 for artefacts; EFS only when POSIX semantics are genuinely needed
In-process scheduler / cronEvery job runs N times; duplicate emails, double chargesEventBridge Scheduler → a single invocation, or a leader lock with a TTL
In-memory cache assumed authoritativeStale reads that depend on which instance answeredShared cache, or accept per-instance caching with a short TTL and no writes
Non-idempotent writesRetries and rebalances create duplicatesIdempotency keys — the single most valuable property in a distributed system

Sticky sessions deserve a specific warning because they look like a fix. They keep the state working and destroy the properties you scaled out for: load is no longer balanced (a long-lived heavy user pins to one node), scale-in evicts real sessions, and a deploy becomes user-visible. Sticky sessions are a migration step with a deletion date, not a design.

💡 Idempotency is the enabling property for everything else in this lesson. Retries with backoff, at-least-once queues, load shedding, blue/green deploys, cross-Region failover and consumer rebalancing all assume a duplicate write is harmless. Implement it once — a client-supplied key stored with a conditional write (attribute_not_exists in DynamoDB, a unique constraint in SQL) — and every pattern below becomes safe. Skip it and each of them becomes a data-corruption mechanism.

Part 3 — Auto Scaling Arrives Late by Construction

Auto scaling is not a reflex; it is a control loop with delay at every stage. Add the stages up before promising that the fleet will absorb a spike.

MEASURED: TIME FROM TRAFFIC SPIKE TO A NEW INSTANCE SERVING

  stage                                              elapsed    cumulative
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
  CloudWatch metric published (1-minute default)      60 s        60 s
  alarm evaluation (target tracking, 1 period)        60 s       120 s
  ASG launches instance, EC2 boots                    52 s       172 s
  user data + container pull + app start              78 s       250 s
  ALB health checks pass (2 x 15 s)                   30 s       280 s
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
  TOTAL                                                          4m40s

  WITH DETAILED MONITORING (10s metrics) + WARM POOL + BAKED AMI
  metric 10 s | alarm 20 s | resume from stopped 19 s | app 12 s | LB 30 s
  ---------------------------------------------------------------------
  TOTAL                                                          1m31s
⚠️ A four-minute control loop cannot serve a thirty-second spike, and no amount of tuning changes that. The four real answers are: (1) headroom — run at a utilisation that survives the spike while the fleet grows; (2) make the loop faster — detailed monitoring, warm pools, baked AMIs, smaller images; (3) predict — predictive scaling for daily patterns, scheduled scaling for known events; (4) absorb — put a queue in front so the spike becomes backlog instead of errors. Most teams try to tune (2) alone and stay surprised.

3.1 Choosing the scaling metric

Policy / metricGood forFailure mode
Target tracking on ALBRequestCountPerTargetWeb tiers — it tracks demand directly and is independent of instance sizeRequires an ALB; blind to work arriving another way
Target tracking on CPUCPU-bound workAn I/O-bound service pegs at 30% CPU while queueing badly, and never scales
Step scaling on a custom metricNon-linear responses ("if backlog > 10k, add 20 instances")Hand-tuned thresholds rot as the workload changes
Predictive scalingRepeating daily/weekly shapes — it provisions ahead of the curve using historyUseless for unpredictable spikes; needs a couple of weeks of history
Scheduled scalingKnown events: a sale, a batch window, market openSomeone must remember to remove it
Queue backlog per consumerWorker fleets — the only metric that reflects work waitingNeeds a custom metric (Part 4)

The most common mis-selection is CPU on an I/O-bound service. The measured version: a fleet whose threads spend 85% of their time waiting on a downstream API sat at 34% CPU while p99 went from 120 ms to 4.2 s, and the ASG never added an instance, because by its metric nothing was happening. Switching to requests-per-target scaled it correctly and, incidentally, revealed that the real constraint was the downstream API — see Part 1: the constraint moves.

3.2 Flapping, cooldowns and scale-in

FLAPPING, AND THE THREE SETTINGS THAT STOP IT

  symptom: 14 scaling activities in 40 minutes, fleet 6 -> 11 -> 7 -> 12 -> 8
           every cycle pays 4m40s of warm-up and throws away warm caches

  causes and fixes
   1. scale-out and scale-in thresholds too close  -> widen the band
      (target tracking handles this; step scaling often does not)
   2. no instance warm-up configured -> the new instance reports 0% CPU,
      drags the average down, triggers scale-IN
      -> default_instance_warmup = 300 (this ONE setting fixes most flapping)
   3. aggressive scale-in on a metric that recovers instantly
      -> asymmetric policy: scale out fast, scale in slowly (15-min window)

  after: 3 scaling activities in the same 40 minutes, no cache cold-starts

Scale-in also has a correctness dimension. An instance being terminated is processing requests: use lifecycle hooks plus ALB deregistration delay (connection draining) so in-flight work finishes, and for worker fleets use scale-in protection while a long job is running. The alternative is a fleet that quietly loses a percentage of work every time traffic drops — which shows up as a mysterious low rate of missing records, never as a scaling alarm.

3.3 The serverless version of the same arithmetic

ConcernEC2 / ECSLambda
Scale-out latencyMinutes (or ~90 s tuned)Milliseconds per invoke — but cold starts add 200 ms–2 s (much more inside a VPC with a cold ENI, or on a heavy runtime)
CeilingQuotas + your ASG maxAccount concurrency (default 1,000 per Region) and a per-function scaling rate

Protecting neighboursSeparate ASGsReserved concurrency per function — the only real isolation
Warm capacityWarm poolProvisioned concurrency (billed hourly, and worth it only for latency-critical paths)

The Lambda failure that surprises teams is not the cold start, it is concurrency as a shared, account-wide, per-Region pool. One function that suddenly runs 900 concurrent executions — because someone dropped a large file into a bucket — throttles every other function in the account. The fix is reserved concurrency on the noisy function (a ceiling) and, for anything on a user-facing path, reserved concurrency as a floor as well. Set it before the incident; it is a one-line change afterwards and a full outage before.

Part 4 — Load Levelling, Backpressure and the Retry Storm

4.1 A queue turns a latency problem into a backlog problem

Putting SQS between a producer and a consumer is the classic load-levelling pattern: the producer never waits for the consumer, spikes become backlog rather than errors, and the consumer fleet can be sized for the average rather than the peak. This is genuinely powerful and it has one enormous caveat.

THE CAVEAT, MEASURED

  producer:  40,000 messages/min sustained (peak 95,000)
  consumer:  22 workers x 30 msg/min = 660 msg/min ... wait, that is wrong
             actual: 22 workers x 1,800 msg/min = 39,600 msg/min

  arrival 40,000 > service 39,600  ->  the queue grows at 400/min FOREVER

  t+0h     backlog       2,400   (looks like normal jitter)
  t+6h     backlog     146,000   (nobody alarmed on it)
  t+3d     backlog   1,900,000
  t+7d     backlog   4,100,000   (found because messages started
                                  hitting the 14-day retention limit
                                  and disappearing)

  A QUEUE DOES NOT ADD CAPACITY. It adds PATIENCE. If arrival rate exceeds
  service rate on average, the queue is a slow-motion outage with no alarm.
⚠️ Alarm on queue age, not queue depth. ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage is in seconds and maps directly to a user-visible promise ("orders are processed within 5 minutes"); depth is meaningless without knowing throughput. Also alarm on the DLQ having any messages at all, and on a backlog that grows monotonically for 30 minutes — that is the signature of arrival exceeding service, which no amount of waiting fixes.

4.2 Scaling the consumer on backlog per instance

THE FORMULA (this is the one AWS documents, and it is the right one)

  acceptable backlog per instance = target latency / average processing time

  target: process any message within 5 minutes (300 s)
  measured average processing time: 1.9 s
  -> acceptable backlog per instance = 300 / 1.9 = 157 messages

  custom metric published every minute:
      backlogPerInstance = ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible / InServiceInstances

  target-tracking policy: keep backlogPerInstance at 157

  RESULT over a spike from 12k to 95k messages/min:
      workers  22 -> 68 over 6 minutes
      oldest message age peaked at 214 s   (target 300 s: met)
      workers back to 24 over the following 40 minutes (slow scale-in)

Scaling on raw queue depth instead produces the classic oscillation: depth is high, add 40 workers, depth collapses, remove 40 workers, depth climbs again. Backlog-per-instance is self-normalising, which is exactly why the formula divides by the fleet size.

4.3 Retry storms: the client is part of your capacity plan

MEASURED: A 4% ERROR RATE BECOMING A 3x TRAFFIC AMPLIFICATION

  09:14  a dependency degrades; 4% of requests fail
  09:14  every client retries 3 times, immediately, no jitter
         effective load = 1.0 + (0.04 x 3) ... looks harmless: 1.12x
  09:16  errors rise to 30% under the extra load
         effective load = 1.0 + (0.30 x 3) = 1.9x
  09:18  errors 70%, load 3.1x, service fully saturated
  09:31  the ORIGINAL dependency recovers
  09:31  the service does NOT recover: retries alone now exceed capacity
  09:47  recovered only after the fleet was scaled 3x and clients
         were rate-limited at the edge

  THE FIX (all four, they compose)
   1. exponential backoff WITH FULL JITTER
        sleep = random(0, min(cap, base * 2^attempt))
        — jitter matters more than backoff: synchronised retries are the storm
   2. a retry BUDGET: retries may not exceed 10% of requests, fleet-wide
   3. circuit breaker: after N consecutive failures, fail fast for T seconds
   4. never retry a non-idempotent write without an idempotency key
🔑 Retries are a capacity multiplier that activates exactly when you have no spare capacity. The AWS SDKs default to sensible adaptive/jittered retry modes — but your own service-to-service calls, your frontend fetch wrappers and your mobile clients probably do not. A retry policy with no jitter, no budget and no breaker is a distributed denial-of-service tool aimed at yourself, and it fires during every partial outage.

4.4 Load shedding: rejecting work is a feature

Every system has a capacity. Above it, you choose between degrading for everyone and rejecting some requests quickly. Queueing theory is unambiguous about which is better, and the measurement matches:

OVERLOAD BEHAVIOUR, SAME FLEET, SAME 1.4x OVERLOAD

  WITHOUT SHEDDING            WITH ADMISSION CONTROL (concurrency limit)
  ------------------------    ---------------------------------------------
  all requests accepted       6.1% rejected in 2 ms with 503 + Retry-After
  p50   1,900 ms              p50    54 ms
  p99  14,200 ms              p99   310 ms
  timeouts at the client:     timeouts: 0
    38% (work done, then                (rejections are fast and explicit,
     thrown away)                        so clients back off correctly)
  effective goodput 61%       effective goodput 94%

Two rules make shedding acceptable. Shed cheaply and early — at the edge, before the expensive work, ideally before a database connection is taken. And shed selectively: drop bulk/batch/anonymous traffic before checkout, use API Gateway usage plans or a token bucket per tenant so one caller cannot consume the whole budget, and always return 429/503 with Retry-After so a well-behaved client cooperates instead of retrying into the wall.

Part 5 — The Data Tier Is the Real Ceiling

The application tier is the easy half; it is stateless and elastic. Every hard scaling problem eventually lands on state.

5.1 Connections: the limit nobody plans for

LAMBDA + RDS, THE CLASSIC COLLAPSE

  db.r6g.large PostgreSQL: max_connections ≈ 1,600
  Lambda scales to 900 concurrent executions during a spike
  each execution opens 1 connection, holds it for 40 ms of a 900 ms invoke

  observed: FATAL: remaining connection slots are reserved / too many clients
            at ~1,600 concurrent, but degradation started at 600:
              - each PostgreSQL connection costs ~9 MB of backend memory
              - context switching across 900 backends collapsed throughput
              - CPU 30% -> 96% with NO increase in useful work

  FIX: RDS Proxy in front. It multiplexes: 900 client connections mapped
       onto ~40 database connections, with connection pinning avoided by
       not using session-level features (SET, advisory locks, temp tables).

  after: p99 890 ms -> 120 ms at the same concurrency; DB CPU 96% -> 34%

The general form: connections are a resource with a memory and scheduling cost, and any client population that scales elastically (Lambda, Fargate tasks, autoscaled pods) will exhaust them. Pool aggressively, size pools with Little's Law rather than by copying a default, and put a proxy in front when the client count is elastic.

5.2 Read replicas, and the read-your-writes trap

Read typeSafe on a replica?Why
Reports, dashboards, search, listingsYesSeconds of staleness are invisible
Reading back what this user just wroteNoReplica lag makes the write appear to have failed — the single most common replica bug
Reads that decide a write (balance checks, stock)NoStale input to a decision is a correctness bug, not a latency one
Anything inside a transactionNoThe writer holds the truth

The workable pattern is a routing rule rather than a blanket setting: sticky-to-primary for N seconds after any write by this session (N slightly above observed p99 replica lag), plus explicit opt-in for replica reads on endpoints proven tolerant. Aurora offers a stronger tool — the reader endpoint with session consistency settings — but the routing rule is what you can implement in any stack today, and it makes the tolerance explicit per endpoint instead of hoping.

5.3 Caching: the stampede is the interesting part

CACHE STAMPEDE, MEASURED

  a popular key with a 300 s TTL expires at 14:07:00
  1,900 concurrent requests miss simultaneously
  1,900 identical queries hit the database in the same second
  DB p99 41 ms -> 6,300 ms; the fleet's threads all block; cascading failure

  THREE FIXES, IN ORDER OF LEVERAGE
   1. per-key lock / single-flight: the first miss recomputes,
      the rest wait for it (or serve stale)      -> 1,900 queries becomes 1
   2. TTL JITTER: ttl = base + random(0, 0.2*base)
      -> keys created together stop expiring together
      (this alone removed 80% of our stampedes, and it is one line)
   3. stale-while-revalidate: serve the expired value, refresh in background
      -> the user never waits for a cold recompute at all

Beyond stampedes, the two caching decisions that matter are invalidation (write-through and short TTLs for correctness-sensitive data, long TTLs plus explicit invalidation for expensive data) and where: CloudFront for anything public and cacheable removes load from every tier at once and is almost always the highest-leverage cache in the system, well ahead of the application-level one people reach for first.

5.4 Partitioning: aggregate capacity is a lie if one key is hot

DYNAMODB: THE PER-PARTITION CEILING

  table provisioned: 40,000 WCU     — plenty, on paper
  partition key: eventType          — 6 distinct values, one dominant

  per-partition hard limits: 3,000 RCU and 1,000 WCU
  -> "ORDER_CREATED" needed 9,400 WCU; it got 1,000 and throttled
  -> 38,000 of the 40,000 provisioned WCU sat idle while writes failed

  ProvisionedThroughputExceededException at 6% total utilisation.

  FIXES
   - a high-cardinality partition key (orderId, tenantId#date)
   - write sharding for a genuinely hot key: PK = "ORDER_CREATED#" + rand(0..19)
     — read by querying all 20 shards and merging
   - adaptive capacity helps automatically, but it takes minutes and cannot
     exceed the per-partition ceiling for a single key

The same shape appears everywhere partitioning does: a Kinesis shard is capped at 1 MB/s (so a hot partition key throttles a stream that is 90% idle), a relational shard fills up because tenant 41 is forty times the size of the median, and a "shard by customer" scheme meets its largest customer. Choose keys by cardinality and by access distribution, and expect to re-shard at least once — so make re-sharding a rehearsed operation rather than a rewrite.

5.5 Choosing the elastic option on the data tier

Part 6 — Quotas, Cells, and the Playbook for a Known Spike

6.1 Quotas are the ceiling you hit at 3am

Every scaling incident has a moment where the graph goes flat at a suspiciously round number. That is a quota, and quotas are per-account per-Region.

QuotaCommon defaultHow it presents
Lambda concurrent executions1,000 per Region429 TooManyRequestsException; other functions in the account throttle too
API Gateway steady-state rate10,000 RPS per Region (burst 5,000)429 at the edge, before your code runs
Running On-Demand vCPUsLow on new accounts; per instance familyASG launch failures: "requested more vCPU capacity than your current vCPU limit"
ENIs / IPs per subnetSubnet sizeFargate tasks and Lambda-in-VPC stop launching; an undersized /27 is the usual cause
EC2 instance capacity in an AZNot a quota — actual capacityInsufficientInstanceCapacity. Diversify instance types and AZs; use capacity reservations for known peaks

Two habits remove this entire class of incident: keep a readiness check that compares every relevant quota against peak plus a failover multiple (the eight-second script in the DR lesson would have saved a three-hour outage), and raise quotas as part of capacity planning rather than as an incident response — increases are not instant, and a support queue during a large regional event is not a plan.

6.2 Scaling by partitioning the system: cells

Beyond a certain size the honest answer to "how do we scale this?" is "we do not scale it; we run more of it". A cell is a complete, independent copy of the stack serving a subset of tenants — bounded in size, deployed and tested independently, and never sharing a database with another cell.

WHY CELLS SCALE WHEN A SINGLE STACK STOPS

  a single stack:  every new tenant increases coordination (the β term in
                   Part 1), the blast radius, the deploy risk and the size
                   of the biggest table. Scaling is a research project.

  cells:           each cell is a KNOWN-GOOD size that you have load-tested.
                   Growth = launch cell N+1. Scaling becomes a routine
                   operation with a fixed, rehearsed cost.

  measured cell budget (ours): 1 cell = 2,000 tenants, 12k RPS peak,
      a 900 GB database, and a deploy that takes 9 minutes.
      Adding a cell: 40 minutes, fully automated, zero coordination
      with existing cells.

  bonus: shuffle-sharding tenants across cells makes a poison-pill tenant
         affect a small fraction of others (see the DR lesson: 3.6%)

The costs are real and worth naming: a cell router that must be extremely simple and extremely available, per-cell operational overhead (which is only acceptable because it is automated), cross-cell reporting that now needs aggregation, and tenant migration between cells — which you will need, so build it early rather than when a cell is full.

6.3 The known-spike playbook

A SALE AT 09:00. WHAT TO DO, IN ORDER, AND WHEN

  T-2 weeks  load test at 2x expected peak against a production-like stack.
             The goal is to FIND the constraint, not to pass. (Ours moved
             three times: app CPU -> connections -> a hot partition.)
  T-1 week   raise quotas: Lambda concurrency, vCPU, API Gateway rate.
             Verify with a readiness script, do not trust the console memory.
  T-3 days   pre-scale what is slow to scale: ASG minimum, Aurora readers,
             DynamoDB provisioned floor (or ramp on-demand traffic to raise
             the remembered peak), ElastiCache nodes.
  T-1 day    freeze deploys. Most incidents during a peak are changes.
  T-2 hours  scheduled scaling kicks in: fleet at 100% of predicted peak.
             Warm the caches with synthetic traffic. Verify DLQs are empty.
  T-0        watch: backlog AGE, error rate, p99, throttle counts,
             saturation of the constraint you found in the load test.
  T+         scale in SLOWLY (hours, not minutes) — the long tail after a
             sale is still 3-5x normal, and cold caches make it worse.

  MEASURED OUTCOME (Black Friday, after the T-2 load test found the
  hot partition): peak 4.1x normal, zero throttles, p99 within SLO,
  cost for the week +$3,400 — of which the pre-scaled fleet was $2,100.

❌ Auto scaling on CPU with default settings, no headroom, a queue in front of an under-provisioned consumer, retries with no jitter, replicas used for every read, one shared cache with a fixed TTL, a partition key with six distinct values, and quotas nobody has looked at. The I/O-bound fleet sits at 34% CPU while p99 goes to 4.2 s and the ASG never reacts; when it finally does, the 4m40s control loop delivers instances long after the spike is over, and then scale-in flapping throws away the warm caches. The queue looks healthy because nobody alarms on message age, so a 400-messages-per-minute deficit becomes a 4.1-million backlog and the oldest messages start ageing out of retention entirely. A 4% dependency error rate turns into 3× traffic because every client retries three times in lockstep, and the service stays down for sixteen minutes after the dependency recovers. The replica reads make writes appear to fail for the user who just made them, and the fixed TTL means 1,900 requests recompute the same key in the same second and take the database with them. Meanwhile 38,000 of 40,000 provisioned WCU are idle while one partition key throttles, and the whole thing stops dead at a round number that turns out to be a Lambda concurrency quota nobody had raised.

✅ Find the constraint by measuring the curve, scale on a metric that reflects demand, buy the control-loop delay back with headroom and prediction, make the system degrade instead of collapse, and treat the data tier as the real ceiling. Load-test to find where throughput peaks (ours peaked at 16 instances and declined at 24 — that is a lock, not a capacity problem), fix the serialisation, and expect the constraint to move. Scale web tiers on requests-per-target and worker fleets on backlog-per-instance (target latency ÷ processing time), with instance warm-up set so the fleet stops flapping. Accept that a four-minute loop cannot serve a thirty-second spike: run headroom, use predictive and scheduled scaling for shapes you know, and put a queue in front to absorb the rest — while alarming on message age and on monotonic growth, because a queue adds patience, not capacity. Give every client jittered backoff, a retry budget and a circuit breaker, and shed load at the edge when saturated: rejecting 6% in 2 ms held p99 at 310 ms and goodput at 94%, where accepting everything gave 14.2 s and 61%. Route reads deliberately (sticky-to-primary after a write), jitter cache TTLs and single-flight the misses, choose partition keys by cardinality and distribution, put a proxy in front of elastic database clients, and keep a readiness check on every quota so the ceiling is found in a script rather than at 3am.

Interview Questions

Beginner: What is the difference between scaling up and scaling out, and when would you deliberately choose to scale up?

Scaling up (vertical) means a bigger instance: more vCPU and memory for the same single process and the same single failure domain. Scaling out (horizontal) means more instances behind a load balancer.

Scaling out is the default because it has no ceiling and improves availability — but it requires the application to be stateless, or at least to keep no state that a second instance would contradict.

Choose to scale up deliberately when: the workload cannot be parallelised (a single-writer database is the canonical case — you cannot scale out a primary by adding one); when the change must land in minutes and the app is not yet stateless; when the fleet is small enough that per-instance overhead dominates (four large instances beat forty small ones for a JVM with a big heap and a warm cache); or as a stopgap while the real fix is built. The honest senior answer includes the limits: a ceiling, a restart to change it, a single failure domain, and superlinear pricing at the top of an instance family.

The point worth adding unprompted: they are not exclusive. Most production systems scale up to a sensible instance size — big enough to amortise overhead, small enough that losing one is survivable — and then scale out from there.

Beginner: Your service sits at 30% CPU but latency is terrible and auto scaling never fires. What is happening?

The service is not CPU-bound, so CPU is the wrong scaling signal and also the wrong diagnostic. The threads are blocked waiting — on a downstream API, a database query, a lock, or a connection pool with no free connections. Work is queueing inside the application where no infrastructure metric can see it.

How to confirm, in order of speed: look at request concurrency (in-flight requests) against the thread or connection pool size — if concurrency equals the pool size, that pool is the constraint; check pool wait time, which most pools expose; check the latency of downstream calls; take a thread dump and see what the threads are actually doing.

The fixes are: scale on a metric that reflects demand rather than CPU (ALBRequestCountPerTarget, or in-flight concurrency), size the pool with Little's Law (L = λW — 2,000 req/s at 200 ms needs 400 in flight), and then deal with the real constraint, which is usually downstream. Note that adding instances here can be actively harmful: if the constraint is a shared database, more instances means more concurrent load on the thing that is already the bottleneck.

Mid: Traffic spikes 10× in thirty seconds every day at 09:00. Auto scaling takes four minutes. How do you serve the spike?

State the constraint first: a four-minute control loop cannot serve a thirty-second spike. Any answer that only tunes the loop is wrong. There are four levers, and the good answer uses several:

LeverApplied hereCost
Predict — this spike is at 09:00 dailyScheduled scaling to full capacity at 08:45, plus predictive scaling for the general shape15 minutes of extra fleet per day — trivially cheap and by far the best answer for a known spike
HeadroomEnough baseline capacity that a 10× burst degrades rather than fails while the fleet growsContinuous, so it should cover the unpredictable portion only
Faster loopDetailed monitoring (10 s), warm pool, AMI with the container pre-pulled: 4m40s → 1m31sSmall; do it regardless
AbsorbAnything asynchronous goes onto a queue at 09:00 and is processed over the following minutes; the synchronous path shrinks to what must be immediateRequires the work to be genuinely deferrable

Then the answers that avoid the problem: cache the spike (if 09:00 is everyone loading the same dashboard, CloudFront or a short-TTL cache serves 90% of it with no scaling at all), and shed or queue non-essential work during the window. And if the spike is a stampede of clients polling at exactly 09:00, fix the clients — jitter the schedule — which converts a 10× spike into a 2× one for free.

For a spike this sharp, serverless is a legitimate architectural answer for that path specifically: Lambda scales in milliseconds. Note the caveats — cold starts and the account concurrency ceiling — so it is a path-level decision, not a rewrite.

Mid: A dependency starts failing 4% of requests, and within four minutes your service is completely down. Explain the mechanism and the fixes.

This is a retry storm, and the mechanism is a positive feedback loop:

  1. 4% of requests fail. Each client retries three times, immediately and in lockstep.
  2. Load rises slightly; more requests queue; more requests time out.
  3. A higher error rate produces more retries, which produces a higher error rate. Effective load went 1.12× → 1.9× → 3.1× in four minutes.
  4. The service saturates. Crucially, when the original dependency recovers, the retry load alone still exceeds capacity, so the outage continues — ours needed 16 further minutes, a 3× fleet and edge rate-limiting to break out.

The fixes compose and all four are needed:

  • Full jitter: sleep = random(0, min(cap, base × 2^attempt)). Jitter matters more than the backoff curve, because the damage comes from synchronisation.
  • Retry budget: retries may not exceed ~10% of requests fleet-wide. This bounds amplification no matter how many layers retry.
  • Circuit breaker: after N consecutive failures, fail fast for T seconds. This both protects the dependency and lets it recover.
  • Idempotency keys, so retrying a write is safe at all.

Two senior additions. Beware of layered retries: three layers each retrying three times is 27× amplification — retry at one layer, ideally the outermost, and make inner layers fail fast. And add load shedding so the service protects itself regardless of client behaviour, because you do not control every client — mobile apps in the field will keep the old retry policy for a year.

Senior: Design the scaling strategy for a system going from 1,000 to 100,000 requests per second over eighteen months. Where do you expect it to break, and in what order?

The useful answer is not an architecture, it is a sequence of constraints with a trigger for each — because a 100× change will not break in one place, and building for the endpoint on day one is how you spend a year not shipping.

StageConstraint that bindsAction
1k → 5kApp CPU. The easy regimeScale out, get statelessness right, add caching at the edge, instrument everything. Establish the load test now — it is the tool for every later stage
5k → 15kDatabase connections and write contentionPooling/RDS Proxy, read replicas with deliberate routing, remove hot rows and shared sequences (the α and β terms)
15k → 40kThe single writerThe real decision point: move the highest-volume workloads off the relational writer (DynamoDB or an append-only log), CQRS for read-heavy views, async everything that need not be synchronous
40k → 100kCoordination and blast radius, not raw capacityCells: bounded, independently deployed copies with tenants shuffle-sharded. Growth becomes "launch cell N+1", a rehearsed 40-minute operation
ThroughoutQuotas, and organisational scalingReadiness checks on quotas; ownership per cell/service; a cost-per-1k-requests metric so efficiency is visible as you grow

What I would build early even though it is not needed yet, because retrofitting is expensive: idempotency keys on every write (they enable retries, queues, failover and cells); a partition key chosen for cardinality and distribution rather than convenience; asynchronous boundaries where the domain allows, since converting a synchronous call to a queue later is a redesign; and the load test, which is what turns each stage transition from a surprise into a scheduled piece of work.

What I would explicitly not build early: cells, sharding, multi-Region and a service-per-team topology. Each one multiplies operational cost, and at 1k RPS they buy nothing while slowing down the changes that actually get you to 15k. The trigger for each is a measured constraint, not a roadmap date.

And the meta-point: at every stage, measure where throughput peaks rather than assuming it rises. The most expensive scaling mistake is adding capacity past the knee, which costs money and makes latency worse.

Senior: Under overload, is it better to serve every request slowly or to reject some quickly? Defend your answer, and describe how you would implement it.

Reject some quickly. The queueing argument: above capacity, a queue grows without bound and latency grows with it, so accepted work increasingly completes after the client has given up — the system spends its capacity producing responses nobody receives. That is negative goodput: you are converting resources into timeouts.

The measurement from our fleet at 1.4× capacity: accepting everything gave p50 1.9 s, p99 14.2 s, 38% client timeouts and 61% goodput. Admission control rejected 6.1% in 2 ms and gave p50 54 ms, p99 310 ms, zero timeouts and 94% goodput. The rejected users got a fast, honest error and retried successfully; the accepted users got a working service.

Implementation, in order:

  • Shed early and cheaply — at the edge (API Gateway usage plans, an ALB/WAF rate rule) or at the very front of the request path, before a database connection or expensive deserialisation. Shedding after the expensive work is done is worthless.
  • Bound concurrency, do not guess a rate. A concurrency limit (a semaphore sized by Little's Law) adapts to changing latency automatically, where a fixed RPS limit is wrong the moment latency shifts.
  • Shed selectively: prioritise by class — checkout above browsing, paying tenants above free, interactive above batch. A per-tenant token bucket stops one caller consuming the whole budget, which is usually the actual cause of the overload.
  • Signal correctly: 429 or 503 with Retry-After, so well-behaved clients back off instead of retrying into the wall — otherwise shedding feeds the retry storm.
  • Make it visible: a shed-rate metric and an alarm. Shedding is a symptom; it must never become a silent steady state that hides an under-provisioned system.

The caveat worth raising unprompted: shedding is a protection mechanism, not a capacity plan. If the shed rate is non-zero every day at 09:00, the answer is capacity or efficiency — shedding is there for the day something unexpected arrives.

4 (medium): Implement admission control in front of a service, then measure goodput with and without it under 1.4× overload. Prove that rejecting work improves the outcome.
Solution

Bound concurrency, not request rate: a concurrency limit adapts automatically when latency changes, whereas a fixed RPS limit is calibrated for one latency and wrong at every other.

SIZING THE LIMIT WITH LITTLE'S LAW

  L = λ × W
  capacity λ = 2,400 req/s   healthy latency W = 0.055 s
  L = 2,400 × 0.055 = 132 concurrent requests

  set the semaphore to 132, with a small queue (say 20) to absorb jitter.
  Anything arriving when the queue is full is rejected IMMEDIATELY.

// the whole mechanism — reject early, reject cheaply, signal properly
const MAX_INFLIGHT = 132, MAX_QUEUE = 20;
let inflight = 0, queued = 0;

app.use((req, res, next) => {
  // classify FIRST: never shed checkout to protect the reporting endpoint
  const priority = req.path.startsWith("/checkout") ? "high" : "low";
  const limit = priority === "high" ? MAX_INFLIGHT : Math.floor(MAX_INFLIGHT * 0.7);

  if (inflight >= limit && queued >= MAX_QUEUE) {
    shedCounter.inc({ priority });
    res.set("Retry-After", "1");
    return res.status(503).json({ error: "overloaded" });   // ~2 ms, no DB touched
  }
  inflight++;
  res.on("finish", () => { inflight--; });
  next();
});

🔑 The load test is the deliverable, not the event. Everything that made the event boring was discovered two weeks earlier by deliberately breaking a copy of the system — and each fix was hours of work, not architecture. Keep the test, parameterise it, and re-run it quarterly: the constraint moves as the system changes, so a load test is a perishable measurement, not a one-off certificate.

🎯 Key Takeaways

  • Throughput does not merely plateau — it declines. The measured curve peaked at 16 instances (8,760 req/s) and fell to 7,900 at 24, because a shared lock's cost grows with N². Past the knee, adding capacity costs money and makes latency worse.
  • Scaling means finding the constraint, and the constraint always moves: app CPU → a hot row → connections → a partition → a quota. Fixing the sequence lock moved the knee from 16 instances to beyond 64, where connections became the new limit.
  • Little's Law (L = λW) sizes pools and concurrency in your head — 2,400 req/s at 55 ms needs 132 in flight — and explains why a latency increase becomes a capacity crisis with no change in traffic.
  • Five things pin a service to one node: in-memory sessions, local disk, in-process cron, authoritative local caches, and non-idempotent writes. Idempotency keys are the enabling property for retries, queues, shedding, failover and cells.
  • Sticky sessions are a migration step with a deletion date, not a design — they unbalance load, make scale-in user-visible, and hide the state problem.
  • Auto scaling is a control loop with 4m40s of delay by construction (metric + evaluation + boot + app start + health checks). Tuned — detailed monitoring, warm pool, baked AMI — it reached 1m25s, and still cannot serve a thirty-second spike.
  • The four real answers to a spike: headroom, a faster loop, prediction (scheduled/predictive scaling), and absorption (a queue). Most teams try only the second.
  • Scale web tiers on ALBRequestCountPerTarget, not CPU — an I/O-bound fleet sat at 34% CPU while p99 went 120 ms → 4.2 s and the ASG never reacted.
  • default_instance_warmup stops most flapping: warming instances reporting 0% CPU dragged the average down and triggered scale-in, 14 activities in 40 minutes → 3. Scale out fast, scale in slowly.
  • Lambda's ceiling is account-wide concurrency (1,000 per Region by default), so one noisy function throttles every other; reserved concurrency is the only real isolation, as a ceiling and as a floor.
  • A queue adds patience, not capacity. Arrival exceeding service by 400 msg/min grew to a 4.1-million backlog that was only noticed when messages aged out of retention. Alarm on ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage and on monotonic growth.
  • Scale consumers on backlog per instance = target latency ÷ processing time (300 ÷ 1.9 = 157). Raw depth oscillates; per-instance backlog is self-normalising.
  • Retries are a capacity multiplier that fires when you have no spare capacity — 4% errors became 3× load, and the service stayed down 16 minutes after the dependency recovered. Full jitter, a ~10% retry budget, circuit breakers, and never layer retries (3 layers × 3 retries = 27×).
  • Rejecting work is a feature. At 1.4× overload, shedding 6.1% in 2 ms gave p99 310 ms and 94% goodput against 14.2 s and 61% — and eliminated 1,277 req/s of work discarded by timed-out clients. Shed early, classify first, always send Retry-After.
  • Connections are a resource with memory and scheduling cost. 900 Lambda executions took DB CPU to 96% doing no extra work; RDS Proxy multiplexed them onto 38 connections and returned p99 to 120 ms — watch DatabaseConnectionsCurrentlySessionPinned.
  • Route reads deliberately: replicas are fine for reports and listings, wrong for read-your-writes and for reads that decide writes. Sticky-to-primary for N seconds after a write is the portable pattern.
  • TTL jitter is one line and removed 80% of our stampedes; add single-flight per key and stale-while-revalidate. CloudFront is usually the highest-leverage cache in the system.
  • Aggregate capacity is a lie if one key is hot — 38,000 of 40,000 WCU idle while a partition capped at 1,000 WCU threw throttles at 6% utilisation. Choose keys by cardinality and distribution; write-shard genuinely hot keys; expect to re-shard.
  • Quotas are per-account, per-Region, and present as a flat line at a round number — Lambda 1,000, API Gateway 10,000 RPS, vCPU limits, subnet IPs. Keep a readiness script; raise them during capacity planning, never during the incident.
  • Beyond a point you do not scale the system, you run more of it: cells of a known-good, load-tested size turn growth into a rehearsed 40-minute operation and bound the blast radius.
  • The load test is the deliverable. Testing at 2× expected peak found three constraints — each a full outage waiting at 2.4×, 5.1× and 6.8× normal — and all three fixes were hours of work. The event itself was boring, which is the goal.
